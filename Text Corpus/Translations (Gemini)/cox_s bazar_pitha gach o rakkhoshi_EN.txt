Having lost his father at a young age, the only son lived a difficult life with his mother. From childhood, the boy loved cakes. Every day, his mother had to make cakes for him, otherwise, the boy would become terribly upset. But for the poor mother, this became increasingly impossible day by day. The last day she made a cake, wondering how she would feed him cakes in the future, she planted one in a corner of the courtyard.

A few days later, a sapling sprouted there. Leaves grew. Branches spread. Thus, like a dream, a strangely beautiful tree grew up. Flowers bloomed within a short time. And there, hanging from the branches, were cakes. The mother and son stared in amazement.

One day, the boy climbed the tree and plucked a cake. It had a wonderful taste. Oh, what joy the boy felt! His days were passing in happiness and peace, eating the delicious cakes.

But within a few days, trouble began. An ogress in the guise of a woman would come under the cake tree. She would try to entice the boy with sweet words. She would ask for cakes from the boy. If he threw a cake to the ogress from a distance, she would make various excuses. She would say, 'This one has dirt on it,' or, 'This one is broken.' 'Give it to me hand-to-hand.' When he tried to hand it to her, she would try to grab him.

In this way, with all sorts of alluring words, the ogress plotted to capture the boy. Sometimes, she would even catch him. When caught, the boy would scream and shout and manage to free himself.

One day, under the pretext of taking a cake, the ogress truly caught the boy. Then she bound his hands, feet, and mouth, stuffed him into a sack, and started carrying him away.

On the way, the ogress went into the jungle by the roadside to answer the call of nature. The boy, struggling inside the sack, suddenly tore the knot at the mouth of the sack open. The boy slowly climbed out of the sack.

After getting out, the boy cleverly filled the sack with clods of earth, tree leaves, thorns, and a water-filled bamboo tube, then tied the mouth of the sack shut and fled home.

Meanwhile, the ogress returned, saw the sack looking as it was before, and hoisted it onto her shoulder to continue on her way. After walking a short distance, water from the bamboo tube trickled down onto the ogress's body. The ogress thought the boy was peeing on her.

So, every time water trickled down, the ogress would say, "Hmph. You're peeing on me. I'm going to eat you."

When a thorn pricked her, she would say, "Hmph. You're pricking me with thorns. I'll teach you a lesson."
'Hut put, son of man,
I will have my revenge.'

When she was hurt by a clod of earth, she would say, "You're kicking me. I'll deal with you."
'Hut put, son of man,
I will have my revenge.'

In this way, burning with rage, the ogress reached her own home.
'Hut put, son of man,
I will have my revenge.'

Shouting this, she tore open the sack. And when, instead of the human boy, she saw clods of earth, a bamboo tube, thorns, and leaves inside the sack, she became furious and began to scream bizarrely.

From the next day on, she again began to coax the boy from the cake tree in various ways. When she was trying to persuade him, the ogress spoke so sweetly that anyone's heart was bound to melt. And so, just like before, the ogress captured the boy one day and took him away.

As she walked, the ogress fumed with rage, remembering the boy's previous escape. She reached home thinking about how she would cut the boy into pieces and eat him.

"Wife, wife, Ghutilla," she called out, shouting, "Quickly, cut this son of man into pieces and cook him."

The wife sent word to everyone at her parents' house through her husband.
Taking the sack to the kitchen, the wife opened its mouth and took the boy out. And with the others, she began preparations to cook him.

The boy, looking around, saw a huge cleaver. Without delay, he chopped off the head of the ogress's daughter-in-law, Ghutilla. Then he quickly put on Ghutilla's clothes and disguised himself as the wife. Pulling the veil down low, he cooked the ogress's daughter-in-law.

Everyone was present, eager to eat. The ogress sat down to eat with her daughter-in-law's mother, father, and sister. While eating, the wife's sister exclaimed, "Mother, mother, look, this hand is just like my sister's hand."

Everyone burst into loud laughter.

The girl said again, "Mother, mother, look at the eye, it's exactly like my sister's."

Everyone scolded her, calling her "silly, silly."

In this way, before the meal was even finished, the boy, disguised as the wife, pretended to go fetch betel leaves and went to the riverbank with a water pot.

At the river landing, he found a merchant. He told the merchant his whole story. The merchant, taking pity on him, took him onto his boat.

Meanwhile, after the meal, not finding the ogress's daughter-in-law, everyone came to the riverbank. Seeing the boy in the wife's disguise from a distance, they thought, "There's the daughter-in-law." Before they could get close, the boy got into the merchant's boat and threw off the ogress's wife's clothes.

The ogress's party had no trouble understanding what had happened. The ogress's son, the wife's mother, father, and sister all began to wail loudly. The ogress, in a rage, started tearing out her hair.

The merchant's boat set off. The freed boy began to chant a rhyme:

"Ogress, wail and cry,
You've eaten the flesh of your son's wife."
