A prince, a minister's son, a merchant's son, and a kotwal's son—the four were great friends.

None of them did any work; they only rode around on their horses. Seeing this, the king, the minister, the merchant, and the kotwal grew annoyed and gave an order: "When the boys come to eat, give them ashes instead of rice."

What could the minister's wife, the merchant's wife, and the kotwal's wife do? With tears in their eyes, they served the ashes. The boys were astonished and got up and left.

But he was her own son, after all; how could she serve him ashes? The queen could not do it. The queen prepared a sweet rice pudding, placed a pinch of ash in a corner of the plate, and gave it to her son to eat.

The prince said, "Mother, why is there ash on the plate?"
The queen replied, "It's nothing, my son, it just fell there."

The prince was not convinced. He said, "No, Mother, I will not eat unless you tell me."

What could the queen do? She told her son everything.

Hearing this, the prince bowed at his mother's feet and rose.

He went to the place where the four friends met every day and asked everyone, "How did you all eat today?"

They all just looked at one another. Then the prince said, "Brothers, we will not stay in this country any longer. Let us leave it and go."
"That is for the best." The four of them set their four horses to a gallop.

Riding and riding and riding, the four friends reached the edge of a vast, desolate plain.

Across the plain, four paths led in four directions.

Which way would each go? It was decided: the kotwal's son would go south, the merchant's son north, the minister's son west, and the prince east. Then they all tore cloth from their turbans and flew four banners in the middle of the four paths. "Whoever returns first shall wait here for the other friends."

The four horses galloped down the four paths.

All day long, the four of them rode their horses, but none saw any village, city, port, or house; after evening, they all arrived at the very same place!

It was a vast forest! The prince said, "Look, we have surely fallen into an ogress's illusion; we must stay awake and be careful tonight! But our bodies are weak with hunger. See if any food can be found." They all tied up their horses and went in search of food.

Not a single fruit could be seen in the forest, nor any living creature, only stones, pebbles, and large banyan, pakur, palm, and silk-cotton trees!

Suddenly, they saw a deer's head lying a short distance away. Their joy knew no bounds. The kotwal's son went to gather wood, the merchant's son went to fetch water, the minister's son went in search of fire, and the prince, resting his head on a tree root, stretched out and lay down.

The prince was asleep. The kotwal's son, returning with the wood, saw that his other friends had not yet come. Putting down the wood, the kotwal's son went to cut the deer's head.

No sooner had he touched it with his sword than a hideous ogress emerged from within the deer's head, devoured the kotwal's son and his horse, and then, becoming a deer's head just as before, lay there. The merchant's son, bringing the water, saw the wood but wondered where his friend, the kotwal's son, had gone. The merchant's son went to cut the deer's head. The merchant's son and his horse went into the ogress's stomach.

The minister's son arrived and saw that the water and wood had been brought, but where were his friends? "Well, I'll prepare the meat."

"Save me, save me! Friends, where are you? I am gone for good!"

At the minister's son's scream, the prince sat up with a start. He saw—what a disaster—an ogress! The ogress, having eaten the minister's son and his horse, seized the prince's horse. The prince drew his sword and stood ready. The prince's winged horse cried out, "Prince, flee, flee, there is no escape!" The prince said, "I will not flee—she has eaten my friends, I will kill the ogress!" The prince raised his sword—his vision darkened, his arm grew weak. As the ogress came to grab the prince, the trees and stones of the forest cried out from all around, "Prince, flee, flee!" Then the prince, losing all sense of direction, began to run wherever his eyes took him.

The prince left one king's kingdom for another, yet the ogress would not give up the chase. Then, helpless, the prince saw a mango tree before him and said, "O mango tree! If you are a tree of truth, protect me from the ogress's grasp." The mango tree split in two, and the prince went inside and breathed a sigh of relief. The ogress pleaded with the tree, threatened it, but the tree paid no heed. Then the ogress took the form of a beautiful woman and sat crying at the foot of the tree.

The king of that country had come to the forest to hunt. Hearing the crying, the king said, "See who is crying in the forest." His men went and saw a woman of supreme beauty beneath the mango tree. The king took the girl to his palace.

The king married the girl from the forest. Having become queen, the ogress thought, "How can I eat that prince!" With that thought, the ogress ate seven-day-old betel leaves and fourteen-day-old tamarind soup to make herself sick. Then the ogress spread reeds under her bed. Spreading them, she lay on the bed, contorting her face, rolling her eyes up to her forehead, and turning from one side to the other.

The king came and saw that the queen was not eating or drinking; having wet her matted hair, she was lying down. Seeing this, the king asked, "What is this, Queen! What has happened?" Her voice would not come out. Groaning and moaning, with great difficulty, the queen said, "I have the bone-cracking disease."

As the queen tossed and turned, were not the reeds under the bed crackling? The king thought, "It's true! The queen's bones are crackling! Alas, what is to be done!"

So many medicines, so many treatments; but was the queen's illness an ordinary one? The illness was not cured! Finally, the queen said, "Medicines will do nothing. My illness will only be cured if that mango tree from the forest is cut down and the smoke from its planks fills the room."

The king's command! At once, thousands of carpenters went and struck the mango tree with their axes!

Inside the tree, the prince said, "O tree, if you are a tree of truth, turn me into a mango and drop me into this pond." Instantly, a mango fell from the tree with a splash into the pond; at that very moment, a large Boal fish, thinking it was food, swallowed it in one gulp. The carpenters cut down the mango tree, took it away, made planks from it, and filled the queen's room with smoke! But the queen knew everything; she said, "No, this did nothing either. In that pond, there is a large Boal fish with a mango in its belly. My illness will be cured only if I eat that mango."

With every kind of net, the fishermen cast into the pond; the Boal fish was caught. Inside the mango, inside the fish's belly, the prince said, "O Boal, if you are a true Boal, turn me into a snail and cast me out." The Boal turned the prince into a snail and cast him out. The fishermen brought the Boal, cut open its belly, and found nothing.

The king thought, "The queen's illness will never be cured!"

A householder's wife had gone to bathe; the prince, as a snail, touched her foot. The householder's wife picked up the snail, and just as she was about to smash it, the prince emerged from within. The householder's wife was paralyzed with fear. The prince said, "Wife, do not be afraid, I am a human—I am in a snail out of fear of an ogress. You have given me my life; from this day, you are my friend Hasan."

The prince stayed at the house of his friend Hasan.

The queen learned everything. She told the king, "My illness will not be cured by anything else. In my father's country, there is the dancing stick of the Hasan Champa flower, the fine-toothed comb of ivory, and the thirteen-cubit seed from a twelve-cubit cucumber. If those are brought, my illness will be cured."

"Who will bring them, who will bring them?"
"In a certain householder's home, there is a prince. He will bring them."
At once, thousands of guards rushed out.

The king's guards were everywhere; friend Hasan was beside herself with fear. The prince said, "Friend Hasan, you are in danger because of me. I will leave this country."

As soon as he stepped outside, the guards seized the prince and took him away! Upon reaching the king, the prince said, "Your Majesty! Your queen is an ogress; save me from her clutches."

Hearing this, the king said, "Lies. That will not do. You must bring the dancing stick of the Hasan Champa flower, the fine-toothed comb of ivory, and the thirteen-cubit seed from a twelve-cubit cucumber from the queen's father's country." The king sent the prince off with a letter.

What could he do? The prince began to walk. Where was this dancing stick, where was this thirteen-cubit seed from a twelve-cubit cucumber—where was the queen's father's country? The prince thought, "Alas! How can I escape the ogress's grasp!" The prince walked on, wherever his eyes took him.

After walking for many days and many nights, he arrived at a place.

The prince saw a massive palace. The prince said, "Ah! At last, I have found shelter."

Going inside the palace, he could see no people. Searching, he saw in one room a princess lying with her head on a golden bedstead and her feet on a silver one. The prince called out, but the princess did not rise! Then the prince saw two wands on either side of the bed—the wand at her head was silver, the one at her feet was gold. The prince took the wand from her head and placed it at her feet, and the wand from her feet he placed at her head. The princess sat up. "Who are you! A god or a demon, a monster or a man—how did you get here? Flee, flee—this is a palace of ogres."

The prince's heart sank. "I came from the clutches of one ogress, and here too are ogres! Princess, where can I go?"

The princess said, "First, tell me who you are."

The prince told her everything. Then he said, "I still have not been able to escape the clutches of that ogress queen. But why is a princess like you in this palace of ogres?"

The princess said, "This palace belonged to my father. The ogres have devoured my parents and my kingdom; they have kept only me. So that I cannot escape, the ogres use the golden and silver wands to leave me for dead when they go out."

Hearing this, the prince began to think about how the two of them could escape the ogres' clutches.

"My, oh my, the scent of a human I spy.
I'll catch him and eat him, by and by!"

Just then, the ogres began to approach, making noise from all sides. The princess said, "Prince, prince—quickly, put me back to sleep and go hide in that Shiva temple, under the flowers and bel leaves."

Shouting "My, oh my," the ogres arrived. The old ogress revived the princess and said:

"Oh, my grandchild, my grandchild! It smells of a human—
Where could a human be?"

The princess said, "A human? Where would a human be? It is only I. Eat me."

The old ogress said, "Ooh hoo hoo, my grandchild, how could I do that! Here, grandchild, see how much food I've brought for you." After feeding her grandchild, the old ogress and all the other ogres poured pots of mustard oil into their noses and ears, and fell asleep, snoring loudly. The princess would pick the gray hairs from the old ogress's head and kill the louse-like lice one by one with a crack between two stones.

This is how the princess's nights passed.

The next day, after putting the princess to sleep again, the ogres left. The prince came out, revived the princess, and they both bathed and ate. The prince said, "Princess, how long can we stay like this? Today, when the old ogress comes, use some pretext to ask her what the secret of their death is."

When the ogres returned again, the prince went and hid in the Shiva temple. After feeding the princess, the old ogress sat on the bed. The princess said, "Granny, oh granny, you've come back weary from wandering through so many kingdoms. Come, let me fan you a little, let me pluck a few gray hairs!"

"Oh, my dear, my sweet!" The old ogress laughed, her eyes rolling up to her forehead, and said, "Yes, yes, grandchild, my feet are aching. Will you press them a little?"

"Of course I will, granny!" Pouring a potful of mustard oil into the cracks of the old ogress's feet, the princess began to press her feet.

While pressing her feet, the princess put oil in her eyes and cried—a drop of her tear fell on the old ogress's foot. Startled, the old ogress lifted the teardrop on her fingertip, touched it to her tongue, and it tasted salty. She said, "Grandchild, you are crying—why, my dear, why? What sorrow could you have?"

The princess said, "I cry, granny, for the day you will die, and all the other ogres will eat me."

Waggling her winnowing-fan-like ears and baring her radish-like teeth, the old ogress laughed and said, "Oh, my golden grandchild, do we have a death that we should die? In this world, nothing can kill us! Only, in that pond, there is a crystal pillar, and inside it is a seven-headed snake. If some prince, holding his breath, can climb that golden palm tree, cut down its palm-leaf sword, break the crystal pillar, take out the snake, and cut it upon his chest, only then will we die. But if even one drop of blood falls on the ground, then from each drop, seven thousand ogres will be born!"

Hearing this, the princess said, "Then what is there to worry about, granny! No one will be able to do that, and you will not die either—so I have no more worries. But granny! The queen of that other country, the ogress, what is the secret of her life? And where can one find the dancing stick of the Hasan Champa flower, the fine-toothed comb of ivory, and the thirteen-cubit seed from a twelve-cubit cucumber, granny?"

The old ogress said, "They are here, grandchild, they are here! They are in the room where your father used to live, and in that room is a parrot, and within it is the life of my daughter, that queen! Don't you tell anyone, grandchild, for I will give everything to you."

The next day, the old ogress went out with all the ogres, saying, "Grandchild, today we will stay nearby." On the days the ogres spoke of staying close, they stayed close by; on the days they spoke of staying far, they went very far away. After the ogres had left, the prince came, revived the princess, and heard everything. At once, after bathing and changing his clothes, he offered flowers and bel leaves at the Shiva temple, and then, holding his breath, the prince climbed the palm tree and cut down the palm-leaf sword. Then he entered the pond, broke the crystal pillar, and saw the seven-headed snake. The prince came up with the snake. The heads of all the ogres in the world began to throb—wherever they were, the ogres came rushing back. With disheveled hair, flinging her long, long legs, the old ogress came running ahead of everyone—

"My, oh my, grandchild, oh grandchild,
So this was in your heart!
I'll chew up your head and eat it!"

But there was no time for eating heads!

The princess said, "Prince, quickly, cut the snake!"

Placing it on his chest, the prince cut the snake's throat with the palm-leaf sword. He did not let a single drop of blood fall.

It was all over. As the ogres reached the edge of the pond, their heads fell off.

The prince and princess, breathing a sigh of relief, went into the house. In one room were the dancing stick of the Hasan Champa flower, the fine-toothed comb of ivory, and everything else, and a parrot was fluttering and screeching. Taking everything, the prince said, "Princess, let us go to my country."

Leaving the princess in a safe place, the prince took the queen's "medicines" and the parrot and went to the king. "Your Majesty, hold court one more time, and I will cure the queen's illness."

Greatly pleased, the king held court. The prince placed the stick, the comb, the flower, and the cucumber in the court. Everyone looked on in amazement. The prince said, "Your Majesty, the queen must come herself to take these things."

Meanwhile, the queen's bone-cracking disease had turned into a heart-pounding one. "So the boy has come back, having ruined everything! Today I will eat him! I will eat the kingdom!!"

Eat the kingdom! As the queen set foot at the court's door, the prince said, "O ogress, you will eat me? Look at this!" The prince took the parrot out of its cage and was about to wring its neck in one motion! The ogress cried, "I won't eat you, I won't eat you, stop, stop!! I fall at your feet!" The queen's form was gone, replaced by a fanged ogress!

The king and everyone in the court trembled.

The prince said, "Give me back my kotwal friend, give me back my kotwal friend's horse! Give me back my merchant friend, give me back my merchant friend's horse! My minister friend, my minister friend's horse, and give me back my horse!"

The ogress retched and vomited them all up one by one! Then the prince said, "Your Majesty, do you see now whether the queen is an ogress or not?"

"Now, ogress—perish!!"

He wrung the parrot's neck—the ogress fell down with a gurgle and died! The death of an ogress—even in dying, she gave a death-grip, almost pulling down the king's throne with her! The king barely escaped.

Everyone's fever broke with a sweat of relief. The king said, "Bravo, prince from wherever you are! Take as much wealth as you desire from my open treasury."

The prince said, "I want nothing. At last, everyone is saved from the ogress—now we will go to our country." The king would not listen; he opened his treasury and gave him all its wealth and jewels.

Taking the princess, the prince and his three friends went to their country. All the ogres in the world were destroyed for good.

Returning to their country, the princes, cherished by their parents, began to live happily.
