A long, long time ago, there lived an old man and an old woman. They had no son, only a single daughter. The girl had a beautiful, radiant face, like a fairy's. They were overjoyed when the girl came into their lives in their old age. They lovingly named her Dh'bi.

The old man and the old woman practiced jhum cultivation. Their jhum field yielded so much paddy that they couldn't finish eating it all. Besides that, there were also sesame, kaporsi, pumpkins, marfa, chinar, and other fruits and vegetables. In short, the old man and woman had a very happy household. But that happiness was not fated to last. One day, a dark cloud suddenly appeared over their home. Their days of sorrow began.

One day, the old man and woman went to the jhum field to weed. The paddy seedlings had just turned a vibrant green. If they could weed the field well at this time, no more weeds would be able to grow later. And the paddy yield would be greater too.

It was the month of Jyeshtha. The old man and woman were clearing weeds in the jhum. The sun was scorching all around. There was not even a sliver of shade nearby. The few trees that stood in the middle of the jhum had not a single leaf on them. All the leaves had been burnt and had fallen to the ground when the jhum was set on fire. It would still be a long time before new leaves sprouted.

The old woman said to the old man, "Come, let's go have some water. I'm terribly thirsty."

The old man said, "Oh, just wait a little! Let me clear this section first. Don't you see how overgrown the weeds are? If we don't clear them quickly, the paddy seedlings won't grow."

But what could be said? The old man himself was very thirsty. So, finishing their work quickly, they rushed off in search of some water.

But where was the water? In the blistering heat of Jyeshtha, the water in all the small streams and canals had dried up. On other days, they would bring drinking water from home, filled in a 'kotti' (a kotti is a clay jug). Today, they hadn't brought any, which had caused a huge problem.

After searching for a while, the old woman spotted a cool-looking place. It seemed there had once been a reservoir there. There were many deer hoof prints around, and a little water had collected in their indentations. Overcome with thirst, the old woman lapped up this water. But it didn't even wet her throat. Far from quenching her thirst, it doubled it.

Meanwhile, the old man had found a crab's hole. Inside was some clean, cool water. Overjoyed, the old man was about to cup his hands and drink when the old woman came up and said in a tearful voice, "Old man, won't you give me a little to drink?"

At that moment, the old man's own chest was bursting with thirst. The old woman's interruption made him suddenly shout, "Dur ho! Dur ho! Dur ho!"

In the Chakma language, 'dur' means tortoise. That time was the 'Satyakal,' or the Age of Truth, when spoken words would come true. And so, as soon as the old man said 'Dur ho,' a strange thing happened! Instead of going away, the old woman truly became a huge 'dur,' that is, a tortoise. Then that tortoise, in search of water, made its way to the river next to their house.

How could the old man drink water now? He was struck speechless, having witnessed such an astonishing event before his very eyes. In the blink of an eye, what was said had come to pass! Realizing what had happened, the old man slapped his forehead and broke into sobs, mourning his wife... "Alas! Alas! What have I done..."

Lamenting the loss of his wife, the old man eventually returned to his home. Seeing her father return alone, the girl ran to him and asked, "Father! Where is Mother?"

Hearing his daughter's words, the old man's heart burned with twice the grief. The little girl had never been separated from her mother for a single day. How would the old man explain this to her now? Looking at his daughter's face, the old man hardened his heart like stone. He told her a white lie, "She will come, she will come! Your mother will come in the evening."

The girl was reassured, but she spent the whole day restlessly, not having her mother near. The poor thing kept looking outside frequently, thinking, "Mother must be here now!" Gradually, the afternoon passed into evening, and dusk fell, but her mother did not return.

The girl could no longer contain herself. She went to her father, buried her face in his back, and began to sob. She asked him again and again when her mother would come.

Seeing his daughter's sorrow, the father could no longer hold back his tears. He pulled his daughter into his arms with great affection and tried to console her over and over, "Don't cry, my child! Don't cry! Your mother will surely come tomorrow morning." By explaining in various ways, the old man somehow managed to calm his daughter. With his clumsy hands, he cooked, fed his daughter, and put some food in his own mouth. Then he took his daughter in his arms and went to bed.

The next day, before the morning was even fully bright, the girl again insisted on going to her mother. Helpless, the old man finally took his daughter to the riverbank. Then, one by one, he told her everything. In the end, he said, "Look! Your mother has become a tortoise in this river's water. She will never return. She has to live in the water."

Hearing her father's words, the girl fell to the ground. She began to cry her heart out in grief for her mother. Hearing her daughter's cries, the tortoise suddenly surfaced with a splash. Stretching its neck above the water, it called to its daughter, "Don't cry, my child! Don't cry! By the writ of fate, I have become a tortoise. There is no way for me to return home. Don't give yourself sorrow by crying in vain. Whenever you wish to see me, come to the bank and clap your hands, and wherever I am, I will come to see you." Comforted by her mother's words, the girl returned home. From then on, whenever the girl thought of her mother, she would go to the riverbank and clap her hands, and immediately the tortoise would float to the surface of the water. It would come near the shore to see its daughter and give her various words of advice.

And so the days went on.

Having lost his wife, the old man became completely alone. He could no longer manage the jhum cultivation by himself. He fell into great hardship with his daughter. However, the old man was skilled at cane work. One way or another, he had to work to eat. So, he began cutting bamboo from the forest, making cane products like winnowing fans, tongs, and thurungs, and selling them by wandering through the entire village.

Bhat dibe jora jora,
Ton dibe thora thora
Tong, kulo lobhani ma bon para?
(Give rice by the handful,
Give curry a little bit,
Will you take tongs, winnowing fans...
O mothers and sisters of the neighborhood?)
In this way, the father and daughter passed their days.

In another village lived an old woman. Her husband had died many days ago. She too was somehow getting by with her only daughter. The daughter's name was K'bi. She was about the same age as the old man's daughter, Dh'bi. When the old man went to her village to sell his wares, that old woman took a great liking to him. From then on, she began trying to find a way to win the old man's heart. Whenever she saw the old man passing by on that road, she would call him into her house. She would give him cool water and various kinds of food to eat. Sitting beside him and fanning him with a hand fan, she would spend a couple of moments with him, sharing stories of happiness and sorrow. The old woman would say, "Oh, old man! You suffer so much! If you had an old woman like me in your house, she certainly wouldn't have let you come out in this scorching sun."

And on some days, the old woman would say, "Oh, old man! I can't bear to see your hardship anymore. You must be exhausted from wandering in the sun all day. Evening is approaching; why don't you stay here tonight!"

The old man would quickly say, "No, no, I won't stay today. My little girl is home alone. Whatever happens during the day, she will cry a river once evening falls."

Another day, the old woman absolutely insisted on keeping the old man at her house. When the old man objected as he usually did, she told him, "You don't have to worry about your daughter. Our villages are right next to each other, and I have a daughter too. I'll send my daughter right now to bring your daughter here." That day, none of the old man's objections held up. The old woman took the tongs and winnowing fans that the old man hadn't sold and hid them inside the house, so that he couldn't escape. Then she sent her daughter to fetch the old man's daughter.

The old woman slaughtered a chicken for the evening meal. Then she cooked it well and served it with great care to the old man and his daughter. She ate with her own daughter as well. At night, when everyone was asleep, the old woman quietly tied a knot between her clothes and the old man's clothes, and then lay down beside him. Meanwhile, before dawn, somehow sensing the old woman's deed, the house rooster flapped its wings and crowed,

"Kok k re kok!
Bujyaloi buri jodon banyonde jok re jok"
(Cock-a-doodle-doo!
The old man and woman have tied a knot, look, oh look!)

Waking up, the old woman, with feigned anger, scolded the rooster, "You wretched rooster, just you wait! I'll slaughter you at dawn." Meanwhile, the old man had also woken up. Getting up, he saw that he was indeed tied to the old woman with a knot. And the old woman acted as if she had just fallen from the sky, saying, "Oh my! What is this, old man! Who tied the knot? Shame! Shame! Shame! How can I show my face in the village now if we don't get married? And what will the two girls say?" Trapped, the old man was forced to marry the old woman. After marrying her, the old man brought the old woman to his own home.

Then began the true days of sorrow for the old man's daughter. As soon as she arrived at the old man's house, the old woman revealed her true self. Gradually, her jealous nature began to show. The old man's daughter was, after all, a co-wife's daughter. The old woman cast her evil eye upon the girl. She started making her do all sorts of household chores, from fetching water to scrubbing pots, pans, and dirty dishes. If she saw the slightest negligence in her work, the old woman would hiss and become aggressive. This became a daily affair.

Despite the hardship, the old man's daughter worked with all her might. Out of fear of the old woman, she couldn't even tell her father about her suffering. When it became too unbearable, she would go to the riverbank and sit, sobbing. And as soon as it heard her cry, her tortoise mother would surface with a splash, come to the edge of the bank, and console her daughter, "Don't cry, my child! Don't cry! Endure it! This is all written in your fate." Then, diving back into the deep water, her tortoise mother would disappear.

At one point, the old woman learned about her co-wife, that she was still alive as a tortoise in the river. She learned that when the old man and his daughter went to the riverbank and called, she would float to the surface to see them. Learning all this, the old woman burned with jealousy. She became determined to chew the flesh of her co-wife. The old woman devised various schemes and tricks to catch the tortoise. She had people set up rows of fish traps upstream. Downstream, she placed a line of nets, and on both banks of the river, she laid out many baited hooks. Understanding the old woman's intentions, the old man's daughter, Dh'bi, found a moment to go and warn her mother beforehand, saying,

O..o.. Ma!
Uguredi gele cheiyyot bajhibe,
Lamnye gele terat bajhibe,
Kulo kay ele bojjit bajhibe,
Moddhe moddhe thak!

(Hey... Mother!
If you go upstream, you'll be caught in a trap,
If you go downstream, you'll be caught in a net,
If you come near the bank, you'll be caught on a hook,
Stay in the middle!)

Receiving her daughter's timely warning, the tortoise could not be caught. Growing desperate from these failures, the old woman became even more furious. The tortoise could go neither upstream nor downstream, and coming to the bank was also a great danger. There was always the fear of being caught. Foraging for food became completely impossible for her. But how long could one survive without eating? After going without food for several days, one day, driven by the pangs of hunger, the tortoise came near the bank to forage. And just like that, it was caught on a hook. Who could describe the old woman's joy then? The enemy was caught at last. She hurriedly dragged the tortoise onto the land, carried it home, and ordered the old man's daughter to clean it by burning it over a fire and then cut it up.

Seeing all this, the old man's daughter was completely stunned. She couldn't understand what to do. It was her stepmother's order, and there was no way to refuse. Finally, forced, the girl lit a fire in a corner of the courtyard and took the tortoise there to roast it. And the old woman stood by, watching the fun with great delight. The girl was in a terrible predicament; though a tortoise, it was her mother. She couldn't figure out which side to burn first! The back was a bit harder, but if she put that side to the fire, the face would be the first thing she'd see. After much thought, the girl first placed its chest on the fire. As soon as it felt the heat, the tortoise writhed and cried out to its daughter,

O... o... Ma!
E bug'ottun dudh habenei tore dangor gojjyong,
Tui m' bukko kityei agunot dor?
(Hey... Mother!
I raised you by feeding you milk from this breast,
Why are you burning my chest?)

Startled, the girl quickly turned the tortoise over onto its back. This time too, the tortoise cried out in pain to its daughter,

O... o... Ma!
E pidhit buginai tore kodhok loi bereyyong
M' pittan kityei agunot dor?
(Hey... Mother!
I carried you on this back and took you so many places,
Why are you burning my back?)

Unable to bear the tortoise's agony, the girl turned it from one side to the other, and as she roasted it, the tortoise eventually thrashed about and died. Seeing this thrashing, the old woman laughed herself to stitches, thinking to herself, "Let it be a tortoise, a co-wife is a co-wife, an enemy. Let it die! One should not keep an enemy alive." The girl, her chest heaving with tears, went to the riverbank, cut up the tortoise, washed the meat well, and brought it to the old woman. The old woman, overjoyed, put the meat on the stove to cook. As the heat made the stew bubble and boil, a voice suddenly spoke from within the pot,

'Bobog bobog sodino madha hang,
Bobog bobog sodino madha hang.'
(Bubble, bubble, I eat the co-wife's head.)

Seeing this, the old woman shrieked in fear! Her eyes widened in terror. What an ominous event! Even after death, her co-wife wanted to eat her! The old woman did not dare to eat the cooked tortoise meat. Who knows what might happen? In fear, she threw the meat onto the garbage heap. After a few days, a gourd seedling was seen sprouting there. The old man's daughter began to tend to it with great care. In no time, the gourd vine grew well and covered the entire roof of the kitchen. Flowers bloomed, and small gourds appeared. Eventually, one gourd grew larger and larger until it hung from the roof right in the middle of the doorway. Day by day, it grew bigger. Every morning when the old woman went to leave the house, it would hit her right in the middle of her forehead, and it would hit her in the same way when she entered. The old woman would then curse it, saying, "Wretched gourd! Couldn't you find any other place to grow? Just wait a few more days, I won't spare you."

After a few days, the gourd had grown quite large. The old woman picked it, chopped it into small pieces, and went to cook it. When the heat from the stove made the stew bubble and boil, the voice from the pot spoke just like before,

Bobog bobog sodinor madha hang,
'Bobog bobog sodinor madha hang.'
(Bubble, bubble, I eat the co-wife's head.)

This time, the old woman was even more frightened, almost fainting in terror. "Good heavens! It's the same voice as before! Who knows what might happen?" Thinking this, she did not eat the curry this time either; she threw the curry, pot and all, onto the pile of husks in the husking shed. After some time, a banyan sapling was seen sprouting there. In a short time, the tree grew quite large, and its branches spread out, covering a large area. The old woman would send the old man's daughter, Dh'bi, to husk paddy. The poor girl would husk paddy alone in the midday sun. Sweat would pour down her entire body from the heat and warmth. At that time, the tree would provide shade to Dh'bi with great affection, fanning her with its swaying branches. Like a mother, it would lovingly wipe away all her fatigue.

Even after putting the old man's daughter through so much hardship, the old woman's desire was not satisfied. The evil old woman was always thinking of new ways to torment her stepdaughter. At one point, the old woman was afflicted with a bone-breaking, joint-breaking disease. Medicine had to be arranged. As a cure for this disease, the old woman supposedly had to drink tiger's milk. But who would go into the deep jungle, find a tigress, and bring back tiger's milk for the old woman? The old woman arranged everything herself. In the evening, the old man's daughter, Dh'bi, and the old woman's daughter, K'bi, would go to get the tiger's milk.

Meanwhile, in the afternoon, the old woman taught her own daughter, K'bi, some wicked tricks. The old woman told her daughter, "This evening, when you go to get the tiger's milk, you will slip and fall from the stairs and pretend to have hurt your waist. If you can't go, Dh'bi will have to go into the forest alone to get the tiger's milk."

The afternoon passed, and evening slowly descended. Darkness also fell upon Dh'bi's face. The old woman told Dh'bi and K'bi to go into the forest and get tiger's milk.

Then the two stepsisters got ready to go into the forest to get the tiger's milk. As per the old woman's instructions, while climbing down the ladder of the stilt house, K'bi pretended to trip and fell to the ground. As a result, the old man's daughter, Dh'bi, had to go into the forest alone to get the tiger's milk. Dh'bi entered the forest alone. The calls of the ferocious animals of the forest made her skin crawl. She thought to herself that the tigers and bears would surely eat her today. She was slowly moving forward when suddenly a huge tigress stood before her. Seeing Dh'bi, the tigress roared and charged towards her. Dh'bi quickly folded her hands and said to the tigress,

Jhu jhu! Mujhi jhu!
Khele kha dele da,
Khele tor bhug jurebo'
Moro bhari dug furebo'.
(Greetings, Auntie, greetings to you!
If you wish to eat me, eat me; do as you please,
Eating me will satisfy your hunger,
And all my sorrows will also end.)

Hearing Dh'bi's words, the tigress spoke in a human voice, "Well, that's good! It's lucky you called me Auntie, or I would have eaten you by now. Whose child are you? Oh my! Your face is as dry as a stick!"

Dh'bi said, "Auntie! My stepmother has a 'bone-breaking, joint-breaking' illness, a very serious illness. I need a little tiger's milk for her medicine, otherwise she won't survive."

The tigress said kindly, "Oh, is that so? Then take some, my child, take some! After all, you called me Auntie! How can I refuse you then? Besides, it's for medicine."

The tigress stood with her legs slightly apart, and milk began to stream from her teats onto the ground. Dh'bi quickly filled a bamboo tube with some milk. Before she left, the tigress said again, "Oh dear! You look like a very sad girl. Wait! I have a lot of clothes, take them! I'm giving them to you. Wear them and remember this tigress auntie of yours, alright?"

Since ancient times, so many people had ended up in this tigress's stomach, and their various clothes lay in a pile to one side. The tigress took Dh'bi there and showed her everything. Now, how could a little girl like her carry such a mountain of clothes alone? So, she carefully chose a few pairs of silk pinon-khadi, tied them in a bundle, and left the rest there.

Taking leave of her tigress auntie, Dh'bi started back home. In one hand, she held the tiger's milk, and in the other, the bundle of clothes. She walked with a happy heart, but suddenly a thought came to her mind. From what she had seen, if she went home with these things, her stepmother might take them all away. Instantly, an idea flashed in her mind. As evening fell and she reached her village, before entering her house, she went to the banyan tree in the husking shed. Going to the tree, she folded her hands and said, "O benevolent banyan tree! If you are a tree from the Age of Truth, then open up!" Hearing Dh'bi's words, the tree truly split open. Then she quickly placed the bundle of clothes inside and went home.

Meanwhile, as Dh'bi had not returned home the previous night, her old father had not slept a wink. The old woman, however, was very happy inside. Seeing the old man's distress, she also feigned crying. The next evening, seeing Dh'bi return alive, the old woman was furious. She said, "You wretched girl. Where were you last night? You surely didn't go to the forest; you must have spent your time gossiping at someone's house and then came back!" But when Dh'bi actually handed the tiger's milk to the old woman, the wicked woman swallowed her anger and fell silent. And on the other hand, her old father was overjoyed to have his daughter back, showering her with affection. But the old woman was not one to give up easily. A few days later, her bone-breaking, joint-breaking disease flared up again. This time, her medicine was snake's milk. This time too, just like before, the old woman's daughter, K'bi, fell on the ladder of the stilt house, so Dh'bi had to go into the forest alone again to get snake's milk. This time too, Dh'bi went into the forest, saw a giant snake, called it Auntie, and bowed in respect. Just like before, the snake gave Dh'bi a tube full of milk. Then the snake said, "You look very sad! Wait! I have a lot of human jewelry lying around. I'll give it to you, take it. You will wear all of this and remember this forest auntie of yours, alright?"

Since ancient times, the snake had swallowed so many people. Their bones had long since been digested in its stomach, but the jewelry could not be digested. The snake had collected it all. The snake took Dh'bi and showed her its treasure trove of jewelry. How could Dh'bi carry all that jewelry alone? So, she cleverly chose a few sets of expensive jewelry, packed them in a bundle, took leave of her snake auntie, and started for home. As evening approached and she neared her house, she once again went to the banyan tree as before and, as usual, placed all the jewelry in the hollow of the banyan tree and went home. At home, she handed the snake's milk to her father.

Seeing Dh'bi return alive twice, the old woman was inwardly seething with rage. She thought to herself, "What a nuisance this is; the co-wife's daughter just won't die." She couldn't say anything out loud, so she had to keep her anger to herself, lest the old man find out everything.

The old man gave the snake's milk to the old woman to drink. The old woman, instead of drinking it, cleverly poured all the milk under the stilt house without the old man seeing. What else could be done? How long could she pretend to be sick for no reason? The old woman herself was getting annoyed. One day, she slowly sat up in bed.

Suffering through such hardship and sorrow, the old man's daughter became completely emaciated. What good was it that she was beautiful? The old woman wouldn't let her wear a single nice piece of clothing. There was no question of jewelry, oil was never put in her hair, her hands and feet were bare; the old woman just made her work around the clock. She would scold her for every little thing. Yet, the girl was growing up. On the other hand, it was the opposite for her own daughter; there was no shortage of jewelry and fine clothes. She didn't have to do any work; her job was to eat, drink, and roam around having fun. And so the days passed.

One day, there was a huge commotion in the village. The king's men suddenly came and announced with the beat of a drum that the king was inviting all the unmarried girls in the kingdom to the palace on the upcoming full moon. The prince would marry whichever one of them he chose. And then what? A festive mood swept through the quarters of the unmarried girls in every village. Such a tempting prospect! Who wouldn't want to test their luck? It was a matter of marrying the king's son. She would become a queen and live in such happiness. A rush began to see who could dress up their daughter and send her to the palace first. Meanwhile, the wicked old woman also decided to send her daughter, K'bi, to the palace and borrowed all sorts of beautiful jewelry from relatives and acquaintances.

Then, on the appointed day, the old woman sent K'bi to the palace in a horse-drawn carriage. And she sent her stepdaughter, Dh'bi, along as a servant. She instructed her daughter to introduce her stepsister, Dh'bi, as a servant. But after going some distance, Dh'bi's carriage broke down. Instead of taking Dh'bi into her own carriage, K'bi abandoned her and went on alone. K'bi was secretly very happy that Dh'bi's carriage had broken down. She was jealous of Dh'bi because she was more beautiful and virtuous. After K'bi's carriage was out of sight, Dh'bi slowly got down from her carriage and went to the banyan tree. From the banyan tree, she asked for a set of gold-inlaid jewelry and beautiful silk clothes. Wearing them, she flawlessly adorned herself and set off for the palace. Dh'bi was already beautiful, with a radiant face and fair complexion. Dressed in inlaid jewelry and silk clothes, she looked as beautiful as a fairy princess. It was difficult to look away once you glanced at her.

When Dh'bi arrived at the palace, the same thing happened there. The music, dancing, and singing in the palace came to a complete halt! Because everyone forgot everything and stared at her in amazement. It was as if no one had ever seen such a beautiful maiden in their life. Was this beautiful girl human or a fairy princess? Within moments, the news spread throughout the palace that a maiden of supreme beauty had arrived. A crowd gathered to see Dh'bi. Hearing the news, the prince also came. He too was mesmerized by her exquisite beauty. As their eyes met, Dh'bi lowered her head in shyness. Her cheeks turned even redder with embarrassment, which enhanced her beauty even more. The prince, having chosen Dh'bi, took her hands and began to dance. Meanwhile, although K'bi didn't recognize her stepsister at first, she eventually did and began to burn with jealousy. The king and queen treated Dh'bi with love and care and kept her at the palace. A few days later, they married the prince to Dh'bi with great pomp and ceremony. The people of the entire kingdom joined in the joyous celebration. For seven days and seven nights, the palace was filled with feasting, dancing, music, and festivities. Meanwhile, K'bi, burning with jealousy, returned home crying. The wicked old woman then consoled her daughter, saying, "When that enemy comes to visit her father's house with the prince, we must find an opportunity to kill her. Then you can disguise yourself as Dh'bi and go to the palace." The wicked old woman and her daughter began to plot how to kill Dh'bi.

Finally, their opportunity came one day. Dh'bi indeed came to visit her old father's house with her husband, the prince. Along with them came maids, servants, elephants, horses, soldiers, and so much more. Seeing this, Dh'bi's old father could not contain his joy. He himself went and welcomed his son-in-law, the prince, into his home with great honor. Dh'bi, lighting up the whole house, happily wandered from room to room. And seeing her stepdaughter's good fortune, the wicked old woman's eyes burned with jealousy. However, the old woman was extremely cunning. Keeping the jealousy in her heart, she outwardly showered the prince with extreme affection and care. She introduced herself as Dh'bi's real mother. Dh'bi's days at her father's house passed with great love and care. A few days later, when the prince wanted to return, Dh'bi's heart was heavy. After so much sorrow, on this happy day, her heart did not want to leave her old father. The wicked old woman did not let this opportunity slip. She too insisted that Dh'bi stay for a few more days, wanting to show everyone how much she loved Dh'bi. Understanding Dh'bi's wish, the prince left Dh'bi at her father's house for a few more days and returned to the palace.

Dh'bi loved the prince very much. So, to ensure that her husband faced not the slightest inconvenience, she sent all the maids and servants back to the palace as well. Now, the wicked old woman and her jealous daughter, K'bi, got down to their real work. They began looking for an opportunity to kill Dh'bi. Of course, they did not forget to act sweet towards Dh'bi to her face. Dh'bi was very fond of weaving on her loom. She would weave every day. Seizing the opportunity, the wicked old woman and her jealous daughter dug a huge pit under the floor of the weaving shed.

One afternoon, Dh'bi was weaving, and her stepsister, K'bi, was sitting beside her, watching. She was asking various questions about Dh'bi's in-laws' house. After asking many things, K'bi said to Dh'bi, "Well, sister, it's been a long time since you got married, but I still haven't had a good look at your wedding ring! I really want to hold it and see. Won't you let me see it?" Hearing her sister's words, the simple-hearted Dh'bi took off her wedding ring and gave it to K'bi. She said, "Here, take a good look, but don't lose it." K'bi took the ring in her hand and began to turn it over and examine it. Suddenly, K'bi intentionally dropped the ring into the pit they had dug earlier under the floor. Then, crying out in mock distress, she said to Dh'bi, "What will happen now, sister? The ring suddenly fell into the pit!" What could Dh'bi do then? She was of a simple nature; she did not understand the wicked intentions of her evil stepmother and jealous stepsister. With a simple heart, she went down into the pit to get the ring. The wicked old woman and her daughter were waiting for this very opportunity. As soon as Dh'bi went down into the pit, the two of them, mother and daughter, poured hot water from above. Then, from both sides, they covered her with earth and killed her. As soon as Dh'bi died, she turned into a yellow bird and flew away towards the palace. Then the wicked old woman and her daughter removed the earth and took Dh'bi's ring and all her jewelry. A few days later, the wicked old woman sent K'bi to the palace disguised as Dh'bi. Upon seeing K'bi, suspicion arose in the prince's mind from the very beginning, because there was a huge difference between Dh'bi's demeanor and K'bi's.

Dh'bi was exceptionally beautiful, and her complexion was not so dark. And her cheeks were not so puffy. Her voice was also so sweet. That's why the prince refused to accept her as his wife. Then K'bi began to lament, "Prince, after you left, my eyes have swollen from crying in your absence, my cheeks have become puffy, and my voice has become hoarse. My complexion has also turned dark from sorrow." Showing the wedding ring, she said, "Look here—the ring you put on my finger at our wedding is still here." The prince was surprised to see the ring on K'bi's hand. Still, the suspicion did not leave his mind. Anyway, the prince allowed K'bi to stay in the palace. And he ordered the maids and servants to test her in various ways.

K'bi may have found a place in the palace, but her real troubles began. On the first day, she was asked to cook. While cooking, she put too much salt in some dishes, no salt at all in others, and too much chili in others. That day, no one could even taste her cooking. Then K'bi started making various excuses. Having eaten idly at her father's house for a long time and not cooking, the food didn't turn out well today. The first day passed. Now, on the second day, she was asked to sing. She then began to sing a song her sister had taught her in a grating voice. Hearing her harsh voice, everyone ran away in whichever direction they could.

K'bi was extremely embarrassed by this. Still, to cover her own fault, she began to say that her voice was in this pathetic state from crying in the prince's absence. No matter how many excuses K'bi made to save herself, she did not get any respite from the palace maids and servants. This time, they brought her a paintbrush and colors to draw a picture. K'bi knew nothing about drawing. She drew a crow's leg and a crane's leg. Everyone mocked her drawing. Finally, she was asked to weave on a loom. Dh'bi used to weave very beautifully. She could create very beautiful flower patterns, and everyone in the palace would praise her for it.

The wicked old woman's daughter, K'bi, had never woven in her life. She couldn't figure out what to do. She was in a terrible predicament. Sitting down to weave, she couldn't understand how to create the flower patterns. At that moment, the yellow bird from the tree above her head said,

'Tin jur felei tin jur tul
Shadanor jhi shadani bigun biji fullo tul.'
(Drop three pairs, pick up three pairs,
The devil's daughter, the devil, weave the eggplant-seed flower.)

Hearing the yellow bird's words, K'bi became very angry. She threw a weaving stick at the yellow bird. The poor yellow bird died instantly. That day, while passing by that way, the prince saw a dead yellow bird lying there. He felt great pity for the bird. The prince brought the dead yellow bird straight to his bedroom. He placed it carefully in a corner of the room. Thinking about the bird, the prince eventually fell asleep.

The next day when he woke up, the prince saw a strange thing! Someone had beautifully decorated and tidied his bedroom. The prince thought to himself that perhaps one of the maids or servants had decorated and tidied the room like this. At that moment, his eyes fell on the corner of the room. Where he had placed the yellow bird, a marigold plant had sprouted overnight. The plant had just one beautiful, large flower in bloom! The prince was completely astonished. He wondered if it was a dream or a trick of his mind.

The next day too, the prince saw the same thing! His room was all decorated and tidy, just like the previous night. Not only that, but someone had also left betel-leaf spittle on his clothes. Seeing this, the prince became very angry. He called all the maids and servants of the palace and asked, "Which one of you did this?" All the maids and servants trembled in fear; no one could say anything, as they didn't know who had done it!

The prince then decided to solve the mystery himself. That night, the prince did not sleep; he lay there pretending to be asleep. When the night was very deep, the prince saw a maiden of supreme beauty emerging from the marigold flower in the corner of the room. The prince stared in amazement; he couldn't seem to blink. The beautiful girl first arranged and tidied everything in the room, then sat down to cook rice. After finishing cooking, she ate half of the rice herself and neatly arranged the rest. After that, she opened the betel-leaf box, prepared a betel leaf, and put it in her mouth. After chewing the betel leaf, her mouth became even more ruby-red. Then she spat the betel juice on the prince's white clothes, entered the marigold flower, and disappeared. Seeing this astonishing event, the prince was completely dumbfounded! The prince waited for the next night.

That night, the same thing happened! But this time, the prince waited for his opportunity. After she had finished all her tasks one by one and was about to spit the betel juice, the prince grabbed the beautiful maiden's hair. Then the prince asked, "Who are you? What is your identity?" The beautiful girl then cried out,

Kumar le kumar,
Lejot nodhorich pejot dhor!
(O Prince, O Prince, don't hold my hair, hold the hem of my garment!)

Then, as soon as the prince went to grab the hem of her garment, the girl zipped back into the marigold flower. What could the prince do then? He waited for the next night. This time, the prince made no mistake. As soon as the beautiful girl was about to spit the betel juice on his white clothes, he suddenly rushed and embraced her, and began to ask for her identity. Then Dh'bi, crying, told him everything, one by one—the conspiracy of her stepmother and stepsister and all her sorrows. The prince was overjoyed to have Dh'bi back; it seemed their conversation would never end. They were so engrossed in talking that they didn't even notice when dawn broke.

The next morning, everyone in the palace was utterly astonished to see Dh'bi! With the king and queen having their beloved daughter-in-law back, a flood of joy swept through the palace. In an instant, the palace regained its former vibrancy.

Meanwhile, the wicked old woman and her jealous daughter, K'bi, saw that they were in great peril. All their conspiracies had been exposed. Now, their lives were surely forfeit. The love for one's life is a great love. To save her life, K'bi threw herself at Dh'bi's feet. Crying, she pleaded for forgiveness. Dh'bi had a very soft heart. Seeing K'bi's tears, her heart melted. She forgave all their faults. However, the people of the palace drove them away with scorn.

The king and queen were overjoyed to have their beloved daughter-in-law back. It was as if a festival of happiness had begun in the palace. By the king's order, a flood of joy flowed through the entire kingdom again for seven days and seven nights. By the prince's order, Dh'bi's old father was also brought to the palace with honor. The prince requested him to stay in the palace permanently. Having her father near, Dh'bi was ecstatic! Tears of joy streamed from her eyes. The prince was also very happy in her happiness. All the subjects of the kingdom became partners in their joy. Some time later, after the king and queen passed away, the prince became king and Dh'bi became queen, and they ruled the kingdom in great happiness.

Legend has it that the yellow bird we see in our country is none other than Dh'bi, who still exists in that form.
