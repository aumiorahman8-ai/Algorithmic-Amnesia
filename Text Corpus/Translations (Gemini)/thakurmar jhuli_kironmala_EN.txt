Days pass, nights pass—Arun, Barun, and Kiranmala grow like the moon, blossom like flowers. When the forest birds heard Arun, Barun, and Kiran's laughter, they would come and sing; when they heard their cries, the forest deer would come running. The three siblings played, swaying and swinging—their dancing broke up a market of moons in the Brahmin's courtyard!

In the blink of an eye, the three siblings grew up. Kiranmala wouldn't let a single speck of dust settle in the house; she wouldn't let a single fly land on their cow, Kajallata. The two brothers, Arun and Barun, would read and listen; when fruits ripened, they would pick them; they would chase and catch the deer of the forest. Then, the three siblings would gather flowers in a basket and adorn their home until it was completely covered.

What more could the Brahmin ask for? Kiranmala, like a mother, would bring a basket full of flowers and offer lamps and sandalwood paste. The Brahmin would light the incense, ring the bell, and perform his worship, chanting “Bom-Bom!”

Days went by like this. Arun and Barun learned all the Brahmin's knowledge; Kiranmala took the Brahmin's household into her hands.

Then one day, calling the three children to him, the Brahmin placed his hands on their heads and said, “Arun, Barun, my dear Kiran, everything is now yours. I have no more sorrows. Leaving you all, I now go to another kingdom; be mindful of all you see and hear before you eat.” The three siblings began to weep, and the Brahmin departed for the heavens.

Days pass in sorrow upon sorrow—the king’s palace is dark. The king said, “No! My kingdom is shrouded in sin. Come, I will go hunting again.” Once more, the drums of the hunt sounded in the royal palace.

The king had gone hunting, and on that very day, the heavens broke open. In the storm, the tempest, the wind and rain—separated from his companions, losing all sense of path and direction, in the pitch-black darkness and pouring rain—the king spent the night in the hollow of a tree.

The next day, the king walked and walked, but there was no end to the path. The sun blazed down, the horizons were desolate; where were there any people, where was there any water? Panting and gasping, tormented by hunger and thirst, the king saw a house in the distance. The king walked towards that house.

Arun, Barun, and Kiranmala, the three siblings, looked and saw—what was this? A man, whose hands, feet, body, and head were all glittering! Seeing this, Arun and Barun were astonished; Kiran went and stood by her brother.

The king called out, “Is anyone there? Save me with a little water.”

Running, the siblings brought water. After drinking the water, the astonished king asked, “Children of gods, divine maiden—who are you in this lonely land?”

Arun said, “We are the children of a Brahmin!”

The king’s heart thumped, his mind grew restless—could such children be born in a Brahmin’s house! But the king could not say anything; gazing and gazing, looking and looking, tears began to well up in his eyes. The king said, “I did not drink water, I drank milk! Listen, my children, I am the sorrowful king of this land. If you ever need anything, let me know, and I will do it.” Saying this, the king rose with a sigh.

Then Kiran asked, “Brother! What do kings have?”

Arun and Barun replied, “We don’t know, sister—only that it is written in books that kings have elephants, horses, and palaces.”

Kiran said, “Where would we get elephants and horses? Build a palace.”

Arun and Barun said, “Alright.”

“Alright”—days passed into nights, who knows from what kingdom they brought what, the sweat of their brow fell to the ground, there was no hunger, no thirst, for twelve months and thirty-six days the sun and moon circled, and Arun and Barun built a palace. Arun and Barun worked, while their sister Kiranmala filled pot after pot with water from the brimming riverbank and brought it to them. In twelve months and thirty-six days, that palace was built.

Seeing that palace, Mayadanava would fast in envy, and Vishwakarma would abandon his home. Arun, Barun, and Kiran’s palace touched the sun’s seat and snatched the moon’s throne! The white marble gleamed, the white jewels shone; silver doors at every entrance, golden pitchers on every spire! Around the palace were flowering trees and fruit trees—so dense the birds could not be contained. The palace was fragrant with sweet scents, a city of sweetness with the calls of birds!

Gods and demons gazed upon the home of Arun, Barun, and Kiran!

One day, a sanyasi was passing by on the other side of the river. As he walked, the sanyasi said:

“In this lonely land, in this lonely forest, who are you, sister and brothers?
Who has built such a palace, that has no equal!”

From the palace, Arun replied:

“The ever-new moonlight falls here on its own,
In the home of the siblings, Arun, Barun, and Kiranmala!”

The sanyasi said:
“Arun, Barun, and Kiranmala’s beautiful royal palace,
A joy to see, a joy to hear, would be even more glorious.
Such a palace would be even more enchanting,
But something is wanted, something is missing, and so its beauty is not complete.
Such a palace should have—golden fruits on silver trees.
Water of falling pearls would cascade down.
I would hear the sweet song of a golden bird in a diamond tree—
Jewel-like pebbles would be scattered on the path.
Then such a palace would be the best in the three worlds,—
Each call of the golden bird a sea of happiness.”

Hearing this, Arun, Barun, and Kiran called out and said:
“Where is such a silver tree,
Where is such a bird,
Where is that pearl-fall,
Tell us, and we will bring them.”

The sanyasi said:
“To the northeast, north of the east,
There is a Mountain of Illusion,
Where golden fruits grow forever
On a real diamond tree.
Cascading down, the pearl-fall
Flows coolly by,
A golden bird sits there
On the branches of the tree!
The Mountain of Illusion is veiled in illusion,
It shoots arrows of illusion.
He who can bring these things
Is a great hero!”

As he spoke, the sanyasi walked away. Arun and Barun said, “Sister, we will bring all these things.”

Arun said, “Brother Barun, sister Kiran, you two stay here. I will go to the Mountain of Illusion and bring everything back.” Saying this, Arun gave a sword to Barun and Kiran. “If you see that the sword has rusted, know that I am no longer alive.” Leaving the sword, Arun departed.

Days pass, months pass; Barun and Kiran take out the sword every day to look at it. One day, upon taking out the sword, Barun’s face grew pale; he called out, “Sister, brother is no longer in this world! Keep this bow and arrow, I am leaving. If the arrowhead falls off and the bowstring breaks, know that I too am gone.”

Seeing the rust on Arun’s sword, Kiranmala was distraught with tears. She took Barun’s bow and arrow and said, “Oh God! Let brother Barun bring brother Arun back!”

Traveling on and on, Barun reached the land of the Mountain of Illusion. At once, music began to play all around, celestial nymphs danced, and call after call came from behind—

“Prince! Prince! Turn back! Turn back! Listen to us!”

The moment Barun turned to look, he turned to stone. “Alas! My brother has also been turned to stone.”

And so he was; who would come to rescue them? Arun and Barun remained as stone for the rest of their lives.

Waking at dawn, Kiranmala saw that the arrowhead had fallen off, the bowstring had broken—brother Arun was gone, and brother Barun was gone too. Kiranmala did not weep, did not despair, did not wipe her tears; she got up, gave straw and feed to Kajallata, gave water to the roots of the plants, and then, dressing in a prince’s attire, with a crown on her head and a sword in her hand—after kissing Kajallata’s calf and the fawn,

In the blink of an eye, Kiranmala set out for the Mountain of Illusion.

On she goes—on she goes—Kiranmala rises like a fire, rushes ahead of the wind; who sees her, who does not! Day and night, mountains and jungles, sun and flood all tumbled past; halting the storm, flashing like lightning, in thirteen nights and thirty-three days, Kiranmala reached the mountain.

Instantly, from all sides, demons, giants, tigers, bears, snakes, elephants, lions, buffaloes, ghosts, and ghouls came and surrounded Kiranmala.

One shouts, “Prince, I will swallow you!”
Another shouts, “Prince, I will eat you!”

“Hmph… Hoom… Hah!”
“Hum… Hum… Hah!”
“Hum!… Hmph…!”
“Grrr!…”

Drums beat on her back—

“Ta kata dha kata
Vang vang chang
Cut off the prince’s
Leg!”

Cymbals clash, jhan jhan—
Clappers clang, khan khan—
Drums large and small—kettledrums—
Swords flash, scimitars swish—

Celestial nymphs dance, “Prince, prince, listen now!”
Arrows of illusion—bowstrings drawn on bow after bow;---

Above, a torrent of rain and thunder, the roar of clouds like a hundred thousand kettledrums—with the sound and the noise, the sky splits apart, mountains and hills overturn, the earth cracks open! The seven worlds tremble violently, lightning, thunderbolts, hail, flashes!

No! Nothing worked! All was in vain, all was false! Kiranmala was not a prince; Kiranmala did not turn to look in any direction. How many stones crumbled under her feet, how many stones melted away—lowering her eyelids, gripping her sword tightly, Kiranmala went straight on with a whoosh and arrived right at the base of the diamond tree with the golden fruit.

And at that very moment, the golden bird in the diamond tree cried out, “You have come? You have come? It is good. Take this spring water, take this flower, take me, take that arrow there, take that bow there, take them, take them, do not delay; take everything, and that drum there, strike the drum.”

With each word the bird spoke, Kiranmala took one thing. After taking them, Kiranmala struck the drum.

Everything fell silent! The Mountain of Illusion was still. Only the call of the cuckoo, the whistle of the magpie-robin, the dance of the peacock!

Then the bird said,
“Kiranmala, sprinkle the cool spring water.”

Kiranmala tilted the golden pitcher and sprinkled the water. All around, the mountain rumbled, all the stones began to crackle—wherever a drop of water fell, all the princes from all the ages who had been turned to stone sat up, stretching their limbs in the blink of an eye.

In moments, all the stones turned into hundreds of thousands of princes. The princes folded their hands and bowed to Kiranmala,
“Blessed hero of seven ages!”

Arun and Barun, their eyes melting with tears,
said, “Blessed sister from our mother’s womb.”

From above, the golden bird said,
“Arun, Barun, Kiranmala,
You have lit up the three worlds!”

(12)

Arriving at the palace, Arun, Barun, and Kiranmala gave grass and water to Kajallata, untied Kajallata’s calf, bathed the fawn, cleaned the courtyard, watered the base of every tree, and cleared away the rubbish. After doing all this, they planted the seed of the silver tree and the branch of the diamond tree in the garden, opened the mouth of the pitcher of pearl-fall water, and scattered the pearl-fruits. They said to the golden bird, “Bird! Now sit on the tree.”

Swiftly, the diamond tree grew tall; with a rustle, the silver tree spread its leaves. On the silver branches and diamond boughs, clusters of bright red golden fruits began to sway; on a diamond branch, the golden bird sat and began to sing in a thousand tunes. All around, pearl-fruits shimmered in layers—and amidst them, the water of pearls from the cool spring began to cascade down.

The bird said, “Ah!”

Arun, Barun, and Kiran, the three siblings, embraced each other.

(13)

If the birds of the forest could not stay away, if the deer of the forest could not stay away, how could people? They came running and saw—“Ah! What a palace! The city of Indra has descended to Earth.”

The news reached the king. Hearing it, the king said, “Is that so! Those Brahmin’s children did all this!”

That night, the golden bird said, “Arun, Barun, Kiranmala! Invite the king.”

The three siblings said, “What! What will we feed the king if we invite him?”

The bird said, “I will tell you that!”

At dawn, Arun and Barun went and invited the king.

The golden bird said, “Kiran! In the room where His Majesty the King will sit to eat, hang me there.”

Kiran said, “Alright.”

With his entourage and great pomp, the king came to the feast and saw—what!! The king arrived, saw, and was startled; he looked and looked—and was left speechless. What was in the nooks and corners of the palace was more than what could fill the royal treasury. “Where did they get all this? Are they even human! Alas!!” One moment the king laughed with joy, the next he was awash in sorrow—ah, if only these were his children!

The king saw the garden, he saw the spring; seeing and hearing everything, with happiness and sorrow, tears streamed from the king’s eyes. Covering his eyes with his hand, the king said, “I can bear no more. Let’s go inside.”

Inside, there were jewels here, pearls there, emeralds here, diamonds there. The king was astounded.

Then, the king went to the dining hall. Foods of every kind, on plate after plate, platter after platter, in bowl after bowl, in pot after pot, were brought before the king! The room filled with their sweet aroma.

In wonder and amazement, the king slowly came and took his seat. Slowly, the astonished king placed his hand on a plate and—

—the king lifted his hand and sat back!

“What! It’s all made of gold coins!”

“What of it?” said a voice. “Can this not be eaten?”

“Why not? Payesh, pitha, kheer, cream, sweets, sweetmeats, juices, ladoos—can they not be eaten?”

The king said, “Who is speaking? Arun, Barun, Kiran! Are you also joking with me? Payesh of gold coins, pitha of pearls, sweets of gems, sweetmeats of jewels—how can a person eat these things? Can this be eaten?”

From above his head, someone said,
“Can a human give birth to a puppy?”

“—What?”
“Your Majesty, can a human give birth to a kitten?”
“—What!” The king started! He saw that it was the golden bird speaking.

“Your Majesty, if a human cannot eat these things, then,
how can a wooden doll be born from a human womb?”

The king said, “That’s right, that’s right—what have I done!!” The king rose from his seat.

The golden bird said, “Your Majesty, do you understand now? These are your children. Your wicked sisters-in-law lied and showed you a puppy, a kitten, and a wooden doll.”

The king, trembling, his face streaming with tears, took Arun, Barun, and Kiran into his arms. “Alas! If only the poor queen were here today!”

The golden bird whispered, “Arun, Barun, Kiran! In the hut on the other side of the river, your mother lives. Your mother’s days pass in great sorrow, on the verge of death; go and bring her here.”

The three siblings, astonished and melting with tears, brought their mother. The sorrowful mother thought, “Ah, I have found my children in heaven!”

The golden bird sang,

“Arun, Barun, Kiran,
Three treasures of the three worlds.
Having lost such gems,
Life was in vain.
Arun, Barun, Kiranmala,
Today you have ended all sorrow.”

And what then? A festival of joy began. The king brought his entire kingdom and established his royal court in the palace of Arun, Barun, and Kiran. For seven days and seven nights, all the subjects played riotously with jewels, pearls, diamonds, and emeralds.

Then one day, a group of the kingdom’s executioners went with a great clamor to the houses of the grass-cutter and the cook, set them on fire, and after burying the queen’s two wretched sisters under a mound of thorns upon thorny stakes, they returned.

After that, the king, the queen, Arun, Barun, and Kiranmala, with their grandchildren and great-grandchildren, became masters of countless fortunes and reigned for ages and ages.
