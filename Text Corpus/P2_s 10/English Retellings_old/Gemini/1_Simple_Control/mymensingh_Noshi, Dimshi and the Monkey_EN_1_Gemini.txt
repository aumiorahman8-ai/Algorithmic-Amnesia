In a small Bengali village, lived two orphan siblings: Noshi, a clever and hardworking sister, and Dimshi, her mischievous and often lazy brother. They lived in a small, crumbling hut on the edge of the forest, struggling to make ends meet. Noshi toiled from dawn till dusk, collecting firewood and selling it in the market. Dimshi, on the other hand, preferred to spend his days lazing around or playing pranks.

One day, as Noshi was gathering firewood deep in the forest, she stumbled upon a magnificent banyan tree. Underneath it sat a large, wise-looking monkey. The monkey was surrounded by a pile of shimmering mangoes, their fragrance filling the air.

"Good day, child," the monkey greeted Noshi in a surprisingly gentle voice. "These are special mangoes. They are sweeter than honey and will never rot. You may have one, if you promise to treat it with respect and never share it with someone unworthy."

Noshi, hesitant but tempted by the tempting fragrance, agreed. The monkey gave her one perfect, golden mango. She thanked him profusely and carefully wrapped the mango in her shawl. As she walked back home, she couldn't help but imagine the delicious treat that awaited her.

Back at the hut, Dimshi saw the wrapped bundle peeking out from under her shawl. He pestered her relentlessly, demanding to know what she was hiding. Noshi, usually patient, finally succumbed to his whining and told him about the magical mango and the monkey's warning.

Dimshi, of course, completely disregarded the warning. "Nonsense!" he exclaimed. "Such a fine mango is too good for just one person. We should share it with the whole village and become popular!"

Noshi pleaded with him, reminding him of the monkey's words, but Dimshi was deaf to her reasoning. He grabbed the mango, ran straight to the village gathering place, and announced that he had a magical mango to share.

The villagers, including the greedy village headman, gathered around with hungry eyes. Dimshi, brimming with pride, began to cut the mango. The moment the knife touched the fruit, however, something strange happened. The golden mango instantly turned into a pile of rotten, maggot-infested mess, emitting the foulest stench imaginable.

The villagers recoiled in disgust, shouting insults at Dimshi. He was chased out of the village, covered in the rotten mango remains. Ashamed and distraught, he returned to the hut, where Noshi was waiting with tears in her eyes.

Noshi, though heartbroken, didn't scold him. "We must go back to the monkey and apologize," she said.

The next morning, Noshi and Dimshi returned to the banyan tree. The monkey was sitting there as before, but his expression was stern. He looked at Dimshi with disappointment.

"You have squandered the gift," he said. "Because of your greed and lack of respect, the magic is gone."

Dimshi, finally understanding the gravity of his actions, was overcome with remorse. He knelt before the monkey, begging for forgiveness. Noshi also pleaded on his behalf, explaining his youthful foolishness and promising that he had learned his lesson.

The monkey, seeing their sincerity, softened. "I cannot give you another mango," he said, "but I can offer you something else." He pointed to a cluster of small, ordinary-looking mangoes on the tree. "These are not magical, but they are sweet and wholesome. Work hard, cultivate them with care, and share them honestly with others. That is a magic of its own."

Noshi and Dimshi, humbled by their experience, gratefully accepted the monkey's offering. They learned to work together, cultivating the mangoes with diligence and sharing them with their neighbors. Dimshi finally learned the value of hard work, honesty, and respect. From that day on, the siblings lived contentedly, their bond strengthened by the lessons learned from the wise monkey and the magical, then rotten, mango. They understood that true wealth lay not in fleeting magic, but in hard work, honesty, and compassion for others. They lived by the words of the monkey and their tale was whispered throughout the villages - a reminder that greed and dishonesty always turn to rot, while hard work and humility bear sweet fruit.
