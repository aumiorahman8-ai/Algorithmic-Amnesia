Okay, here’s my attempt at retelling the folktale of Dalim Kumar from Thakurmar Jhuli, aiming to retain the original Bengali cultural nuances, kinship terms, and specific rituals as much as possible:

**Dalim Kumar**

Listen, baba (child), there once lived a raja (king) who had seven rani (queens). But alas, not a single one bore him an heir. He was old, his beard was white, and his heart ached with the thought of who would rule after him. He went to the temple every day, praying to Devi (Goddess Durga) for a son and heir.

The youngest rani, the *choto rani*, was good, kind, and devoted to her husband. She suffered the most from the other ranis’ taunts. They called her barren, *banjha*, and whispered behind their veils, scheming for ways to remain in the Raja's favour.

One day, a *sadhu* (holy man), his jhola (bag) overflowing with sacred ash and wisdom, came to the palace gates, chanting "Haribol! Haribol!" The Raja, always mindful of his duty, greeted him with respect. He washed the Sadhu's feet with perfumed water, a customary pranam, and offered him a seat.

"O Maharaj (Great King)," the Sadhu declared, "I have seen your sorrow. Devi feels your pain." He instructed the Raja to prepare a special *puja* (prayer ritual) to Devi Durga at the *ghat* (riverbank). "Offer seven *pedas* (sweets) made with pure ghee and honey to the Goddess. Only one rani, the queen with the purest heart, must offer these pedas and ask for a son."

The Raja did as he was told. He informed all seven ranis, but knowing the *choto rani's* piety, he secretly wished she would be the chosen one. The six older ranis, full of jealousy, saw their opportunity. They mixed poison into their pedas, hoping to ensure whoever ate them died and they remained the Raja’s favorite.

The *choto rani*, unaware of their wicked plan, offered her peda to Devi with true devotion. The Goddess, pleased with her pure heart, appeared in a dream and warned her, "Do not eat the peda. Take it far away and bury it in the garden."

Next morning, the choto rani, afraid and obedient, secretly buried her peda just as Devi told her to. Days turned into weeks. At the very spot, a beautiful, shimmering pomegranate tree sprouted from the ground. The tree grew laden with magnificent pomegranates, each one glowing as if lit from within.

The other ranis, consumed by jealousy, saw the tree and realized the choto rani wasn't dead. They hatched another malicious plan. They told the Raja the choto rani had planted the cursed tree herself to destroy the kingdom. They said the fruits were poisonous and asked the Raja to chop down the tree.

The Raja, heartbroken and believing their lies, ordered the tree to be felled. As the woodcutter swung his axe, the largest pomegranate burst open, and out sprang a radiant boy, his eyes filled with the wisdom of ages. He pleaded, “Baba (Father) do not kill me! I am Dalim Kumar, your son!”

The Raja, stunned, embraced his son. The choto rani, hearing the commotion, ran outside and fainted with joy at the sight of her son.

The wicked ranis, seeing their treachery revealed, panicked. They cursed the boy, saying, "He will turn into a stone every night and only turn back into a boy with the touch of his mother's tears."

Sure enough, every night, Dalim Kumar turned into a stone murti (statue). The choto rani wept every single night, her tears falling on the cold stone until Daalim Kumar turned back into an alive, beautiful boy every dawn.

The six older ranis, seeing the success of their curse, were even more furious. This time they bribed the Raja's minister, the *deowan*, to take Dalim Kumar away. They paid him to take Dalim Kumar deep into the forest and leave him there, to be devoured by tigers and wolves.

The loyal deowan however realized the evil of the other ranis and couldn't kill the boy. Instead he took Dalim Kumar to a village and left him with a poor Brahmin family, telling them the Raja himself had sent the boy to be cared for.

Dalim Kumar grew up with the Brahmin family, strong and wise. One day, while tending cows he heard of a beautiful princess who was mute. Every Raja and prince in the world had tried to make her speak, but none had succeeded.

Dalim Kumar, with nothing to lose, decided to try his luck. He travelled to the kingdom with his newfound fortune and asked to try to make the princess speak. At first, the Raja refused, but was so impressed by Dalim Kumar's beauty that he eventually agreed.

Dalim Kumar sat near the princess and played a melodious tune on his bansuri (flute), a raga (musical mode) filled with the music of Bengal’s rivers and fields. He sang a song about a cursed boy, a jealous queen, and a mother’s love. The princess, hearing the tale, was moved to tears. She cried out, "He is Dalim Kumar! He is the rightful heir!"

The Raja, overjoyed that his daughter could speak, immediately agreed to have her marry Dalim Kumar. Word traveled to the old Raja who immediately came to the kingdom with his choto rani and repented with full heart.

Dalim Kumar reunited with his parents, punished the wicked ranis and ruled justly as a wise and benevolent Raja. He loved his rani and was a good son, honoring his foster parents and all who had helped him along the way.

And so, that is the story of Dalim Kumar, born of a pomegranate and the unwavering love of a pure-hearted mother. Joi Maa Durga! (Victory to Mother Durga!)
