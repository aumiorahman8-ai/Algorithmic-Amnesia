**Khumpui: A Rangamati Jumma Folktale**

The story whispers through the bamboo groves of Rangamati, a tale of a mischievous spirit named Khumpui. In a humble Jumma (tribal community of Chittagong Hill Tracts) village nestled amidst the hills, lived a family consisting of an old grandfather – *Tata* – a grandmother – *Aama*,  their son’s wife - *Bou*, and their bright-eyed granddaughter, Laochi. Laochi was *Tata's* darling, his constant companion, helping him with everything from tending the *Jhum* (slash-and-burn cultivation practiced in the hills) fields to fetching water from the *chhara* (stream).

Now, Khumpui was a restless spirit, invisible to most, but with a penchant for playful, sometimes troublesome, pranks. He lived in the deep *ban* (forest) that surrounded the village, a realm sacred and respected. This forest was not just a source of livelihood; it was the home of spirits, ancestors, and the very soul of the Jumma people.

One day, *Bou*, busy with household chores—pounding rice, weaving *Thami* (traditional wrap-around skirt)—kept setting aside rice for the family's evening meal.  Khumpui, unseen, took a sneaky interest. Every time  *Bou* tried to measure out the rice,  Khumpui, invisible to her, would magically whisk grains away, leaving her perplexed.  “*Ki holo eta!*” (“What is happening?!”) she’d exclaim, attributing it to bad luck or perhaps a mischievous rat.

Laochi, returning from the *chhara* with a heavy *kolshi* (earthen pot) of water on her head, noticed her mother-in-law's frustration.  “*Bou*, what troubles you?” she inquired. *Bou* explained about the disappearing rice, her voice laced with worry.

That evening, Tata, a man wise in the ways of the forest, sensed an unusual energy in the house. He knew that Khumpui sometimes visited, drawn by the smell of food or the sounds of merriment. Rather than fear him, he believed in respecting the spirit world and maintaining harmony.  He advised *Bou* to leave a small offering – a handful of cooked sticky rice and a piece of dried fish – near the hearth, along with a whispered message: "O Khumpui, *daada* (elder brother), if you need food, please take this. But do not frighten us by making the rice disappear. We are simple people."

That night, as Laochi slept beside *Tata*, she dreamt of a little creature, no bigger than her hand, with twinkling eyes and a mischievous grin. He danced in the moonlight filtering through the bamboo walls, humming a song of the forest. In her dream, he apologized for troubling the family and promised to behave.

The next morning, *Bou* measured the rice, and lo and behold, it stayed put!  The family knew – Khumpui had heard *Tata's* plea, and Laochi’s dream validated their belief.

From then on, the family always kept a small offering by the hearth for Khumpui – not out of fear, but out of respect.  They understood that the spirit world was interwoven with their own, and that maintaining a peaceful relationship with *ban-devata* (forest deities) like Khumpui was essential for their well-being.

Years passed. Laochi grew into a young woman, learned the traditional songs and dances, and married a *jumbo* (village elder) son from a neighboring village.  Whenever a new house was built or a bountiful harvest reaped, the people of the village would perform a small *puja* (prayer ceremony), offering rice beer and chicken to appease the spirits of the forest, remembering to always leave a portion for Khumpui, ensuring that the mischievous spirit remained a playful guardian, rather than a troublesome prankster. The story of Khumpui became a cherished part of their oral tradition, a reminder to respect the delicate balance between the human world and the spirit world, and to treat even the smallest of beings with kindness and understanding. It reminds them that even a mischievous spirit can be appeased with a little respect, and even brought into the fold of familial harmony.
