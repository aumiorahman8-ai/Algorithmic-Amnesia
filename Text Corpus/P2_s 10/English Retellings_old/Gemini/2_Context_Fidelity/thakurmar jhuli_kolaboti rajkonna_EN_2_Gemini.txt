Alright, let's retell the story of Kolavati Rajkonna (কলবতী রাজকন্যা), the Banana Blossom Princess, from Dakshinaranjan Mitra Majumdar’s Thakurmar Jhuli, trying to preserve its cultural nuances and kinship terms.

The story begins in a kingdom, long ago, where lived a Raja (King) and his Rani (Queen). They were blessed with six sons, all brave and accomplished. Yet, their hearts ached for a daughter. They prayed to the gods night and day, offering puja (worship) and dan-dakshina (charity) to the brahmins. Finally, Bhagoban (God) heard their pleas. A divine voice instructed the Raja, "Do not despair. You will find your daughter within a Kalagach (banana plant)."

Overjoyed, the Raja searched his entire kingdom until he found a Kalagach in his own bari (compound) which was unlike any other. It shimmered with an otherworldly glow. He, along with the Rani and the six Rajkumars (Princes), gathered around the Kalagach. With trembling hands, the Raja ordered his most trusted servants to carefully slice open the kola (banana) flower, the shimmering kolaboti.

And lo! From the heart of the kolaboti emerged a beautiful baby girl, radiant as the morning sun. Their prayers answered, the Raja and Rani named her Kolavati Rajkonna, the Banana Blossom Princess. The entire palace echoed with joyous uludhvani (a high-pitched, tremulous sound made by women during auspicious occasions) and sankha sounds (blowing of conch shells).

Kolavati Rajkonna grew up surrounded by love and luxury. Her six brothers doted on her, buying her the finest silks from Banaras and the sweetest sandesh (Bengali sweets). They were fiercely protective of their only sister.

Now, the time came for the Rajkumars to marry. The Raja organized a swayamvara (a ceremony where the princess chooses her husband from a group of suitors) for each of his six sons. Each Rakjumar, one after the other, went to distant lands and bought beautiful brides and with a pomp and fanfare, brought them to the Rajbari(king's palace).

The six new boudis (sister-in-laws) were jealous of Kolavati’s beauty and the love she received from her brothers. The seeds of jealousy began to sprout in their hearts like weeds. They secretly poisoned the minds of their respective husbands, whispering that Kolavati was pampered and lazy, contributing nothing to the family.

One day, the eldest bou (wife of the eldest prince), driven by spite, suggested a test. “Dada (elder brother), let Kolavati prove her worth. Let her make the finest pithas (rice cakes) for the upcoming Durga Puja!” The other boudis chimed in agreement, their eyes gleaming with malice.

Kolavati, though saddened by their coldness, accepted the challenge. She went to the kitchen and, with great devotion and skill, began to prepare the pithas. She ground the rice flour, grated the coconut, and mixed in jaggery with the best of her ability. But as she was cooking, the boudis, with evil intentions, threw a handful of ash into the batter.

Kolavati, unaware of their treachery, continued to cook. When the pithas were ready, they were, of course, horrible. Bitter and gritty, they were inedible. The boudis spread rumors throughout the Rajbari, declaring Kolavati incapable and worthless. She was now a target for ridicule.

Heartbroken and humiliated, Kolavati retreated to her room, tears streaming down her face. One night, overwhelmed by despair, she decided to leave the palace. She packed a small bundle of clothes and a few silver coins. Under the veil of darkness, she slipped out of the Rajbari, leaving behind everything she had ever known.

She wandered through forests and fields, her feet sore and her heart heavy. Finally, she came to a dense jungle and found a small, dilapidated hut. Taking shelter within, she fell asleep, exhausted.

The next morning, she woke up to the sound of birdsong. She noticed a gach (tree) laden with plump berries. She picked and ate them, her hunger momentarily appeased. Resolving to survive, she decided to gather firewood and try to sell it in a nearby gram (village).

She worked tirelessly, collecting wood and carrying it to the gram. Slowly, she began to earn a meager living. She bought food and clothes, but never forgot her former life of luxury. She lived simply, always maintaining her dignity and grace.

One day, while selling firewood in the gram, she encountered a wealthy merchant, a shaudagor (merchant). Impressed by her beauty, her gentle nature, and her hard work, he fell in love with her. He proposed marriage, promising her a life of comfort and happiness.

Kolavati, though hesitant at first, saw the sincerity in his eyes. She confided in him about her past, her royal lineage, and the injustice she had suffered. The shaudagor, far from being deterred, vowed to avenge her. They were married with great pomp and ceremony, and Kolavati became the shaudagor’s wife.

Now, the shaudagor, being a shrewd businessman, devised a plan. He began trading with the kingdom of Kolavati's father. He deliberately bought and sold goods at a loss, creating a situation where the Raja's treasury was dwindling rapidly.

Hearing of this economic crisis, the Raja called a meeting of his ministers. He lamented his misfortune, wondering how to save his kingdom from ruin. The shaudagor, posing as a loyal merchant, offered his services. "Maharaj (Your Majesty)," he said, "I can save your kingdom, but I have one condition."

The Raja, desperate for help, agreed to any condition. The shaudagor then revealed his true intention: "I will only help if your six boudis cook a feast for me and my wife."

The Raja, though puzzled, agreed. He summoned his six boudis and instructed them to prepare a lavish feast for the shaudagor and his wife. The boudis, arrogant and confident, readily accepted the challenge.

As the boudis bustled about the kitchen, preparing the feast, the shaudagor and his wife arrived at the palace. The Raja greeted them with honor and led them to the dining hall. The boudis, eager to impress, presented their dishes with pride.

But as the shaudagor and his wife tasted the food, they began to choke and cough. The dishes were bland, burnt, over-salted, and utterly inedible. The Raja was horrified. He demanded to know what was wrong.

Then, the shaudagor's wife, Kolavati, stepped forward. With tears streaming down her face, she revealed her true identity. "I am Kolavati Rajkonna, the daughter you abandoned because of these wicked women! They threw ash in my pitha batter, causing me to flee my home!"

The Raja was stunned. He looked at his boudis, their faces pale with fear and guilt. The truth was revealed. Their jealousy, their treachery, their cruelty, all stood bare for everyone to see.

The Raja was filled with remorse. He banished his boudis from the kingdom, showering them with scorn. He begged Kolavati for forgiveness. Kolavati, though hurt, forgave her father and her brothers. She returned to the Rajbari, not as a pampered princess, but as a wise and respected woman.

The shaudagor helped restore the kingdom's wealth. Kolavati ruled alongside her brothers, advising them with her wisdom and compassion. The kingdom prospered under her guidance, and the story of Kolavati Rajkonna, the Banana Blossom Princess who overcame adversity, became a beloved folktale told and retold in the villages of Bengal, a timeless reminder of the power of resilience, forgiveness, and the ultimate triumph of good over evil.  The story is a popular one retold for generations in every bengali household, especially to the grandchildren by their 'thakuma' (grandmother).
