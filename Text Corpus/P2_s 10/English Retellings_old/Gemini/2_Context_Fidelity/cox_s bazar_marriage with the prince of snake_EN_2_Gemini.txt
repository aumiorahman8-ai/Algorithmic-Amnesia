**The Tale of Champa and the Shaaper Rajkumar**

In a small fishing village nestled along the Naf River, close to Teknaf, lived a beautiful young woman named Champa. Her *baba*, father, was a poor fisherman, and her *maa*, mother, spent her days weaving intricate fishing nets and tending their small vegetable garden. Champa helped her *maa*, often singing her melodious songs while drawing water from the *pukur*, the pond.

One day, while drawing water near a mound of earth overgrown with *shaak*, leafy vegetables, she heard a soft hissing sound. Startled, she saw a magnificent snake, its scales shimmering like emeralds in the sunlight. But this wasn't just any snake; this was the *Shaaper Rajkumar*, the Snake Prince of the deep forest, cursed to live as a snake until a maiden accepts him as a husband willingly.

The *Shaaper Rajkumar* spoke to Champa in a voice that sounded like the rustling of leaves, telling her of his plight. He told her that he had been cursed by a vengeful *daini* (witch) because he refused to give her a priceless jewel from his kingdom. He begged Champa to break the curse by marrying him.

Champa, though initially frightened, felt a strange pull towards the creature. She saw a deep sadness in his emerald eyes and felt compassion for his suffering. Remembering the stories her *didima*, grandmother, used to tell of *nagpuran* (snake worship) and the power of a woman's devotion, she bravely agreed.

News of Champa's decision spread like wildfire through the village. The villagers were terrified. "A snake! She will marry a *shap*!" they cried, but Champa's *baba* and *maa*, seeing her resolve and remembering their own faith in the supernatural, reluctantly agreed to support her.

The *Biye*, the wedding, was a simple affair, a poignant mix of fear and faith. Under the canopy of the *shimul tola*,the cotton-wood tree, a *chatal* was built, a makeshift platform for the rituals. The *gaye holud* ceremony, the turmeric anointment, was performed with hushed reverence.Champa, clad in a simple red *shaari* and adorned with jasmine flowers in her hair, was smeared with turmeric paste, her face radiating a mixture of apprehension and determination.Her *boron dala* was a simple bamboo tray filled with sweets and fruits.

The *kalratri*, the night before the wedding, was filled with nervous energy. The women of the village sang folk songs like *Bhaatiali* and *jari gaan*, attempting to ward off bad luck and strengthen Champa’s resolve. Special protective rituals were performed, involving the chanting of *mantras* by the village *moulvi* (religious leader) and the tying of *rakhis* (protective threads) on Champa's wrist.

On the wedding day itself , the *Shaaper Rajkumar*, still in his snake form, was placed inside a jeweled *dala*, a basket, and brought to the *chatal*. The *kazi* (marriage officiate), a respected figure in the village, recited the *nikaah*, the marriage vows.  Instead of accepting jewels and gold as *meher*, the bride price, a traditional practice (that is practiced by many Bengals in Coz's bazar also), Champa demanded only the promise that the *Shaaper Rajkumar* would always protect her and her family.

As the *kazi* declared them husband and wife, a miracle happened. The snake shed its skin, and before everyone's astonished eyes, a handsome prince stood in its place, his skin the color of polished bronze and his eyes sparkling with gratitude and love. The *daini's* curse had been broken by Champa's selfless act.

The prince revealed that his kingdom lay hidden beneath the sea, accessible only through a secret portal. He promised to take Champa there, not as a captive but as his queen. He revealed the secrets of his kingdom, where riches flowed like rivers and the people lived in harmony with nature.

The next morning, before they left, the prince showered Champa's family and the villagers with gold and precious gems. He taught them sustainable fishing practices, showing them how to respect the sea and its creatures. He even gave them a magical herb that would protect them from disease.

Thus, Champa, the humble fisherman's daughter, left her village to become the queen of the *Shaaper Rajkumar's* underwater kingdom.  She never forgot her roots and often visited her family, bringing with her the blessings of the sea and the bounty of her husband’s kingdom. The tale is still told in Cox's Bazar, a testament to the power of compassion, courage, and the enduring belief in the magic that lives within the heart of the land.
