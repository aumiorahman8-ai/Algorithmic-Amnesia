Alright, let's delve into the heart of Joypurhat and retell the haunting folktale of 'Morar Shakkhi' – 'Witness of the Bubbles.' I will try my best to preserve the cultural nuances, kinship terms, and rituals as accurately as possible.

The story begins in a small, bustling village nestled amidst rice paddies near Joypurhat. There lived two brothers, Boro Bhai (older brother) and Choto Bhai (younger brother). Boro Bhai, married to a demanding and sharp-tongued Bou (sister-in-law/wife), was known for his hard work. Choto Bhai, a gentler soul, was married to a kind and pious Bouthan (younger brother's wife), a woman of quiet grace.

Their father, Babathakur (respected father), before his death, had divided the ancestral land equally between the two brothers. However, Boro Bou, fuelled by greed, whispered poison into Boro Bhai’s ear. She convinced him that Choto Bhai was cheating him, that he was secretly reaping a larger harvest.

One Amavasya (new moon night), as black as coal, Boro Bhai, consumed by avarice and egged on by his wife, ambushed Choto Bhai on his way back from the haat (market town). He struck him down and, with his Bou’s help, dragged the lifeless body to the nearest pukur (pond). With heavy hearts, they weighted down the corpse and tossed it into the depths, hoping the darkness would swallow their crime.

Back home, they concocted a story about Choto Bhai disappearing, perhaps lured away by wandering bauls (mendicant singers) or taken by dacoits (bandits). Bouthan, grief-stricken and numb, performed the necessary rituals for her missing husband, offering prayers for his return. She kept vigil, lighting a diyak (oil lamp) every evening, hoping its flickering light would guide him back.

Days turned into weeks, but Choto Bhai did not return. A panchayat (village council) was convened. Boro Bhai loudly mourned his brother's supposed disappearance, even tearing his clothes to exemplify his grief. The matter remained unresolved, a dark cloud hanging over the village.

One day, a group of boys were playing near the pukur. They noticed something peculiar. Every day, at the precise same spot near the bank, shimmering bubbles rose to the surface of the water. They were unlike any bubbles they had ever seen. They seemed… to be whispering.

Being children, they initially dismissed it as mere fancy. But the recurring pattern intrigued them. They told their elders, who, hardened by life, scoffed at their tales of talking bubbles. However, one old Dada (grandfatherly figure), known for his wisdom and patience, listened intently.

He remembered an old lore. It was said that when a person is murdered unjustly, the ‘atmachchaya’ (shadow of the soul) remains tethered to the site of the crime. The bubbling water, he suspected, was the anguished spirit trying to speak.

He advised the villagers to observe the bubbles closely. He suggested performing a Pujah (Hindu prayer ritual) with offerings of dhoop (incense), phool (flowers) and ghee (clarified butter) near the pukur.

The villagers followed Dada's instructions. They gathered near the pukur at dusk, the air thick with incense and anticipation. As the pujah commenced, the bubbles grew in intensity. An old woman, a Shitolata (community leader with spiritual connections), began to chant mantras. Slowly, the story unfolded.

The bubbles, influenced by the chants and prayers, seemed to coalesce, forming fleeting images in the water: the dark Amavasya night, Boro Bhai’s angry face, the blow to the head, the body being dragged to the pukur.

The whispers grew louder, clearer. In hushed tones, the villagers heard the story of the murder, the greed, and the treachery. They recognized the face of Boro Bhai in the watery apparitions.

Confronted with this ‘morar shakkhi’ (witness of the bubbles), Boro Bhai and his wicked Bou collapsed, confessing to their heinous crime. Justice was served, not through human intervention alone, but through the silent testimony of the murdered soul. Boro Bhai and his wife faced village justice, which inevitably involved ostracization and restitution.

Bouthan, finally knowing the truth, performed the proper antyesti kriya (last rites) for her beloved husband, laying his soul to rest. The pukur, once a site of tragedy, became a hallowed place, a reminder that no crime, however well hidden, can escape the eyes of the divine, the whisper of the soul, and the truth that emerges from the depths of stillness, even in the form of ‘morar shakkhi’ – the witness of bubbling water.

This tale of 'Morar Shakkhi' reflects beliefs about justice, the importance of ritual, and the unwavering connection between the living and the departed spirit. The pond, in this story, is not just a body of water, it is a portal to the spiritual dimension, holding the memories and carrying the truth of the silenced voice.
