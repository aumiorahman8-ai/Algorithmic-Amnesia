Alright, let's dust off the pages of Thakurmar Jhuli and revisit the crafty tale of Sheyal Pandit (Fox Scholar). Imagine, now, sitting on a verandah bathed in the golden light of dusk, listening to Thakurma (Grandmother) narrate this familiar story:

"Anek anek din ager kotha. One upon a time, in a distant land, there lived a crafty Sheyal Pandit. He thought himself very clever, you see, a veritable 'bou-bhat-e achcha' (good at feasts). But his cleverness was mostly borrowed, a mask for pure laziness and greed.

Now, near a bustling village, there lived a hardworking peasant, a Chasha Bhai (Brother Farmer). The Chasha Bhai, with the sweat of his brow, tilled his fields, growing luscious pumpkins, kumuRo. He would load them onto his bullock cart, carefully arranged like precious jewels, and take them to the haat (market) to sell.

One day, Sheyal Pandit, stomach rumbling louder than a monsoon thunder, spied the Chasha Bhai's cart laden with kumuRo. His eyes gleamed like chana bhaja (fried chickpeas) under the sun. 'Ah,' he thought, 'opportunity knocks!'

He raced ahead and, finding a narrow part of the path, lay down, feigning death. He stiffened his legs, lolled his tongue out, and closed his eyes. He looked, truly, quite gone to meet his ancestors!

The Chasha Bhai, driving his bullocks slowly, noticed the seemingly lifeless Sheyal. 'Ram Ram!' he exclaimed, crossing himself. 'Ei ki holo? What happened? Poor creature!' He stopped his cart and, with a heavy heart, poked Sheyal Pandit with his Lathi (stick). No movement.

'Shotti korei toh more geche (Truly, he's dead)!' he sighed. The Chasha Bhai, being a pious and kind soul, thought it a sin to leave a creature unburied. He knew the proper rite for an animal death involved burial under a Neem tree, a sacred spot cleansing of earthly sins.

Thinking to give the Sheyal a proper 'antim sanskar' (final rites), he carefully picked up the seemingly lifeless creature and placed him on top of the kumuRo, amongst his precious pumpkins. 'Jaak, punno hobey (Well, I'll gain merit),' he muttered. Then, he continued on his way, humming a tune.

Now, Sheyal Pandit, as soon as the Chasha Bhai turned his back, sprang to life! He was heavier than he had anticipated! He quickly rolled off the largest, juiciest kumuRo, biting into it with gusto. 'Aha! Bhari mishti (So sweet)!' He devoured a considerable portion, throwing the rind aside. This continued all the way to the haat. He feasted like a Raja (king) riding in his palanquin!

Finally, the Chasha Bhai arrived at the haat. Eagerly, he began to unload his pumpkins. One by one, he lifted them down, surprised to find each a little lighter than he remembered. Then, he reached the bottom of the pile.

'O ma go!' He exclaimed. The biggest pumpkin was half-eaten, with muddy paw prints all over it! He instantly understood. 'Sheyal er chhal! (The fox's trick!)' he cried.

Enraged but also somewhat amused, he hatched a plan. He sold the remaining kumuRo for a good price, then bought a large, empty Dhol (drum). He placed it strategically near a tree, just outside the village.

That evening, Sheyal Pandit, stomach full of pumpkin and swaggering with self-importance, sauntered past the tree. He heard the faint sound of music emanating from the Dhol. Curiosity, a trait that often accompanies foolishness, got the better of him.

'Ki re baba? (What's going on?)' He inquired, peering inside. He saw the reflection of his own cunning face staring back at him. Thinking it was another Sheyal, a challenger maybe, he puffed out his chest and roared, 'Ke re tui? Who are you? Come out and fight!'

Of course, only his own echo answered him. He roared again, even louder. Still nothing.

Frustrated, he jumped inside the Dhol, determined to confront this 'challenger'. Now, the Chasha Bhai, who had been hiding nearby, quickly clamped the lid shut and secured it tight.

Locked inside the Dhol, Sheyal Pandit realized his folly. Panic set in. He banged and screamed, but it was no use.

The Chasha Bhai, chuckling to himself, took the Dhol to the Dhobi ghat (washerman's place) by the river. He bribed the Dhobi Dada (Brother Washerman) with a piece of gud (jaggery) and instructed him to give the Dhol a thorough beating all day.

All day long, the Dhobi Dada pounded the Dhol with his bat, the rhythmic ‘dhuk dhuk’ echoing through the village. Inside, Sheyal Pandit was getting the beating of his life!

By evening, when the Chasha Bhai returned, the cries of the Sheyal had subsided to feeble whimpers. The Dhobi Dada, his arms aching, handed over the Dhol.

The Chasha Bhai opened the lid and out crawled a thoroughly bruised and humbled Sheyal Pandit. He was no longer the swaggering 'bou-bhat-e achcha', but a sorry sight, whimpering and covered in dust.

'Ebar bujhli? (Now you understand?)' the Chasha Bhai said, sternly. 'Laziness and dishonesty never prosper. Go and earn your own living honestly!'

Sheyal Pandit, completely defeated, limped away into the jungle, never again to trouble the Chasha Bhai, or anyone else in the village, with his cunning tricks.

And that, my dear granddaughter, is the story of Sheyal Pandit. Remember, cleverness without honesty is like a boat without a rudder – it will eventually sink!"

Thakurma would then pat your head, her face crinkled with a knowing smile. The air would be filled with the scent of burning incense sticks and the distant calls of jackals, a reminder that even in the deepest jungle, honesty and hard work are always the best path.
