Once upon a time, there was a king who had seven queens: the Eldest Queen, the Second Queen, the Third Queen, the Fourth Queen, the Fifth Queen, the Unfavored Queen, and the Youngest Queen. The king had a vast kingdom and a magnificent palace, with elephants in the elephant-stables, horses in the horse-stables, jewels in the treasury, and chambers full of gold coins. The royal court was bustling with ministers, courtiers, soldiers, and guards.

However, the king was unhappy because none of his seven queens had given birth to a child. The kingdom was filled with sorrow as everyone passed their days in sadness.

One day, the queens went to the river ghat to bathe when a hermit appeared. The hermit placed a tree root in the Eldest Queen's hand and said, "Grind this and share it among the seven queens, and you will have sons as beautiful as the golden moon."

The queens were overjoyed and quickly finished their bathing. They returned to the kitchen, where they had different tasks: the Eldest Queen cooked the rice, the Second Queen cut the vegetables, the Third Queen cooked the curries, the Fourth Queen drew the water, the Fifth Queen assisted, the Unfavored Queen ground the spices, and the Youngest Queen cleaned the fish.

The hermit's root was with the Eldest Queen. She asked the Unfavored Queen to grind the root first. While grinding, the Unfavored Queen ate a small piece of the root. She then placed the paste in a golden bowl, covered it with a silver plate, and gave it to the Eldest Queen. The Eldest Queen ate some and passed it to the Second Queen, who ate a portion and gave it to the Third Queen. The Third Queen ate some and gave it to the Fifth Queen. The Fifth Queen ate the rest. When the Fourth Queen arrived, she saw only a tiny bit of residue left in the bowl. She ate that, leaving nothing for the Youngest Queen.

The Youngest Queen, who had cleaned the fish, ran frantically to catch up with the other queens. She met the Fourth Queen, who said, "Oh, you unfortunate one! You didn't get to eat the ground root? Go, hurry!" The Youngest Queen ran but arrived to find that not a speck of the ground root was left. She fell to the ground in a faint.

The five queens started blaming each other, and they all began to quarrel. The Youngest Queen's fish scattered across the courtyard, and her tears flooded the ground.

The Fourth Queen arrived and said, "Oh my! Did you all not save anything for her? What kind of people are you! Come, sister Youngest Queen, if a tiny bit is stuck to the grinding stone, I will wash it off and give it to you to drink. If God is willing, even that will give you a son as beautiful as the golden moon."

The other queens said, "Yes, that's right, that's right, there must be some on the grinding stone, wash it and give it to her." But to themselves, they thought, "Drinking water from a washed grinding stone—she won't get a golden-moon son, but a monkey-moon son."

The Youngest Queen wept and drank the little bit of water from the washed grinding stone. Afterward, she and the Fourth Queen went together to fetch water. The other queens chattered on about various things.

Ten months and ten days passed, and the five queens each gave birth to a son. Each son was like a golden moon! But the Eldest Queen's words came true; the Fourth Queen gave birth to an owl, and the Youngest Queen gave birth to a monkey.

Drums and cymbals began to play in front of the Eldest Queens' chambers. In the rooms of the Fourth Queen and the Youngest Queen, there was only weeping.

The king and all the people of the kingdom came and escorted the five queens to their chambers with triumphant celebration. No one even asked about the Fourth Queen or the Youngest Queen. Some time later, the Fourth Queen became a maid in the menagerie, and the Youngest Queen a servant who collected dung cakes, and they began to live their lives in sorrow and hardship.

Gradually, the king's sons grew up; the owl and the monkey grew up as well. The five princes were named Prince Hira, Prince Manik, Prince Moti, Prince Shankha, and Prince Kanchan. The owl was named Bhutum, and the monkey was named Buddhu. The five princes would ride five winged Pakshiraj horses. They were always accompanied by many soldiers and guards. Bhutum and Buddhu would play together on a branch of a small bakul tree next to their mothers' hut. When the five princes went out, they would beat one person today, another tomorrow; take one man's head today, another's tomorrow. The people of the kingdom grew utterly weary of them.

After playing, Bhutum and Buddhu would go to their respective mothers. Buddhu would help his mother gather dung cakes, and Bhutum would feed the baby birds in the menagerie. And every few days, the two of them would go for a walk in the forest to the south of the palace.

Bhutum's mother was the menagerie maid, and Buddhu's mother was the dung-cake-collecting servant. Some days they had food to eat, some days they did not. Buddhu would bring various kinds of fruits from the forest for both mothers. Bhutum would carry betel nuts in his beak for them to chew. In this way, Bhutum, Bhutum's mother, Buddhu, and Buddhu's mother passed their days.

One day, the five princes came galloping on their Pakshiraj horses to see the menagerie. On their way, they saw an owl and a monkey sitting on a bakul tree. As soon as they saw them, they ordered their soldiers and guards, "Catch that owl and monkey. We will keep them as pets." At once, the soldiers and guards cast a net over the bakul tree. Bhutum and Buddhu could not break the net. They were caught, put in a cage, and taken with the princes to the royal palace.

After cleaning the menagerie, Bhutum's mother came and saw that Bhutum was gone! After spreading out the dung cakes, Buddhu's mother came and saw that Buddhu was gone! Bhutum's mother dropped the broom from her hand and collapsed on the ground. Buddhu's mother threw down her dung-smeared basket and fell in a faint.

Arriving at the palace, Bhutum and Buddhu were astonished! There were massive buildings, elephants, horses, soldiers, guards—so many things!

Seeing all this, they thought, "Wow! Then why do we live on a bakul tree? And why do our mothers live in a hut?" Thinking this, they said, "O Prince Brothers, since you have brought us, bring our mothers too."

The princes saw this and thought, "Well! They speak like humans!" So they said, "Very well, very well. Tell us where your mothers are; we will bring them and keep them in the menagerie."

Bhutum said, "The menagerie maid is my mother."

Buddhu said, "The dung-cake-collecting servant is my mother!"

Hearing this, the princes burst out laughing.

"How can an owl be born to a human!"

"How can a monkey be born to a human!"

The princes did not know the story of the Youngest Queen and the Fourth Queen, but there was a soldier who did. He said, "Why not? We had two queens who gave birth to an owl and a monkey. For that reason, the king banished them. These must be that owl and monkey son."

Hearing this, the princes cried out, "Fie, fie!" They immediately kicked the cage and told the soldiers and guards, "Drive these two away." Saying this, the king's sons mounted their Pakshiraj horses and rode off.

Bhutum and Buddhu now knew that they too were the king's sons! Bhutum's mother was not a maid, and Buddhu's mother was not a servant. Then Buddhu said, "Brother, let's go to our father."

Bhutum said, "Let's go."

Inside the palace, the five queens were sitting, resting their heads on golden beds and their feet on silver beds, parting and decorating their hair. A maid came and brought news that a Shukpankhi boat had arrived at the river ghat, with silver oars and a diamond rudder. Inside the boat sat a maiden with hair as dark as rain clouds and skin as fair as a kunch berry, talking to a golden parrot.

Immediately, guards were posted at the river ghat. The queens, stumbling over each other in their haste, rushed to see the fair-skinned maiden in the Shukpankhi boat.

By then, the sail of the Shukpankhi boat was unfurled; the Shukpankhi was gliding swiftly away.

The queens called out—

"Maids with skin so fair, and hair like clouds above,
Take with you, maid, this flower of pearl, my love."

From the boat, the fair-skinned maiden replied,—

"The flower of pearls, the flower of pearls, it lies afar,
Send your son to Kalabati's city, beneath a distant star.
Market wares are sold to the beat of a drum, fruit grows on the tree,
Past the kingdom of three old crones, and across the crimson sea."

As she spoke, the Shukpankhi boat traveled far into the distance. The queens all called out—

"From what land, O princess, from what country do you hail?
My son, a golden moon, will be your groom, without fail."

By then, the Shukpankhi had gone even farther; the fair-skinned maiden answered,—

"Princess Kalabati is my name, with hair as dark as night,
Send your son to Kalabati's land, to prove his royal might.
If he can bring the flower of pearls, and the drum that calls the fair,
I will come to your house as that prince's slave, his life and home to share."

The Shukpankhi was no longer visible. The queens immediately sent word to their sons. The sons came galloping home on their Pakshiraj horses. Hearing everything, the king ordered the Mayurpankhi boats to be prepared. After giving the order, the king went to the royal court to hold assembly.

The king was seated in his grand court. Bhutum and Buddhu arrived there. The doorkeeper asked, "Who are you?"

Buddhu said, "The Monkey Prince."

Bhutum said, "The Owl Prince."

The doorkeeper let them pass.

Then Buddhu, in one leap, went and sat on the king's lap. Bhutum flew and perched on the king's shoulder. The king was startled; everyone in the court cried out, "Hey! Hey!"

Buddhu called out, "Father."

Bhutum called out, "Father!"

The entire court fell silent. Tears streamed down the king's face. The king kissed Bhutum's cheek and gathered Buddhu into his arms.

Immediately, the king dismissed the court and rose, taking Buddhu and Bhutum with him.

Meanwhile, preparations were in full swing. Five Mayurpankhi boats, flying five banners, arrived and moored at the ghat. The princes boarded them. The queens, with auspicious cries, sent the five princes off to the land of Princess Kalabati.

At that moment, the king arrived at the river ghat with Bhutum and Buddhu.

Buddhu asked, "Father, what are those leaving?"

The king replied, "Mayurpankhi boats."

Buddhu said, "Father, we want to go on a Mayurpankhi; give us a Mayurpankhi."

Bhutum said, "Father, give us a Mayurpankhi."

The queens all shrieked—

"Who is that, who is that? Is it the maid's brat?"

"Who is that, who is that? Is it the dung-collector's brat?"

"Oh my, oh my, fie! Fie!"

The queens flicked Bhutum off with a sharp rap on his cheek and slapped Buddhu across his face, sending them tumbling. The king could not say a word; he stood there in silence.

The queens, seething with anger, took the king and returned to the palace.

Buddhu said, "Brother?"

Bhutum said, "Yes, brother?"

Buddhu said, "Let's go to the carpenter's house and have a Mayurpankhi built. We will go where the princes have gone."

Bhutum said, "Let's go."

Day and night, Bhutum's mother and Buddhu's mother passed their time weeping. They too heard that the princes had set off for the land of Princess Kalabati in Mayurpankhi boats. Hearing this, the two of them embraced and cried even more. After weeping their hearts out, the two sisters finally went to the riverbank. Then, they took two small boats made of areca-nut sheaths, placed two cowries, some paddy, and durva grass in them, marked the bow and stern with vermilion dots, and set them afloat.

Buddhu's mother said,—

"Buddhu, my son, my life!
What sin have I done?
For what sin did you leave me, filling my heart with grief?

The Mayurpankhi follows the Shukpankhi's trail,
Had my child been here, you would have sailed in this mother's boat.
Wherever you are in this world, O God, –
For my child's sake, I offer this paddy and grass."

Bhutum's mother said—

"Bhutum, my son, my life!
What sin have I done?
For what sin did you leave me, filling my heart with grief?
The Mayurpankhi follows the Shukpankhi's trail,
Had my child been here, you would have sailed in this mother's boat.
Wherever you are in this world, O God, –
For my child's sake, I offer this paddy and grass."

After setting the areca-nut sheath boats afloat, Bhutum's mother and Buddhu's mother returned to their hut, weeping.

On their way to the carpenter's house, Bhutum and Buddhu saw two small areca-nut sheath boats floating by.

Buddhu said, "Brother, these are our boats! Let's get in."
Bhutum said, "Let's get in."

Then, Buddhu and Bhutum each boarded a boat. The two brothers' two Mayurpankhis floated side by side.

People who saw them said, "Oh my! What is this?"
Buddhu and Bhutum would say, "We are Buddhu and Bhutum."
And on they went.

And the princes? Their Mayurpankhi boats sailed on until they reached the kingdom of the three old crones. At once, the three old crones' three old guards came and seized the boats. They captured the five princes, along with all the boatmen, sailors, and soldiers, stuffed them into sacks, and took them to the three old crones.

The three old crones drank them down with their evening water and fell asleep, snoring loudly!

Late at night, from inside the crones' stomachs, the princes began to talk among themselves.

"Brothers, we are trapped in these crones' stomachs for life. We will never see our mothers again, never see our father again."

Just then, someone came and called softly, "Brother! Brother!"

The princes whispered in reply, "Who is it, brother? We are in the crones' stomachs!"

A voice from outside replied, "Hold my tail." "Hold my feathers." The princes grabbed the tail and the feathers and were pulled out through the crones' nostrils. When they came out, they saw Buddhu and Bhutum!

Buddhu said, "Quiet, quiet! Quickly, cut off the crones' heads with your swords."

The princes did as they were told. The princes, sailors, and boatmen all came out. Once free, they all hurried back to the Mayurpankhis and raised the sails. No one even spoke a word to Buddhu and Bhutum.

The Mayurpankhis sailed all night and at dawn entered the waters of the Red River. The Red River had no banks, no shore, only red water.

The boatmen lost their way; the five Mayurpankhis drifted and ended up in the sea. The princes, sailors, and boatmen all cried out in despair.

For seven days and seven nights, the Mayurpankhis were tossed about in the sea. Finally, the boats were on the verge of breaking apart; all seemed lost!

The princes said, "Alas, brother, if only brother Buddhu were here, he would have saved us now!" "Alas, brother, if only brother Bhutum were here, he would have saved us now!"

"What is it, brothers, what is it!
What do you need, what do you need?"

Saying this, Buddhu and Bhutum tied their areca-nut sheath boats to the prows of the Mayurpankhis and came to the princes. They told the boatmen, "Raise the sails and head north."

In no time, the Mayurpankhis left the sea and entered a river. The river's water was crystal clear and shimmering. On both banks were thousands of mango and jackfruit trees. The princes all ate their fill of mangoes and jackfruits and felt revived.

Then the princes said, "Why are there a monkey and an owl on the Mayurpankhis? Throw these two into the water." The boatmen threw Buddhu and Bhutum into the water; they untied their areca-nut sheath boats and tossed them aside. The Mayurpankhis began to move again on the river.

As they sailed on, they reached a certain spot, and all five Mayurpankhis, with the princes, sailors, and boatmen, suddenly sank without a trace.

Sometime later, Buddhu and Bhutum's boats arrived at that very spot. Buddhu said, "Brother!"

Bhutum said, "What is it?"

Buddhu said, "My heart feels strange, as if something has happened here. Come, let's dive in and see."

Bhutum said, "Let it be! We're better off if they're dead. I'm not diving anywhere."

Buddhu said, "Fie, fie, don't say such things. Well, you stay here. I am tying this thread around my waist. Don't pull it up until I give it a tug."

Bhutum said, "Alright, I can do that."

Then Buddhu dived into the river water; Bhutum sat holding the thread.

Diving deeper and deeper, Buddhu reached the netherworld and saw a huge tunnel. Buddhu descended through the tunnel.

After passing through the tunnel, Buddhu saw a royal palace, as magnificent as the court of Indra!

But in that kingdom, there were no people, no life, only a hundred-year-old crone sitting and sewing a small quilt. As soon as the crone saw Buddhu, she threw the quilt in her hands at him. Instantly, thousands of soldiers appeared, bound Buddhu, and took him inside the palace.

The soldiers took him and locked him in a dark cell. As soon as he was inside, many voices in the cell cried out, "Brother Buddhu, brother Buddhu, come, brother, come!" Buddhu saw that it was the princes and the sailors and boatmen!

Buddhu said, "I see! Very well, then!"

The next day, Buddhu contorted his face and lay still, pretending to be dead. A maid used to bring food for the princes every day. When she came, she saw a dead monkey lying in the cell. On her way out, she threw the dead monkey away. And what then? Buddhu slowly blinked his eyes open. Seeing no one, he got up. As he stood, Buddhu saw the maiden with hair like rain clouds and skin like a kunch berry on the third floor of the magnificent palace, talking to her golden parrot. Buddhu moved from tree branch to tree branch, from rooftop to rooftop, and stood behind the fair-skinned maiden. At that moment, the fair-skinned maiden was saying,—

"O golden bird, my dear parrot, my hopes were all in vain,
The silver oars, the diamond rudder—no one came!"

The princess had a flower of pearls in her hair bun. Buddhu gently lifted the flower.

Then the parrot said,

"Princess with skin so fair, and hair like clouds above,
What happened, maiden, to your flower of pearl, my love?"

The princess touched her hair and saw that the flower was gone.

The parrot said,—

"Princess Kalabati, worry no more,
Lift your head and see, your groom is at your door!"

Kalabati, startled, turned around and saw—a monkey! Kalabati's head bowed in shame. Tossing aside her bracelets and letting loose the braid of her cloud-dark hair, Princess Kalabati fell to the ground.

But what could the princess do? She had made a vow that whoever could pass through the kingdom of the three old crones, cross the waters of the Red River, escape the clutches of the quilt-crone and the dark cell, and come to her palace to take the flower of pearls would be her husband. So what else could the princess do? She rose and placed a garland around the monkey's neck.

The princess said, "From now on, I will do whatever you say. Let us go then; but you cannot take me like this. I will stay inside this small casket; you must carry me in the casket."

Buddhu said, "Alright."

The princess climbed into the casket.

At once, the parrot flew quickly and struck the dhol-dogor drum. In an instant, a vast marketplace appeared within the palace. The princess's casket blended in with the shopkeepers' caskets.

Buddhu saw this and thought it was quite a trick. He took the dhol-dogor and began to play it. Striking the right side of the dhol-dogor made the marketplace appear; striking the left side made it vanish. Buddhu sat with his eyes closed and played. The shopkeepers, raising and lowering their stalls, became utterly exhausted and could not go on. Then they all said, "Stop, stop, take the princess's casket; we don't want to trade anymore."

Buddhu struck the left side of the dhol-dogor, and the market vanished. Only the princess's casket remained.

This time, however, Buddhu did not let go of the drum. With the drum on his shoulder, he went to the casket and called out,—

"Princess, princess, are you asleep?
To claim my bride, the dhol-dogor I keep."

The princess came out of the casket and said, "I am very hungry. Bring me the fruit from the leaves of the trees to eat."
Buddhu said, "Alright."

The princess got back into the casket. Buddhu, with the drum on his shoulder and the casket in his hand, went to fetch the fruit from the leaves of the trees.

When he got there, Buddhu saw that every leaf on the tree bore a different kind of fruit. Seeing this, Buddhu too was tempted! But, oh my! A huge python was hissing at the base of the tree!

Buddhu then slowly circled the tree and made a dash for it. The python got entangled in the thread tied around his waist and was sliced in two. Then Buddhu climbed the tree, plucked the fruit from the leaves, and called the princess.

The princess said, "No more, everything is done... Now let us go to your home!"

Buddhu said, "No, not everything is done; I must take my prince-brothers and the crone's quilt." The princess said, "Take them."

Then, with the five princes, the sailors, the boatmen, the Mayurpankhis, the dhol-dogor on his shoulder, the casket in his hand, the flower of pearls in his ear, and the crone's quilt on his body, Buddhu, while eating the fruit from the leaves of the trees, gave a tug on the thread around his waist.

Bhutum understood that Buddhu was now returning. He pulled on the thread. Buddhu rose up, bringing with him the five princes, the soldiers, the sailors, the boatmen, and the Mayurpankhis.

Once they surfaced, the sailors and boatmen raised the sails with a cry of 'Heave-ho!' Buddhu went and sat on the roof of the Mayurpankhi, and the owl went and sat on the mast.

This time, the Mayurpankhi set off for home with everyone aboard.

On the roof, Buddhu would blink his eyes and occasionally open the casket to speak with someone inside. The helmsman saw this and reported it to the princes.

Hearing the news, they remained silent. At night, when everyone was asleep, including Bhutum and Buddhu, the princes crept up, took the casket, placed the dhol-dogor by Buddhu's head, wrapped him in the crone's quilt, and pushed him into the water. Bhutum was on the mast; they shot him with an arrow. Buddhu and Bhutum fell into the water and floated away.

Then they opened the casket, and the princess with hair like rain clouds and skin like a kunch berry emerged. The princes said, "Princess, whose are you now?"

The princess replied, "I belong to whoever owns the dhol-dogor."

Hearing this, the princes said, "Oh! We understand that! Seize the princess."

What could she do? The princess was locked in a cabin of the Mayurpankhi.

The Mayurpankhi arrived at the ghat, and a great celebration began throughout the kingdom. The king came, the queens came, and everyone in the kingdom came to the riverbank. The princes had returned with the maiden of cloud-dark hair and kunch-berry skin.

The queens welcomed Princess Kalabati with paddy, durva grass, five-wicked lamps, and the blowing of conch shells, and brought her into the palace.

The queens asked, "Princess, whose are you?"
