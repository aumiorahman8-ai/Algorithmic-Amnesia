**The Story of the King, His Seven Queens, and Their Sons**

A childless king ruled a prosperous kingdom with seven queens: Eldest, Second, Third, Fourth, Fifth, Unfavored, and Youngest. Despite his wealth, he was deeply unhappy because none of the queens bore him children.

While bathing at the river, the queens met a hermit who gave the Eldest Queen a magical tree root. He instructed her to grind it and share it among all seven queens to bear sons "beautiful as the golden moon."

Back at the palace, the queens prepared a meal. The Eldest Queen gave the root to the Unfavored Queen to grind with the spices. While grinding, the Unfavored Queen secretly ate a small piece. She returned the paste to the Eldest Queen, who distributed it: Eldest, Second, Third, and Fifth Queens ate most of it. The arriving Fourth Queen found only residue and consumed it. The Youngest Queen, cleaning fish separately, received none.

Devastated, the Youngest Queen fainted. The Fourth Queen found a minuscule residue on the grinding stone, washed it in water, and gave it to the Youngless Queen to drink. The other queens scornfully thought she'd only bear a "monkey-moon" son.

Ten months later:
*   The Eldest, Second, Third, Fifth, and Unfavored Queens each bore a golden-moon-like son (Princes Hira, Manik, Moti, Shankha, Kanchan).
*   The Fourth Queen bore an owl (Bhutum).
*   The Youngest Queen bore a monkey (Buddhu).

The king celebrated the five princes. Humiliated, the Fourth and Youngest Queens were banished: one became a menagerie maid, the other a dung-cake collector. Bhutum and Buddhu grew up playing near their hut, aiding their mothers, while the cruel princes terrorized the kingdom.

One day, the princes captured Bhutum and Buddhu. At the palace, Buddhu called the king "Father." Overwhelmed, the king embraced his unusual sons. However, the five queens scorned them and prevented them from joining the princes' quest for Princess Kalabati (a fair-skinned maiden who arrived in a magical boat).

Princess Kalabati promised to marry whoever retrieved a pearl flower and drum from her land. After the princes departed on Mayurpankhi boats, Bhutum and Buddhu built tiny boats from floating areca-nut sheaths (sent mournfully by their mothers) and followed.

The princes failed:
1.  Captured by three crones, Bhutum and Buddhu rescued them through the crones' nostrils.
2.  Lost in the Red River/Sea, Bhutum and Buddhu guided them to safety. Ungrateful, the princes threw them overboard.
3.  Sank into the underworld, Buddhu rescued them from a dark cell (captured after encountering a powerful quilt-crone).

Buddhu accomplished Kalabati's tasks:
*   Rescued the prisoners.
*   Tricked Kalabati into marrying him after retrieving the pearl flower.
*   Acquired the magical dhol-dogor drum that conjured a marketplace.
*   Obtained fruit from enchanted trees by killing a guardian python.

On the return voyage, the princes drugged Bhutum and Buddhu (wrapped in the quilt-crone's blanket) and threw them overboard, stealing Kalabati and the drum.

Welcomed as heroes with Kalabati, the princes asked who owned her. She replied "whoever owns the dhol-dogor" stalled with a vow.

Meanwhile, Bhutum and Buddhu survived and returned to their despairing mothers. The magical drum materialized a marketplace, enchanted fruit trees, and soldiers near their hut, signaling their power to the king.

The king realized the truth. He restored the Fourth and Youngest Queens. Kalabati identified Buddhu as her husband via the drum.

Buddhu (wedded to Kalabati) and Bhutum (wedded to Princess Hirabati) transformed at night into handsome princes disguised by their skins. Kalabati and Hirabati burned the skins, breaking a hermit's spell and revealing their true forms permanently. They became Princes Budhkumar and Rupkumar.

The five jealous queens and princes locked themselves away; the king sealed their rooms. Budhkumar, Rupkumar, their wives, the Fourth Queen, the Youngest Queen, and the king lived happily ever after.
