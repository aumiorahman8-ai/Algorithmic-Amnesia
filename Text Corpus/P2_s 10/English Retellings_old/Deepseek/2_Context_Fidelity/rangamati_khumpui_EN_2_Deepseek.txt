Here is a faithful retelling of the Chakma folktale "Khumpui" from the Rangamati region of the Chittagong Hill Tracts, preserving cultural nuances, kinship terms, rituals, and the Chakma worldview:

**The Tale of Khumpui**

In the mist-wrapped green hills near Rangamati, within a bustling **Chakma** *Jhum* village under a wise **Raja** (chief), lived an extraordinarily beautiful girl named **Khumpui**. Her beauty was whispered to be a blessing from the mountain spirits (*Buraga*), radiating like the morning sun on dewy leaves. Khumpui lived with her elder brother (**Dada**) and her **Bhabi** (sister-in-law, her brother's wife).

As Khumpui blossomed into womanhood, her **Bhabi** grew intensely jealous. The young men of the village, dazzled by her fairness and grace, neglected the **Bhabi**, focusing their songs and gifts only on Khumpui. Worse, Khumpui's **Dada** doted on his sister excessively in the **Bhabi**'s eyes, often bringing her delicate forest flowers or the sweetest fruits from the Jhum. This favouritism festered like a thorn in the **Bhabi**'s heart.

Consumed by envy, the **Bhabi** began plotting. On a day when Khumpui's **Dada** was away tending the distant Jhum fields, the **Bhabi** approached Khumpui sweetly:

"**Bon** (Sister/Sister-in-law)," she said, masking her malice, "let us go fishing in the deep pool near the **Khagrachari** stream. It is teeming today!"

Eager and unsuspecting, Khumpui agreed. At the secluded, shadowy pool, the **Bhabi** waited until Khumpui was absorbed in watching the fish. Then, with sudden, savage force, she pushed her into the deep water. Khumpui struggled, but the **Bhabi**, driven by hatred, held her down until her movements ceased. The **Bhabi** then sank Khumpui's body deep under rocks at the pool's bottom.

Returning alone, the **Bhabi** feigned sorrow: "Oh **Dada**!" she wailed as the brother returned. "Our beloved **Bon** Khumpui slipped and fell into the deep pool! The current took her! I tried to save her, but she was gone!"

The **Dada** was utterly devastated. His heartache echoed through the hills. He searched desperately for days, but found no trace, only an unnatural stillness over the once-lively pool. The villagers mourned deeply, sensing a darkness beneath the **Bhabi**'s story. Khumpui's spirit, filled with sorrow and injustice, could not find peace. Instead of ascending to the ancestor realm, she underwent a profound transformation. From her physical beauty dissolved in the water arose **Ganga**, a powerful and fierce *Buraga* (spirit), embodying the protective rage of the drowned.

Years passed. Khumpui's **Dada** eventually remarried. His wife (**Bou**, wife) became pregnant. When she was far along, she developed an intense craving (**Hiyang**), the most powerful the villagers had ever seen. She craved fresh *Maitong Gajar* (Hill Trout) – but only those *exactly matching the length of her middle finger*. Instinctively, she knew only the deep pool where Khumpui had vanished held such fish.

Distraught, the **Dada** went to the Raja. "How can I satisfy this **Hiyang**? It threatens her life!" The Raja consulted village elders and spiritual practitioners (*Vaidyas*, medicine men/spiritual healers). Understanding the demand was likely charged with supernatural significance, the Raja declared:

"This **Hiyang** is a message from the spirits. The fish must be caught precisely as demanded. Go to the deep pool, **Dada**. But go prepared with purity and prayers."

Following ritual cleansing and offerings to appease the restless spirits dwelling near water (**Tuni-Buraga**), the **Dada**, accompanied by terrified villagers, went to the accursed pool. Casting nets woven with sacred threads and chanting prayers (*Mantras*), they hauled them in with trembling hands. Miraculously, the net held fish – shimmering Maitong Gajar – each *perfectly* matching the length of the pregnant woman's middle finger.

Back at the village, the womenfolk meticulously cleaned the fish. But horror struck: inside the belly of the largest trout, they found not guts, but glistening strands of *golden hair* – unmistakably Khumpui’s famous hair. The **Dada** collapsed, consumed by renewed grief and the dreadful confirmation of foul play.

The Raja assembled the villagers. "This sign cannot be ignored. There is dark magic (**Jadu**) or betrayal here." He performed rituals seeking truth, invoking blessings from clan deities (**Gosain**) and ancestral spirits. The spiritual forces stirred intensely.

Under the collective gaze heavy with accusation and the unbearable weight of the supernatural evidence, the first **Bhabi** broke. Terrified by the manifestation of Khumpui's spirit as Ganga and the damning golden hair, she confessed her vile deed beside the **Khagrachari** stream.

The Raja pronounced stern justice: the first **Bhabi** faced grave punishment, likely banishment (*Deshantaron*) or harsher penance prescribed by spirits and elders, forever marked by her crime. Khumpui's **Dada's** new wife safely gave birth, the **Hiyang** satisfied. Ganga, witnessing justice done, slowly released her vengeful rage. Her spirit merged peacefully with the landscape. Her lingering sorrow seeped into the hills, the streams, and the very wind.

**Legacy:**
Khumpui did not vanish. Her essence transformed the land. The deep pool retained its unsettling aura, a place villagers approached with offerings and caution, forever linked to the *Buraga* Ganga. The tale became an eternal cautionary tale among the **Chakma** people:

1.  **The Sanctity of Kin:** It underscores the sacred bond between siblings (**Dada-Bon**). Violating this trust invites catastrophic spiritual retribution.
2.  **The Poison of Envy:** Jealousy, especially within the complex affinal kinship (**Bhabi-Bon**), festers and destroys households and souls.
3.  **Power of Hiyang:** The intense craving of a pregnant woman (**Hiyang**) is sacred and potent, often a conduit for ancestral or spirit intervention demanding justice.
4.  **Spirit World Intervenes:** Actions disrupt cosmic balance; the spirit realm (*Buraga*, ancestral spirits) actively engages, punishing wrongdoers and demanding propitiation through rituals and truth.
5.  **Respect for Nature:** Water bodies house potent spirits (**Tuni-Buraga**); betrayal enacted upon nature becomes inseparable from it, transforming victims into protectors or avengers. The mountain near Rangamati where the village stood, touched eternally by Khumpui's sorrow, became known as **Khumpui Pahar** (Khumpui Mountain), a testament to her story woven into the Chakma soul and their landscape. Locals say you can still hear a faint, sorrowful melody, like a bamboo flute (**Muruli**), echoing in the hills on quiet nights, Khumpui's eternal song.
