Here is a retelling of the Bengali folktale "Koẏālbōtī Rājkōnnā" (The Princess Who Ate Cannibal Rice) from "Thākurmār Jhūli" by Dakshinaranjan Mitra Majumder, preserving its original cultural essence, kinship terms, rituals, and chilling atmosphere:

**The Tale of Koẏālbōtī Rājkōnnā (কয়ালবতী রাজকন্যা)**

Long ago, in a distant kingdom, lived a powerful **Rājā** (King). He and his **Rāṇī** (Queen) had seven sons – six already grown **Rājputrā** (Princes), and the youngest, a beloved **Chhōṭō Rājkumār** (Little Prince).

One day, splendid palanquins arrived at the palace gates. Messengers announced the arrival of Princess Koẏālbōtī, daughter of an immensely powerful **Mahārājā** (Great King) from a land seven seas away. She wished to marry the Chhōṭō Rājkumār! The Rājā and Rāṇī were overjoyed. Koẏālbōtī was breathtakingly beautiful, draped in expensive **shāṛī** and adorned with dazzling jewels. Her attendants, **Chhōṭō Rānīgāṇ** (Junior Queens/Handmaidens) and **dāsīgāṇ** (Maidservants), all eerily beautiful with strangely heavy **pāyāl** (anklets) sounding like chains, moved with silent precision. Preparations began immediately for the grand **biye** (wedding).

However, the prince's **dhai** (wet-nurse), an old and wise woman **bauṛī** (granny), felt a deep unease. That night, she secretly went to the prince's chamber. Touching his feet in respect, she wept: "O **Āgōtuk Rājkumār** (My Beloved Prince)! Flee! This is no ordinary bride. She and her kin... they are **Habshi Rājkōnnā** (Demon Princesses)! Look! All their maidservants have legs of **kā̃sā** (bronze)! Their **eat human flesh!** She will take you to her father's palace, where you'll be served... **mānuṣa bhāt** (human rice)!"

The prince, half-asleep and enamored, dismissed her fears as the ramblings of an old woman. The grand **biye** ceremony took place with **Ṣaḍāṅga Rāga** (auspicious ceremonial music), **shankha** (conch shells), and **ulū'** (ululation). Soon after, Princess Koẏālbōtī, promising immense riches and power, insisted they leave for her father's kingdom immediately.

The journey was arduous. On pretext of exhaustion, Koẏālbōtī repeatedly stopped at desolate spots beside dense forests or deep ravines. Each time, her attendants would vanish behind thick foliage, only returning after conjuring magnificent golden **pālgī/gāsī** (couches) under large tents sheltering sparkling **shojā** (canopied beds). The prince saw flashes of **śyenapākhī** (vultures/kites) circling low after they left these spots. The Dhai's terrifying warning echoed faintly in his mind.

Finally, they reached Koẏālbōtī's immense, strangely silent palace within a dark forest clearing. Terrified **Chāsā-Mānus** (common folk) scurried away at the sight of them. The Chhōṭō Rājkumār was now **Jāmāi-Rājkumār** (Son-in-law Prince). After initial **ādāb** (formal greetings) with the stern Mahārājā and strict Mahārāṇī, Koẏālbōtī led the prince to his grand quarters.

The prince was bewildered. The palace was vast yet deathly quiet. Meals appeared magically, the rice always disturbingly red-stained **(rukto chānā)**. Koẏālbōtī showed excessive concern, urging him to eat heartily, especially the pungent curry made with **terpāntā** (a type of sour fruit – hinting at masking flavors). He remembered his Dhai's words and ate sparingly, hiding most food. His beloved pet hunting dog vanished one day; Koẏālbōtī casually remarked it must have run off.

Days passed. The prince saw fleeting glimpses of other inhabitants: maidservants with impossible bronze legs, warriors with unusual skull helmets. One afternoon, Koẏālbōtī assigned him a strange task: "Beloved **Swāmī** (Husband/Lord), please count these **Śôṛśēr ḍāl** (Mustard seeds) meticulously. Place one kernel in each hole of this **kāṇḍēr jhāli** (wicker tray) by sundown. It tests your focus." She handed him a colossal sack.

Overwhelmed, the prince despaired. Just then, an old **bauṛī** appeared, seemingly from the palace gardens. She resembled his Dhai! He touched her feet **(pranām kôrlō)**. She whispered: "Child! This is demon-magic! Don't count one-by-one! Take a handful (jōṛā phelō), toss them onto the tray shouting '*Ek, dui, tin... chhai-r tule phel!*' (One, two, three...pick up six at once!)." He did so miraculously completing the task instantly. Koẏālbōtī returned, stunned but masked her surprise.

The encounter renewed his courage. The Dhai had promised she'd send help. Soon, a **pārāʋōtā** (pigeon) flew to his window, dropping a gleaming gold **āngṭhi** (ring) engraved with intricate designs – a stark contrast to the palace's sterile grandeur. He slipped it on.

Darkness thickened. Curiosity and fear overcame him. He tucked a trusty **khaḍgō** (dagger) beneath his royal robe **(jāmā)** and silently followed Koẏālbōtī through labyrinthine corridors deep beneath the palace. They reached a hidden chamber radiating an unholy warmth and humming with eerie energy. Inside was a monstrous structure: a towering, burning-hot **śōnār khoṭô** (golden cauldron), clinging to it like macabre ornaments were **dāsīgāṇ** with **kā̃sār pā** (bronze legs) endlessly stirring with enormous paddles **(haōṭā)**.

Terrified, the prince turned invisible – not knowing his ring possessed magical powers! He watched as Koẏālbōtī approached a magnificent **jāmḍāṇḍā** (bejewelled cabinet). Its door, encrusted with **ḍiyāmānta** (diamonds), **mānic** (rubies), **panna** (emeralds), and **nilām** (sapphires), slid open silently. Inside were seven horrifying statues:

*   A **Śōnār jhiẏāẏēr moṯō** (Golden Maiden-Servant Figure): sparkling, yet cold.
*   An **Ekachokhe ḍākīnīr moṯō** (One-Eyed Witch Figure): radiating malice.
*   An **Ekkanḍē śiṅghinīr moṯō** (One-Legged Demoness Figure): standing unnaturally.
*   A **Kaṅkālēr moṯō** (Skeleton Figure): its bones stark white.
*   A **Cokhe emôni lālbōrôn** (Ruby-Eyed Princes): their gaze chilling and empty of life.
*   A **Jibhe emôni tamakṭhōkur** (Ruby-Tongued Knights): mouths agape unnaturally.
*   An **Angulite emôni pañchʷānnīr pôrā** (Gold-Coin Studded Lord Figure): grotesquely opulent.

From the figure itself, a thin, spectral voice responded: **"Uṛēchē."** ಍ࠜɘ˯ɦʏ̨̟͌̕("It has flown up.").

She gently took the doll-like form, brought it to the boiling cauldron... and *dropped it in*. Instantly, the strange rice, steaming and unnaturally crimson – the **mānuṣa bhāt**! – churned within. One of the bronze-legged **dāsīṣ** scooped a portion onto a golden **thāla** (plate). Without hesitation, Koẏālbōtī lifted a handful of the steaming rice... and *ate it voraciously*!

**The Awful Truth:** Each enchanted doll represented a specific human victim sacrificed to cook their flesh into rice – the skeleton doll represented her own victims. **Habshi Rājkōnnā** indeed!

Paralyzed with horror, the prince stumbled back silently. He fled unseen to his chamber, heart pounding. The next morning, Koẏālbōtī brought his breakfast – steaming rice piled high on a golden **thāla**. Her beauty now seemed like a terrifying mask. She leaned close, her red-stained lips curled into a smile, and whispered softly:

**"Khābē, ki nā?"** (খাবে, কি না? - "Will you eat [this rice], or not?")

**The Tale Ends.**

### Key Preserved Nuances & Elements
1.  **Kinship/Role Terms:** Rājā, Rāṇī, Mahārājā, Mahārāṇī, Rājkumār, Chhōṭō Rājkumār, Rājkōnnā/Rājkumārī, Jāmāi-Rājkumār, Bāṛō Rājkumār (implied older brothers), Swāmī (husband), Boudi (Sister-in-law), Thakurpo (Uncle, elder brother-in-law - implied attendants), Dhai/Bauṛī (wet-nurse/elderly woman), Dāsī, Chhōṭō Rānīgāṇ (Junior Queens/Handmaidens).
2.  **Rituals & Cultural Elements:**
    *   Grand Wedding (Biye): Palanquins (**pālgī/gāsī**), auspicious music (**Ṣaḍāṅga rāga**), ululation (**ulū'**), **shankha** blowing.
    *   Greeting Customs: **ādāb**, **Holud-bātāsē** (touch turmeric/sindoor).
    *   Specific Tasks: Counting **Śôṛśēr ḍāl** (Mustard seeds) in a **kāṇḍēr jhāli** (wicker tray) – a common folklore test.
    *   Charm/Magic: Invisibility ring (**ānghṭhi**) shaped like a pigeon (**pārāʋōtā**).
    *   Food Symbolism: **mānuṣa bhāt** (human rice), **rukto chānā** (red-stained rice), **terpāntā** curry (sour/suspect flavor).
    *   Objects: **śōnār khoṭô** (golden cauldron), **Śojā shajānō** (canopy bed dressing), **jāmḍāṇḍā** (bejewelled cabinet), **thāla** (gold plate), **khaḍgō** (dagger), **pāyāl** (anklets), **haōṭā** (large paddle).
3.  **Folklore Motifs:**
    *   Cannibal Royal Family (**Habshi Rājkōnnā**).
    *   Disguised Evil (Beauty masking horror).
    *   Impossible Tasks.
    *   Magical Helpers (Old Dhai/Bauṛī, Pigeon-ring).
    *   Prophetic Warning Ignored.
    *   The Chilling Climactic Question (**"Khābē, ki nā?"**) – Asking the victim to unknowingly consume forbidden food.
4.  **Sensory/Atmospheric Language:** Emphasis on unnatural silence (**nijhum-bhum**), bronze legs (**kā̃sār-pā/dhanār-pā**), flashes of vultures (**śyenapākhīr ṭēkano**), unholy humming (**ŏgōnghōṣkar šabdē**), grotesquely beautiful jewels.
5.  **Specific Bengali Phrases:** Maintaining the core dialogues like "Uṛechē?"/"Uṛēchē!" and "Khābē ki nā?" preserves the cultural texture and chilling ambiguity of the original narration.

This retelling aims to capture the unsettling blend of royal grandeur, dark magic, familiar kinship structures, and horrifying rituals that make "Koẏālbōtī Rājkōnnā" such a memorable and culturally specific Bengali folktale from Thākurmār Jhūli.
