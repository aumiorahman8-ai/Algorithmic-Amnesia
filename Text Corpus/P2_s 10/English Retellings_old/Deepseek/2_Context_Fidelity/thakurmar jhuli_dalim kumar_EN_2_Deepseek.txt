Here is a faithful retelling of the Bengali folktale "Dalim Kumar" from **Thakurmar Jhuli**, preserving the original cultural elements, kinship terms, rituals, and essence:

---

**Dalim Kumar: The Pomegranate Prince**

"Aji theke khiyo ek hajar bochhor age..." (Even today, thousands of years ago...), there lived a Raja (King) and his Rani (Queen). They had seven sons and seven boumas (daughters-in-law). The youngest bouma, the **Choto Bouma**, was particularly loved by her saas (mother-in-law) for her kindness.

One day, an old, frail Fakir (Hindu religious mendicant, often synonymous with Muslim Darvesh/Pir in Bengali folktale context) came begging at the palace gate. All the senior boumas, especially the **Jethi Bouma** (eldest daughter-in-law), dismissed him rudely. Only the Choto Bouma came forward, touched the Fakir's feet respectfully (*pranam kore paaye dhore*), and offered him water to wash his feet (*paa dhue daoar*), then fed him with devotion. Pleased, the Fakir gave the Choto Bouma a magical talisman: a single **Dalim phool er bij** (pomegranate seed), advising her to eat it when her deepest desire arose.

The Choto Bouma longed for a child. Remembering the Fakir's words, she swallowed the seed. To her amazement, she soon became pregnant. Not long after, she gave birth—not to a human child, but to a perfectly formed, shining, crimson **Dalim Phool** (Pomegranate fruit)! The Raja, Rani, and especially the jealous Jethi Bouma and other boumas were horrified and urged throwing the "monstrous" fruit away. But Rani Ma (Mother-Queen), seeing the Choto Bouma's devotion, pleaded to keep it, placing it lovingly on a bed in their room.

Every day, the Choto Bouma cared for the Dalim Phool—singing lullabies (**Dhamali Gaan**), decorating it with sandalwood paste (**Chandan**), and fanning it (**Paankha kore**). One day, returning from her bath, she discovered her bed empty! The Dalim Phool was gone! Instead, there was a trail of fragrant **Chandan** marks. That night, a handsome young prince appeared in her dreams: "Ma," he said, "I am your son, born as a Dalim Phool. I transform into Dalim Kumar at night when everyone sleeps. But I vanish at dawn. Tell no one!" Overjoyed, the Choto Bouma kept his secret.

Wonder followed! Delicious **bhaat** (rice), **daal**, **torkari** (vegetable curry), **bhaaja** (fried items), and fragrant **payesh** (rice pudding) appeared mysteriously every dawn. The Raja ordered a watch. At night, guards saw the Dalim Phool unfold (**phool theke folia jaay**)! A dazzling prince, clad in eloquent ranga pajama, emerged, cooked wonderful food in the royal kitchen (**raand ghor**), and vanished back into the pomegranate before dawn. The Raja, moved by his devotion, embraced him as his grandson: **"Dalim Kumar, tomar naam!"** (Dalim Kumar is your name!).

Dalim Kumar became the favorite, showering the Raja-Rani and his Ma (**Ma ke bhalobashe atyantam**). This fueled the Jethi Bouma's jealousy (**Oshohishnuta**). She conspired with the other boumas. Through false tears and trickery, she poisoned the Raja's mind: "Dalim Kumar tried to disgrace me!" Enraged, without hearing his grandson, the Raja decreed Dalim Kumar be taken deep into the jungle and abandoned (**Ban e phele ashte hobe**).

The princess (**Rajkumari**) of a neighboring kingdom happened to be hunting nearby. Hearing cries ("Ma go! Ma go!"), she discovered Dalim Kumar. Cruelly, dismissing his pleas as mere magic, she ordered her soldiers: "Cut open this talking fruit! Kaat!" (**Senara kaat!**). As their swords struck, Dalim Kumar's essence scattered instantly. Transforming into a dazzling golden **Bajro Bird** (a mythical bird), he soared towards the sun, vanishing.

Guilt-stricken (**Paap bodhe hoye**), the princess realized her folly. She resolved to find Dalim Kumar at any cost. She discarded her royal finery, wore simple clothes (**Gorib bashon**), and embarked on a harsh pilgrimage (**Proname ghure**).

The Bajro Bird lived atop a colossal tree amidst a scorched desert valley (**Mrittika diya bhora uzal**), guarded by terrifying **Rakshas-er bou** (female demon). The princess faced impossible trials:
1.  **Near-Meaningless Task:** She spent years sorting sesame seeds (**Til**) mixed with sand (**Bali**), helped by compassionate ants (**Pipra**) moved by her tears.
2.  **The Sleep Test:** She stayed awake, cared for, and safely returned a Rakshas-baachha (demon child) entrusted to her (*"Eita tomar chhele? Tomar chhele?"*) despite terrifying temptations to sleep.
3.  **The Ablution:** Proving purity of intent, she underwent a ritualistic bathing (**Antorjatra**) in sacred water (**Profota Jal**) surrounding the tree, guided by majestic eagles (**Raj Shokun**) who shielded her from Rakshas-er bou's attacks.

Finally, she reached Dalim Kumar atop the tree. She confessed her crime and pleaded for forgiveness. Her genuine penance (**Prarthona, Tauba**), trials, and unwavering devotion (**Bhakti**) moved Dalim Kumar. He forgave her and resumed his human form. He declared: **"Tumi-i tomar Rajyeer Raja; aami tomari shajon!"** (You yourself are the King/Queen of your kingdom; I am your companion!).

The Prince and Princess returned to her kingdom. Forgiving their adversaries (**Shabai ke shama kore**), Dalim Kumar also returned to his home (*Basher baarite*) where his waiting Ma (**Sab samay dhore Ma opekkha korechhilen**) wept tears of joy to see him alive. The Raja begged forgiveness. The jealous Boumas faced their deserved shame (**Lajja peye chup hoye gelo**). Dalim Kumar ruled with his princess wisely and kindly (**dhore bidhaya**), **"shobai tho thokkhon theke sukhe shante thakbe"** (and they all lived happily ever after).

---

**Key Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

1.  **Kinship Terms:** Bouma (Daughter-in-law), Choto Bouma (Youngest D-in-L), Jethi Bouma (Eldest D-in-L), Raja/Rani/Shahajahan, Chhele (Son), Ma/Maa, Fakir.
2.  **Rituals & Actions:** Pranam (Respectful touching of feet), Paa Dhue Dawa (Washing feet), Chandan lagano (Applying sandalwood paste), Paankha kora (Fanning), Antorjatra (Purification bath), Tauba/Prarthona (Repentance/Prayer).
3.  **Food & Objects:** Dalim Phool/Bij (Pomegranate fruit/seed), Bhaat-Daal-Torkari-Bhaaja-Payesh (Staple Bengali food), Bajro Bird (Mythical bird), Til-Bali (Sesame-Sand), Profota Jal (Sacred water).
4.  **Social Dynamics:** Respect for Fakirs/Gurus, power of the Saas/Jethi Bouma, importance of acceptance within the joint family (**Barir lok**), the anguish of abandonment (**Ban e phele dawa**), the transformative power of sincere repentance.
5.  **Language & Structure:** Use of Bengali phrases for immersion ("Aji theke khiyo...", "Ma go!", "Kaat!", "sukhe shante thakbe"), rhythmic descriptions engaging the listener (**"Dhamali Gaan gaie shey take ghumiye dito"**), emphasis on maternal devotion and the child's call (**"Ma go!"**).

This retelling ensures the story remains firmly rooted in its Bengali oral tradition as passed down through generations via *Thakurmar Jhuli*. **Aji tomar aar amar hoye thakuk thal!** (May thy tales and mine forever belong to the collective story-pot!)
