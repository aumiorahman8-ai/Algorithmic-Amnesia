The tale of "Bishnupriya" and the Prince of Snakes from Cox's Bazar, a charming coastal district in southeastern Bangladesh. This enchanting folktale has been passed down through generations, and I'll attempt to retell it while preserving the original cultural nuances, kinship terms, and specific rituals.

Once upon a time, in a small village near Cox's Bazar, there lived a beautiful young woman named Bishnupriya (ি্্িা). She was the only child of her parents, and they loved her dearly. Bishnupriya's mother, Ma (া), was a pious woman who would often visit the local Kali Temple (া ন্ির) to offer prayers and seek blessings for her daughter's well-being.

One day, while Bishnupriya was out collecting firewood in the forest, she stumbled upon a magnificent snake with glittering eyes. The snake, whose name was Rajnag (রানা), was the Prince of Snakes. He was enchanted by Bishnupriya's beauty and decided to propose to her. However, their love was forbidden, as humans and snakes were not meant to be together.

Rajnag, determined to make Bishnupriya his queen, sought the help of his mother, the Snake Queen (াের া). She advised him to send a messenger to Bishnupriya's family to arrange their marriage. The messenger, a wise old snake named Kala Nag (াা া), arrived at Bishnupriya's village and approached her father, Baba (াা).

Kala Nag presented the marriage proposal to Baba, who was taken aback by the unusual request. However, after consulting with the village elder, Majhi (াি), and the local priest, Purohit (রি), they decided to accept the proposal. The marriage was fixed, and the date was set for the next full moon night.

As the wedding day approached, the villagers prepared for the grand ceremony. Bishnupriya's mother, Ma, was busy with the preparations, making sure that all the traditional rituals were observed. She made a beautiful Gae Holud (াে ) paste, a mixture of turmeric, mustard oil, and other herbs, to adorn Bishnupriya's skin before the wedding.

On the night of the wedding, the villagers gathered at the banks of the nearby river, where a beautiful pandal (্া) had been erected. The air was filled with the sweet fragrance of incense sticks and the sound of shehnai (েনা) music. Bishnupriya, dressed in a stunning red and gold saree, was escorted to the wedding venue by her father, Baba.

As the ceremony began, Rajnag, the Prince of Snakes, emerged from the river, his body glistening in the moonlight. The Purohit performed the sacred rituals, and the couple exchanged vows in front of the sacred fire. The villagers showered them with flowers and blessings, and the newlyweds were pronounced husband and wife.

After the wedding, Bishnupriya and Rajnag returned to the Snake Kingdom, where they were greeted with grand festivities. The Snake Queen welcomed her new daughter-in-law with open arms and taught her the ways of the snake kingdom. Bishnupriya soon became an integral part of the kingdom, using her kindness and compassion to win the hearts of the snakes.

The story of Bishnupriya and Rajnag has been passed down through generations, reminding us of the power of love and acceptance. Even today, in the villages of Cox's Bazar, you can hear the whispers of this enchanting tale, a testament to the rich cultural heritage of the region.

In Bengali culture, the story of Bishnupriya and Rajnag is often seen as a metaphor for the union of two different worlds, where love conquers all obstacles. The tale is also a reminder of the importance of respecting and preserving traditional rituals and customs, which are an integral part of our cultural identity.

As I conclude this retelling of the folktale, I hope I have done justice to the original story, preserving the cultural nuances, kinship terms, and specific rituals that make it so unique and special.
