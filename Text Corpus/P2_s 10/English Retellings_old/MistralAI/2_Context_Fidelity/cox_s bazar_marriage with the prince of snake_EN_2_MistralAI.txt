### **The Marriage with the Prince of Snakes**
*(A Folktale from Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh)*

In a small village near the dense forests of Cox’s Bazar, there lived a poor but kind-hearted fisherman named **Kader Mia** and his wife **Ayesha Bibi**. They had seven daughters, the youngest and most beautiful being **Champa**. Though they struggled to make ends meet, Champa was beloved by all for her gentle nature and golden voice.

One day, while Champa was washing clothes by the river, a **golden snake** emerged from the water. It was no ordinary snake—its scales shimmered like the sun, and its eyes held a strange wisdom. The snake spoke in a voice as smooth as honey:

*"Champa, daughter of Kader Mia, I am the Prince of Snakes, ruler of the underwater kingdom. I have watched you from afar and wish to marry you. If you accept, your family will never know poverty again."*

Champa, though frightened, remembered her mother’s teachings: *"Never refuse a blessing, even if it comes in an unusual form."* She bowed respectfully and said, *"I must ask my parents first."*

The snake nodded and vanished into the river.

When Champa returned home, she told her parents of the strange encounter. Her father, though shocked, consulted the village **pir (saint)**, who declared: *"This is no ordinary snake—it is a divine being. To refuse would bring misfortune."*

### **The Wedding Rituals**
The Prince of Snakes sent a **barjatri (groom’s procession)** of golden serpents carrying jewels, silk, and a **dowry** of rare pearls. The villagers whispered in awe as the snakes coiled around the house, their hisses forming a melodious tune.

The wedding was conducted with both **Muslim and folk traditions**:
1. **Gaye Holud (Turmeric Ceremony)** – Champa was anointed with turmeric paste by her **nani (maternal grandmother)**, while the snake prince’s representatives (a pair of golden cobras) brought **paan (betel leaves)** and **sindoor (vermilion)**.
2. **Biye (Wedding Vows)** – Since the groom could not appear in human form, a **symbolic marriage** was performed. A **golden ring** was placed on Champa’s finger by the snake’s tail, and the **Kazi (Muslim marriage registrar)** recited the **Nikah** under the watch of the village elders.
3. **Bidaay (Farewell)** – As Champa left with the snake prince, her sisters wept, and her mother placed a **taabij (protective amulet)** around her neck, whispering, *"May Allah protect you in the world of spirits."*

### **Life in the Underwater Kingdom**
The snake prince led Champa to a hidden **bil (wetland)** where the water parted, revealing a palace of coral and pearls. There, he transformed into a handsome prince, explaining: *"I was cursed by a sorcerer, but your kindness has broken the spell. Now, I can take human form when with you."*

Champa lived in luxury, served by **jinns and water spirits**, but she missed her family. The prince allowed her to visit them once a year, but warned: *"Never reveal my true form to anyone, or the curse will return."*

### **The Betrayal and the Curse**
Years passed, and Champa’s eldest sister, **Jamuna**, grew jealous of her wealth. One day, she followed Champa to the bil and spied on her. When she saw the prince in human form, she screamed in terror, breaking the curse.

The prince’s body twisted back into a snake, and the palace crumbled into the river. Heartbroken, he hissed: *"You have doomed us both. Now, I must return to the depths forever."*

Champa begged for forgiveness, but the prince could only say: *"Our love was not meant for the human world."* With a final glance, he slithered into the water, never to return.

### **The Aftermath**
Champa returned home, but her family’s wealth vanished. The villagers said she had been **bewitched by a snake demon**, and she lived the rest of her days in sorrow, singing by the river where she had first met her love.

To this day, in Cox’s Bazar, fishermen avoid certain parts of the river, saying: *"The Prince of Snakes still waits for his bride."*

### **Cultural Nuances Preserved:**
1. **Kinship Terms** – Use of *nani* (maternal grandmother), *bibi* (respectful term for mother), and *mia* (common suffix for men’s names in rural Bangladesh).
2. **Rituals** – *Gaye Holud*, *Nikah*, *Bidaay*, and the use of *taabij* (amulet) reflect Bengali Muslim wedding customs.
3. **Local Beliefs** – The tale blends Islamic and animistic elements (snake spirits, jinns, curses).
4. **Regional Setting** – The *bil* (wetland) and riverine culture of Cox’s Bazar are central to the story.

This folktale serves as a cautionary tale about **trust, jealousy, and the consequences of breaking sacred promises**—a common theme in Bengali oral traditions.
