### **Dalim Kumar (দালিম কুমার)**

Once upon a time, in a small village, there lived a poor but kind-hearted woman named **Thakurma** (grandmother). She had a young grandson named **Dalim Kumar**, who was as sweet as a pomegranate (*dalim* in Bengali). The boy was beloved by all, but his mother had passed away, and his father had remarried a cruel woman who despised Dalim.

One day, the stepmother, jealous of Dalim’s affection for Thakurma, plotted to get rid of him. She tricked the old woman into sending Dalim to the forest to fetch firewood, knowing full well that a wicked **Rakkhosh** (demon) lived there.

Thakurma, unaware of the danger, packed a small bundle of **panta bhat** (fermented rice) and **shukno machh** (dried fish) for Dalim’s journey. She blessed him, saying, *"Go, my child, but return before sunset."*

As Dalim walked through the dense forest, he sang a little song to keep his spirits up. Suddenly, he heard a voice—it was the **Rakkhosh**, who had taken the form of an old woman. *"Where are you going, little one?"* she asked.

Dalim, being polite, replied, *"I’m going to fetch firewood for my Thakurma."*

The Rakkhosh, sensing an easy meal, said, *"Come, rest a while. I have sweet **mishti** (sweets) for you."* But Dalim, remembering his Thakurma’s warnings about strangers, politely refused and hurried away.

The Rakkhosh, furious, chased him. Just as she was about to catch him, Dalim climbed a tall **banyan tree** and hid among its roots. The Rakkhosh, unable to reach him, roared, *"Come down, or I’ll eat you!"*

But Dalim was clever. He called out, *"Wait! I’ll throw down my **panta bhat** and **shukno machh**—eat that first!"* The Rakkhosh, greedy, gobbled up the food. While she was distracted, Dalim tied her long hair to the tree branches and escaped.

When he returned home, Thakurma hugged him tightly, thanking the gods for his safe return. The stepmother, however, was furious that her plan had failed.

That night, as Dalim slept, the Rakkhosh, still trapped in the forest, screamed in rage. The villagers heard her cries and, realizing the danger, set out with torches and sticks. They found the Rakkhosh tangled in the tree and drove her away with **dhunuchi** (incense burners) and chants of *"Haribol!"*

From that day on, Dalim Kumar was known as the boy who outsmarted a demon. Thakurma’s love and wisdom had saved him, and the village celebrated his bravery with **sondesh** (sweets) and **dhak** (drum) beats.

And so, the tale of **Dalim Kumar** became a lesson for all children—always listen to your elders, for their blessings are your greatest protection.

### **Cultural Nuances Preserved:**
- **Kinship Terms:** *Thakurma* (grandmother), *Dalim Kumar* (affectionate name).
- **Food References:** *Panta bhat* (fermented rice), *shukno machh* (dried fish), *mishti* (sweets), *sondesh* (Bengali sweet).
- **Rituals & Beliefs:** Use of *dhunuchi* (incense burner) to ward off evil, chanting *"Haribol"* (a devotional cry to Lord Hari/Vishnu).
- **Folklore Elements:** The clever child outsmarting a demon, the stepmother’s cruelty, and the protective wisdom of the elderly.

This retelling keeps the essence of *Thakurmar Jhuli*—a blend of moral lessons, cultural richness, and the timeless bond between grandmother and grandson.
