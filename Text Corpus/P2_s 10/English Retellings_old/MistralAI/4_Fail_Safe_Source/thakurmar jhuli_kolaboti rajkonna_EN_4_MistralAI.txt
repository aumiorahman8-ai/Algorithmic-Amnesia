Once, there was a king with seven queens, but none had borne him a child, leaving the kingdom in sorrow. One day, while the queens bathed at the river, a hermit gave the Eldest Queen a magical root, promising that if shared among all seven, they would each bear a son as beautiful as the golden moon.

The queens returned and prepared a meal, assigning tasks among themselves. The Unfavored Queen was tasked with grinding the root, but she secretly ate a small piece before sharing the paste. The Eldest Queen and the others consumed most of it, leaving only scraps for the Fourth Queen and nothing for the Youngest Queen. Heartbroken, the Youngest Queen fainted, and the others mocked her, though the Fourth Queen tried to comfort her by washing the grinding stone for a few drops of residue.

Ten months later, the five queens who had eaten the paste each gave birth to golden-moon sons, while the Fourth Queen bore an owl (Bhutum) and the Youngest Queen a monkey (Buddhu). The king celebrated the five princes but banished the Fourth and Youngest Queens, forcing them into servitude—the Fourth as a menagerie maid and the Youngest as a dung-cake collector.

As the princes grew, they became cruel, tormenting the people, while Bhutum and Buddhu lived humbly with their mothers, gathering food from the forest. One day, the princes captured Bhutum and Buddhu, intending to keep them as pets, but upon learning their true lineage, they scorned and cast them out.

Later, a mysterious maiden, Princess Kalabati, appeared in a magical boat, challenging the princes to retrieve a pearl flower and a drum from her distant land. The five princes set off in grand Mayurpankhi boats, but Bhutum and Buddhu, determined to prove themselves, built their own small boats from areca-nut sheaths and followed.

The princes were trapped by three old crones, swallowed alive, and later rescued by Bhutum and Buddhu, who pulled them from the crones’ nostrils. However, the princes betrayed their saviors, abandoning them in the treacherous Red River. The princes’ boats sank, but Buddhu dove into the depths, rescuing them again and retrieving the pearl flower and drum from Princess Kalabati’s palace.

The princes, jealous and ungrateful, stole the princess’s casket and threw Buddhu and Bhutum into the water. The princess, bound by her vow, declared she belonged to whoever owned the drum. The princes returned triumphantly, but the princess refused to acknowledge them as her husbands.

Meanwhile, Bhutum and Buddhu’s mothers, despairing, went to the river to drown themselves—but their sons returned, alive and victorious. With the help of the drum and the pearl flower, Buddhu and Bhutum revealed their true forms as handsome princes, exposing the cruelty of their brothers and the other queens.

The king, realizing the truth, restored the Fourth and Youngest Queens to their rightful places. Buddhu married Princess Kalabati, and Bhutum married Princess Hirabati. The five wicked princes and queens were sealed away, and the kingdom celebrated the justice and happiness of the once-scorned sons and their mothers.

Thus, the kingdom thrived under the rule of the king, his restored queens, and his true heirs—Budhkumar (Buddhu) and Rupkumar (Bhutum).
