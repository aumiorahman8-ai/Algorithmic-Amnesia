### **Retelling of the Story of Khumpuiti and Khumbarti**

In a large village called Naran, where even crows and kites struggled to fly over, lived a busy priest named Darbasing Ochai. He had two daughters—Khumpuiti, the elder, and Khumbarti, the younger. While Khumbarti was strikingly beautiful, Khumpuiti was plain. Their father was always occupied with rituals, leaving the sisters to work in the Jhum fields alone.

One day, exhausted from labor, Khumpuiti sat under a tree, lamenting their hardships. She wished for help, even from a forest creature, and vowed to accept it as her husband. Suddenly, a radiant, multi-colored snake appeared, transformed into a man, and offered assistance—on the condition that she never reveal his true form and feed him rice daily. Khumpuiti agreed, and the snake helped them build a Jhum hut, clear weeds, and protect their crops.

However, Darbasing noticed Khumpuiti growing weaker from sharing her food with the snake. Pressing Khumbarti for answers, he learned the truth. Despite promising not to harm the snake, he tricked it into coming to their home and killed it. Khumbarti, heartbroken, placed the snake’s severed pieces in places her sister frequented.

When Khumpuiti returned, she found the pieces, strung them into a garland, and wore it, calling out to her snake-husband, Dungui. The river’s waves swallowed her, but before disappearing, she advised Khumbarti to sit on a banyan tree at a crossroads, where the king’s hunters would find her.

Following her sister’s instructions, Khumbarti was discovered by the king, who, enchanted by her beauty, married her. However, the king’s other six queens, jealous of Khumbarti, conspired against her. When she gave birth to seven children (six sons and a daughter), they replaced them with puppies and kittens, banishing her to herd ducks.

Years later, the children—raised by Khumpuiti in the river—appeared under the banyan tree. The king, hearing of their existence, tested the women of the kingdom to find their mother. Only Khumbarti could squeeze her breast and send seven streams of milk to her children. Reunited, the king punished the wicked queens, restored Khumbarti as queen, and lived happily with his true family.

Thus, the story ends with justice prevailing, the evil queens exiled, and Khumbarti and her children reunited with the king in prosperity.
