Here’s a retelling of the story based strictly on the source text:

---

A jackal, proud of his father’s legacy, decided to open a grand school in the Shanti forest. He attracted many students—grasshoppers, tortoises, millipedes, earthworms, scorpions, beetles, cockroaches, frogs, crabs, and spiders. The forest was always noisy with their lessons.

A crocodile, seeing this, worried his seven sons would remain uneducated. He enrolled them in the jackal’s school, where the jackal promised they’d become scholars and master archers in seven days. The crocodile left happily, but the jackal secretly ate one of his sons each day as a snack.

On the sixth day, the crocodile visited to check on his sons. The jackal showed him the last remaining son, claiming they needed one more day to perfect their skills. The crocodile agreed and left.

The next day, the jackal ate the last son, closed the school, and fled. When the crocodile returned, he found the school empty and realized the jackal had tricked him. Furious, he vowed revenge, hiding in the canal where the jackal hunted for crabs.

Hunger eventually drove the jackal to the canal. The crocodile ambushed him, but the jackal tricked him into biting a reed instead of his leg and escaped.

Later, the crocodile faked his death to lure the jackal. Suspicious, the jackal tested him by saying a dead creature’s ears twitch and tail slaps. The crocodile, fooled, moved—and cowherds attacked him, thinking he was the calf-eating crocodile. The jackal escaped again.

Hungry, the jackal ate eggplants but got a thorn stuck in his nose. He went to a barber, who accidentally cut off his nose while removing the thorn. The jackal took the barber’s nail-clipper as compensation.

Passing a potter’s house, the jackal showed off the nail-clipper, which broke. The potter gave him a pot to replace it. Then, at a wedding, a firecracker shattered the pot. The groom gave him a bride to avoid trouble.

The jackal left the bride with a drummer’s family while he fetched a priest. But the bride was accidentally chopped in half by the drummer’s wife, who hid the pieces. When the jackal returned, he took the drummer’s *dhol* (drum) instead.

Climbing a palm tree, the jackal played the *dhol* and sang about his misfortunes—losing his nose, the nail-clipper, the pot, the bride, and ending up with the drum. In his excitement, he slipped and fell.

---

The story ends abruptly here, leaving the jackal’s fate unresolved, much like the original text. The tale follows his cunning, misfortunes, and escalating demands after each loss.
