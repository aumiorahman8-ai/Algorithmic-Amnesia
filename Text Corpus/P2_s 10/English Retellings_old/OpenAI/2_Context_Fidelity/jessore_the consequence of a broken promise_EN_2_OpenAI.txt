**The Consequence of a Broken Promise – A Jessore Folktale**

*Setting the Scene*  
The story is set in the lush, river‑lined countryside of Jessore, a district in the southwestern part of Bengal. The fields of mustard and paddy sway under a sky that shifts from the soft pink of *shondhya* (evening) to the deep indigo of *raatri* (night). Villagers gather at the *pukur* (pond) for the daily *paal* (ritual bath) and for the occasional *bhandara* (community feast). In this world of rice‑stalks, mango trees, and the ever‑present hum of the *banshi* (flute), the values of *shrot* (honor) and *bhratṛtva* (brotherhood) are the invisible threads that hold the community together.

---

### 1. The Characters

| Bengali Term | English Approximation | Role in the Tale |
|--------------|----------------------|------------------|
| **Baba** (বাবা) | Father | The head of the household, a respected *kul* (family) elder. |
| **Maa** (মা) | Mother | Keeper of the hearth, the one who prepares *pitha* and *payesh*. |
| **Dada** (দাদা) | Elder brother | The village’s *panchayat* (council) member, known for his fairness. |
| **Didi** (দিদি) | Elder sister | The gentle mediator between the men and women of the house. |
| **Kaka** (কাকা) | Uncle (father’s brother) | A traveling merchant who brings news from the market towns. |
| **Pishi** (পিসি) | Aunt (mother’s sister) | The storyteller, keeper of oral tradition. |
| **Boro Babu** (বড় বাবু) | The wealthy landlord | Holds the *taluk* (estate) and is the patron of the village’s *Durga Puja*. |
| **Mohan** (মোহন) | A young, ambitious farmer | The protagonist whose promise is tested. |
| **Rani** (রানি) | Mohan’s betrothed | A diligent girl from the neighboring *gram* (village). |

---

### 2. The Promise

During the auspicious *Makar Sankranti* festival, when the sun begins its northward journey, the whole village gathers at the *ghat* (riverbank) to offer *prasad* (blessed food) to the river goddess *Matsya Devi*. The air is scented with *pitha* made of *kheer* (sweetened milk) and *til* (sesame), and the rhythmic beats of the *dhak* (drum) echo across the fields.

Mohan, a diligent farmer, is about to be married to Rani, the daughter of his neighbor *Boro Babu*. As part of the marriage negotiations, *Boro Babu* asks Mohan to bring a **golden *til* (sesame) pendant**—a family heirloom that has been in his own lineage for generations—to be presented as *dowry* (though modest, it is a symbol of goodwill). The pendant is kept in a small *shankha* (conch shell) box, wrapped in a *khadi* (cotton) cloth.

Mohan, eager to prove his worth, **promises** his *pishi* and the whole village that he will retrieve the pendant from the *Bengal* (the distant market town of Jessore) within a fortnight. He swears on the *Shiva Linga* in the village shrine, saying:

> “*Ami shotti kotha bolchi, ei shoptaho’r moddhe ami shei shob shobuj shobdo niye asbo.*”  
> *(I speak the truth; within this week I will bring back that green‑glimmering treasure.)*

The villagers, especially *Dada* and *Didi*, bless him with *til* and *jaggery* sweets, believing his promise will bring prosperity to the whole *paribar* (family).

---

### 3. The Journey and the Temptation

Mohan sets out on a *bicycle* (the new *palan* that his father bought) and rides through the *paddy fields* and *bamboo groves* toward Jessore town. On the way, he meets **Kaka**, who is returning from a trade trip. Kaka tells him about a *fair* (mela) happening at the *Bazar* where a *shakha* (merchant) is selling a **silver *kora* (bracelet)** that is said to bring *shubho* (good luck) to its wearer.

Mohan, already burdened by the weight of his promise, feels the pull of *lobh* (greed). He thinks: *If I bring back a silver bracelet, perhaps Boro Babu will be even more pleased, and my own family will be richer.* He decides to **break his original promise** and buys the silver bracelet instead, promising himself that the *til* pendant can be fetched later.

He returns to the village **two weeks later**, not with the golden *til* pendant, but with the glittering silver bracelet. He presents it to *Boro Babu* with a grand *pranam* (bow). The landlord, though impressed by the fine work, feels a subtle unease—*the promise was not kept as spoken*.

---

### 4. The Consequence Unfolds

That night, the village experiences a **storm** (*badh*). The *pukur* overflows, the *kath* (thatched roofs) of many houses are torn, and the *paddy* fields are flooded. The *panchayat* convenes under the *banyan tree* (*bargad*) to discuss the calamities.

*Dada* recalls Mohan’s broken promise and says:

> “*Jodi amra shotti kotha bolte na pari, tahole shob kichu bhanga hoye jay. Ekhane amader shobai ekta sharthe ache—*shrot* o *bhratṛtva*.*”  
> *(If we cannot keep our word, everything shatters. Here we all stand on the pillars of honor and brotherhood.)*

The *panchayat* decides that the storm is a **karmic repercussion** (*phala*) of the broken promise. According to the local belief, a promise made before the *Shiva Linga* is a *sacred vow* (*pratigya*). Breaking it invites *bhoot* (spiritual imbalance) that manifests as natural disorder.

Mohan, feeling the weight of guilt, goes to the *shrine* at dawn. He performs a ***prayaschitta* (atonement)** ritual:

1. **Bathing in the river** (*snan*) at *Matsya Devi* while chanting *mantras*.
2. **Offering a *pinda* (rice ball)** made of *khichuri* (rice and lentils) and *payesh* (sweet rice pudding) to the deity.
3. **Reciting the *Shiva Tandava Stotram*** while holding the *til* pendant he had left behind in his home.
4. **Fasting** (*upavasa*) for three days, breaking the fast only with *kheer* and *pitha* prepared by his mother.

During the third day of his fast, a **traveler** arrives at the village. He is a *sadhuk* (wandering monk) who carries a small *casket* containing a **golden *til* pendant**—the very one Mohan had promised to bring. The monk explains that he had found the pendant abandoned at the *Bengal* market after a thief stole it, and he had been looking for its rightful owner.

Mohan, humbled, accepts the pendant, thanks the monk, and **places it on the *Shiva Linga* as an offering**. He then returns the pendant to *Boro Babu* with a sincere *pranam* and a heartfelt apology:

> “*Ami bhul korechhi, ami shotti kotha bhul korechi. Ei shesh prarthona, ami shudhu tomader kache bhulti chesta korchi.*”  
> *(I have erred, I have broken my true word. This is my final prayer; I only seek forgiveness from you.)*

*Boro Babu*, moved by Mohan’s genuine remorse and the *prayaschitta* he performed, accepts the pendant and blesses the marriage. The storm subsides, the *pukur* recedes, and the fields begin to dry.

---

### 5. The Moral – A Lesson for the Village

At the *Durga Puja* that follows, the whole *gram* gathers under the *pandul* (temporary shrine) to worship *Maa Durga*. *Pishi* tells the children the story again, emphasizing the cultural values:

> “*Shrot o pratigya—ei duita amader jibon er mul mantra. Jodi amra kotha bhul kori, prithibir shob shobuj o shob shobdo bhanga hoy.*”  
> *(Honor and promise—these two are the core mantras of our lives. If we break our word, the world’s green and its sounds shatter.)*

The children repeat the phrase **“Shrot rakh, pratigya dhoro”** (keep honor, hold onto promises) as they dance to the *dhak* and *shankh* (conch) beats.

## Key Cultural Nuances Preserved

| Element | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Kinship terms** (*baba, maa, dada, didi, kaka, pishi*) | Used to show the relational web that defines social responsibilities. |
| **Rituals** (*prayaschitta* at the *Shiva Linga*, *Makar Sankranti* offerings, *Durga Puja* celebrations) | Highlight the spiritual framework that interprets moral outcomes. |
| **Village council (*panchayat*)** | Demonstrates communal decision‑making and the collective belief in *karma*. |
| **Food items** (*pitha, payesh, til, jaggery, khichuri*) | Symbolize hospitality, gratitude, and the sharing of blessings. |
| **Festivals** (*Makar Sankranti, Durga Puja*) | Anchor the story in the seasonal calendar that structures rural life. |
| **Natural elements** (*river, flood, storm*) | Serve as metaphors for the moral balance of the community. |
| **Language** | Bengali phrases are retained in their original form, with transliteration and translation, to keep the oral‑tradition flavor. |

---

### Closing Thought

In Jessore, as in many parts of Bengal, a promise spoken before the divine is not merely a personal commitment—it is a *sacred contract* that binds the individual to the community and to the cosmos. The tale of **Mohan’s broken promise** reminds us that *honor* (*shrot*) and *repentance* (*prayaschitta*) are the twin pillars that keep the world in harmony. When those pillars falter, the very earth seems to protest, but sincere atonement can restore balance, allowing love, marriage, and the rhythm of village life to continue its timeless dance.
