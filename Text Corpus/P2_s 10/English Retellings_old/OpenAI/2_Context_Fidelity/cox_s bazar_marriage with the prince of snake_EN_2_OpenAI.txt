**The Tale of the Princess and the Prince‑of‑Snakes (Nāga‑Rāja) – A Cox’s Bazar Folktale**

*Note: The story is told in the oral style of the coastal villages of Cox’s Bazar. Bengali kinship terms, ritual names and local colour are kept in the original language wherever they give the tale its cultural texture.*

---

### 1. The Setting

In a small **bari** (thatched house) on the fringe of the **Sundarban** fringe, lived **Mona**, a bright‑eyed girl of sixteen. She was the only child of **Baba** Rashid and **Ma** Sultana. The family was modest; Rashid earned his living as a **mouka‑bazar** (seasonal fish‑seller) while Sultana wove **tant** (hand‑loom sarees) for the village market.

Mona’s **nana** (maternal grandfather) often told her stories of the **Naga‑Rāja**—the Prince of Snakes—who dwelt in the deep mangrove water‑ways. According to the old men, the Naga‑Rāja could take human form on the night of **Pôtrôb** (the full moon) and would sometimes marry a mortal girl, bringing her wealth and protection.

---

### 2. The Omen

One **Bôshonto** (autumn) night, as the **shôni** (cricket chorus) rose, a **shôkto** (large) **cobras** slithered into the courtyard, coiling around the **pata‑chhata** (bamboo mat) where Mona was sleeping. Instead of striking, the snake lifted its hood and, in a voice that sounded like the rustle of mangrove leaves, said:

> “*Mona, daughter of Rashid, I am the Naga‑Rāja, son of the river‑god. I have come to ask for your hand.*”

Mona, though frightened, remembered her **nana’s** warning: “Never turn away a Naga’s request; the river will remember your kindness.” She whispered a **prayer** to **Maa Durga**, then answered, “If you are truly the Naga‑Rāja, I will listen.”

The snake shed its skin, revealing a handsome youth with **kohl‑lined eyes** and a **silver‑threaded dhoti**. He bowed and said, “*Tomorrow, at the **Bôri‑Bôri** (the first light of dawn), you must come to the **Bhairab‑ghat** (the riverbank) with a **pankha** (hand‑fan) and a **mishri‑pati** (sugar‑cane). There I will await you.*”

Mona obeyed, and the next morning she set out, clutching the fan and the sugar‑cane, her heart beating like the **dhol** (drum) of a wedding procession.

---

### 3. The River‑Bank Meeting

At **Bhairab‑ghat**, the water glimmered under the rising sun. The Naga‑Rāja stood on a **bamboo raft**, his hair tied with **jui‑phool** (jasmine). He greeted Mona with the traditional **“Nomoskar”** (bow) and offered her a **til‑pati** (sesame seed) as a token of respect.

He explained the terms of the marriage:

1. **Shubho Drishti** – the first eye‑contact between bride and groom must be at the **shukla‑paksha** (bright half) of the moon, when the river is calm.
2. **Mangal‑Sutra** – instead of a gold necklace, the bride will wear a **kadam‑bali** (a garland of mangrove bark) that the Naga‑Rāja will place around her neck.
3. **Biday** – the bride’s family will give a **pitha** (rice cake) and **payesh** (sweet rice pudding) to the river as an offering, asking the water spirits for a safe union.

Mona agreed, and the Naga‑Rāja gave her a **bengali **“**shankha**” (conch) to blow at the **shubho** (auspicious) moment, promising that its sound would keep the **bhoot** (evil spirits) at bay.

---

### 4. The Preparations at Home

Back at the **bari**, the whole village gathered for the **“**Biyer Gaan**”** (wedding songs). **Didi** Rashida (Mona’s elder sister‑in‑law) sang **“**Bhorer Alo**”**, while **Pishi** Mina (Mona’s aunt) prepared **“**Misti**”** (sweetmeats). The **purob** (elderly women) washed Mona’s hair with **shikakai** (soapnut) and **tulsi** (holy basil) water, chanting **“**Mona, shubho shondhya, shubho biday**”**.

The **shubho drishti** was arranged for the **full moon night** (Pôtrôb). The **bride’s side** set up a **“**piri**”** (a small altar) with **shankha**, **kalash** (pot filled with water), **kumkum** (vermilion), and **sindoor** (vermillion). The **groom’s side**—the Naga‑Rāja’s attendants, who appeared as **bengali fishermen** with **bamboo spears**—brought a **“**pata‑chhata**”** (woven mat) and a **“**bora‑bora**”** (large earthen pot) filled with **panta‑bhat** (fermented rice) for the **“**bhoj**”** (feast).

---

### 5. The Wedding Night (Pôtrôb)

When the **full moon** rose, its silver light reflected on the **river’s surface** like a **mirror of the heavens**. The Naga‑Rāja, now in his **human form**, approached the **piri**. Mona, dressed in a **white **“**tant**”** sari with a **red **“**bora**”** (border), stepped forward. The **shankha** was blown three times, each note echoing across the mangroves.

The **Mangal‑Sutra**—the **kadam‑bali**—was placed around Mona’s neck by the Naga‑Rāja. As the garland touched her skin, a gentle **humming** rose from the water, and the **river** seemed to smile. The **purob** recited the **“**Brahma‑samhita**”**, invoking **Maa Ganga** (the river goddess) to bless the union.

The **Biyer Bhat** (wedding feast) began: **panta‑bhat**, **shorshe ilish** (hilsa fish in mustard sauce), **dal**, **bhorta** (mashed vegetables), and **mishti doi** (sweet yogurt). The **elders**—**chacha** Maqsood, **chhoto‑baba** Abdul—raised their glasses of **borhani** (spiced yogurt drink) and shouted **“**Bhalo thako, shanti thako!**”** (Live well, live in peace).

---

### 6. The Return to the River

After the **midnight** **“**Biday**”**, the Naga‑Rāja led Mona to the **riverbank**. He whispered the ancient **“**Naga‑Mantra**”**:

> “*Jal‑jal‑jal, shanti‑shanti‑shanti,  
>  Naga‑Raja‑ra‑shmi, tumi‑amra‑sathi.*”

He then **transformed**—his skin turned to shimmering scales, his eyes glowed like **bioluminescent algae**. Yet his **human heart** beat within his chest. He took Mona’s hand, and together they **dove** into the **river**, disappearing beneath the **moon‑lit water**.

The villagers watched in awe. **Pishi** Mina wept, but **nana** Hafiz smiled, saying, “*The river has taken our daughter, but she will be safe. The Naga‑Rāja protects his own.*”

---

### 7. The Aftermath

The next morning, the **bari** was quiet. **Baba** Rashid found a **silver‑threaded **“**pata‑chhata**”** left on the doorstep, embroidered with the **Naga‑Rāja’s** symbol—a **coiled serpent**. Inside the mat lay a **small **“**shankha**”** that still resonated faintly when touched.

From that day onward, the **village of Cox’s Bazar** enjoyed **abundant fish**, **calm tides**, and **prosperity**. Whenever a child fell ill, the elders would blow the **shankha**, and the river’s gentle lullaby would bring comfort.

Mona, now known as **“**Naga‑Mona**”**, was said to appear at the river’s edge during **Pôtrôb**, wearing a **kadam‑bali** and a **white sari**, singing the **“**Mongol‑giti**”** (song of the Naga). She became a guardian spirit for the fishermen, ensuring safe voyages and bountiful catches.

### 8. Cultural Nuances Preserved

| Bengali Term | English Approximation | Cultural Meaning |
|--------------|----------------------|------------------|
| **Bari** | House (thatched) | The basic family dwelling in rural Bengal |
| **Baba / Ma** | Father / Mother | Core parental figures |
| **Nana / Dada / Chacha / Pishi** | Maternal grandfather / elder brother / paternal uncle / aunt | Extended kinship network |
| **Shubho Drishti** | Auspicious eye‑contact | First gaze between bride and groom, believed to seal destiny |
| **Mangal‑Sutra** | Auspicious necklace | Symbol of marital bond; here replaced by a garland of mangrove bark |
| **Biday** | Farewell (ritual) | The ceremonial sending off of the bride |
| **Shankha** | Conch shell | Used to ward off evil and announce auspicious moments |
| **Pôtrôb** | Full moon night | Considered a powerful, mystical time |
| **Biyer Gaan** | Wedding songs | Folk songs sung by women during the ceremony |
| **Panta‑bhat** | Fermented rice | Traditional staple for celebrations |
| **Shorshe Ilish** | Hilsa in mustard sauce | Iconic Bengali delicacy, often served at weddings |
| **Kadam‑bali** | Mangrove bark garland | Local material symbolising the riverine environment |
| **Naga‑Mantra** | Snake‑prince chant | Invocation of the river deity’s protection |

---

### 9. Closing Thought

The tale of **Mona and the Prince‑of‑Snakes** is more than a romance; it is a reminder of the **interdependence** between the people of Cox’s Bazar and the **mangrove river** that sustains them. By honoring the **Naga‑Rāja** through **ritual**, **respect**, and **song**, the villagers keep the balance alive—just as their ancestors have done for generations.
