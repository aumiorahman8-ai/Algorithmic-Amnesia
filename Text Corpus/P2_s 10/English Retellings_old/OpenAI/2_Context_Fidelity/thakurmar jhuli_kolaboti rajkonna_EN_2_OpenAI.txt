**Kolaboti Rajkonna – A Retelling from *Thākurmār Jhuli***  

*The tale is told in the lilting cadence of rural Bengal, where the rustle of palm‑leaf thatch and the scent of jasmine mingle with the clatter of the village market. The names of relatives—*pishi* (father’s sister), *kaka* (father’s brother), *dadi* (grandmother), *ma* and *baba*—are woven into the story as living threads, and the rituals that mark birth, marriage and death are performed with the same reverence that has sustained generations of Bengali folk.*

---

### 1. The Kingdom and the Princess

In a far‑flung kingdom of the Bengal delta, ruled by a just and benevolent king, lived a beautiful princess named **Kolaboti**. “Kolaboti” means “the one who is like a lotus,” and indeed her skin shone with the soft glow of a moon‑lit pond. She was the only child of **Raja Bishwanath** and **Rani Madhuri**, and the whole realm adored her for her kindness, her skill at weaving *pata* (hand‑loom cloth), and her sweet voice that could coax even the shyest nightingale from the mangrove thickets.

The palace courtyard was always fragrant with *misti* (sweet) *pitha* and *payesh* (rice pudding) prepared by the royal cooks, while the *shankh* (conch) was blown at sunrise to announce the day’s first *prayer* (*sandhya arati*). The princess learned the *shlokas* (verses) of the *Vedas* and the *bhajans* (devotional songs) from the *guru* who lived in the *ashram* behind the palace.

---

### 2. The Prophecy and the Curse

One monsoon night, as the rain hammered the *chhatri* (umbrella‑shaped roof) of the palace, an old *siddha* (hermit) arrived, his body bent like a *bamboo* stalk after a storm. He bowed before the king and said:

> “O Raja, a darkness will soon descend upon your house. If Kolaboti ever marries a man of false heart, the kingdom shall be swallowed by a river of fire.”

The king, fearing for his beloved daughter, ordered the hermit to stay as a *guru* for Kolaboti, hoping his wisdom would protect her. The hermit taught her the *shakti* (inner power) of the *trishul* (trident) and the *mantras* that could ward off evil.

---

### 3. The Suitors and the *Biyer* (Wedding) Preparations

When Kolaboti turned sixteen, the kingdom’s *panchayat* (council) announced a *biyer* (wedding) *sankalpa* (intention). Suitors from distant lands arrived, each bearing gifts of *gold*, *silk* and *gifts of the river*—shimmering *shankh* and *lotus* garlands.

Among them was **Prince Ranjit**, son of the neighboring king. He was handsome, rode a white *ashva* (horse), and sang love songs in the *baul* style. Yet, the old hermit sensed a hidden *kala* (darkness) in his heart. He warned Kolaboti’s *pishi* (her father’s sister) and *kaka* (her father’s brother) that the prince’s lineage was tainted by a *bhoot* (evil spirit) that had once possessed his ancestors.

Kolaboti’s *dadi* (grandmother) whispered a protective chant, “*Om Shanti Shanti*,” and instructed the princess to keep a *kalam* (ink pot) and a *kora* (sacred thread) close to her during the *bida* (wedding ceremony.

---

### 4. The *Bida* (Wedding) Day

The *bida* was held under a grand *pandal* (temporary canopy) decorated with *shola* (pith) flowers and *bengal* *pata* (hand‑woven cloth). The *pandit* (priest) recited verses from the *Manusmriti* while the *shankh* sounded thrice, marking the auspicious *muhurat* (auspicious moment). Kolaboti, dressed in a *dhuti* of white silk embroidered with golden *kantha* (embroidery), wore a *mukut* (crown) of fresh *lotus* and *marigold*.

As the *saptapadi* (seven steps) began, Kolaboti slipped the *kora* around her wrist, whispering the *mantra* taught by the hermit. The *pandit* asked the bride and groom to exchange *tilak* (red vermilion) and *sindoor* (vermilion powder). When Prince Ranjit placed the *tilak* on Kolaboti’s forehead, a faint, cold wind brushed the *pandal*—the hermit’s warning had come true.

---

### 5. The Unveiling of the Curse

That night, after the *bida* feast of *pitha*, *payesh* and *shorshe* (mustard) fish, the palace was filled with *dhak* (drum) beats and *shehnai* (wind instrument) melodies. Yet, as the moon rose high, a terrible roar echoed from the riverbank. The *bhoot* that haunted Prince Ranjit’s lineage emerged—a serpentine *nagini* (female cobra) with eyes of fire.

The *nagini* slithered into the palace courtyard, its hiss turning the *shankh*’s sound into a mournful wail. It threatened to devour the kingdom unless Kolaboti broke the *kala* (curse). Remembering the hermit’s teachings, Kolaboti stood firm, lifted the *kora* and chanted:

> “*Om Namo Bhagavate Vasudevaya*,
>  *Shanti, Shanti, Shanti*.”

The *kora* glowed with a golden light, and the *nagini* recoiled. The princess then took a *shankha* (conch) and blew it with all her might. The sound resonated like a thunderclap, shattering the *nagini* into a thousand droplets of water that fell back into the river, restoring its calm.

Prince Ranjit fell to his knees, his eyes clearing of the dark veil. He confessed that a jealous *rakshasa* (demon) had cursed his bloodline, hoping to ruin his marriage. Kolaboti, with compassion befitting a *Rajkonna* (queen), forgave him and promised to help lift the curse forever.

---

### 6. The Ritual of Purification

The next dawn, the palace courtyard was transformed into a *puja* (ritual) ground. *Dadi* and *pishi* prepared a *pabitra* (sacred) *panchamrit* (mixture of milk, honey, ghee, sugar, and yogurt) and placed it in a *kalash* (pot) topped with a *mango leaf* and a *cotton* *bandha* (cloth). The *guru* from the hermit’s *ashram* arrived, carrying a *trishul* and a *sacred* *ash* (holy ash) from the *Ganges*.

Kolaboti, now fully aware of her inner *shakti*, performed the *abhisheka* (ritual bathing) of the *kalash* with *panchamrit*, while chanting the *Vedic* *mantras* for protection. The *guru* sprinkled *ash* over the *kalash* and over Prince Ranjit, symbolically cleansing the *kala* that had plagued his lineage.

The *panchayat* declared the curse broken. The *shankh* was blown three times, each blast louder than the last, announcing the triumph of good over evil.

---

### 7. The Kingdom’s Renewal

With the curse lifted, the kingdom blossomed anew. The *paddy* fields turned a richer shade of green, the *mango* trees bore fruit earlier, and the *river* sang a gentle lullaby as it flowed past the palace. Kolaboti and Prince Ranjit ruled together as *Rajkonna* and *Rajkumar*, embodying the virtues of compassion, wisdom, and courage.

Every year, on the anniversary of the night the *nagini* was vanquished, the people of the kingdom celebrate **Kolaboti Rajkonna Utsav**. The festivities begin with a *prayer* at the *shiva* *lingam* in the palace courtyard, followed by a *pandal* of lotus flowers, *pitha* making contests, and a *baul* song competition. Children dress in *dhuti* and *saree* and perform the *saptapadi* dance, reenacting the seven steps of the original *bida*—a reminder that love, when guarded by righteousness, can dispel even the darkest of curses.

---

### 8. Closing Thought

*Kolaboti Rajkonna* is more than a story of a princess and a cursed prince; it is a tapestry of Bengali cultural threads—*pishi* and *kaka* offering counsel, *dadi* preserving oral tradition, the *shankh* announcing the rhythm of life, and the *puja* that binds the community to the divine. The tale reminds us that true royalty lies not in crowns or palaces, but in the *shakti* (inner strength) that each person carries, and in the rituals that keep that strength alive across generations.
