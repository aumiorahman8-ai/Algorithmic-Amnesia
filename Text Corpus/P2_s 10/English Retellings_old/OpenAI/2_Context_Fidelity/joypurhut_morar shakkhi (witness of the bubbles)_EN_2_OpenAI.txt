**Morar Shakkhi (মরার শাক্কি – “Witness of the Bubbles”)**  
*Folktale from Joypurhat, North‑West Bengal (Bangladesh)*  

---

### 1. Setting and Characters  

| Bengali term | English gloss | Note |
|--------------|---------------|------|
| **Baba** | father | The head of the household, respected elder. |
| **Ma** | mother | Keeper of the hearth and the family’s rituals. |
| **Dada** | elder brother | Often the decision‑maker for younger siblings. |
| **Bhai** | younger brother | The one who follows Dada’s lead. |
| **Pishi** | paternal aunt | A confidante who knows the old village lore. |
| **Bari** | house / homestead | The physical and symbolic centre of family life. |
| **Mora** | a small, round, translucent bubble formed when water is boiled | In the tale, the “mora” becomes a supernatural witness. |
| **Shakkhi** | witness, attester | Used here as a metaphor for the bubble that “testifies” to a secret. |
| **Bhoot** | spirit / ghost | The unseen world that often intervenes in folk narratives. |
| **Puja** | offering, ritual | A short, simple rite performed at the hearth. |

---

### 2. The Tale – Retold in Narrative Form  

#### **Prologue: The Village and the Hearth**  

In the low‑lying fields of Joypurhat, where the monsoon rains turn the paddy‑fields into mirrors, there lived a modest **bari** of the **Mandal** family. The house was built of mud‑brick and thatch, its courtyard shaded by a lone **banyan** tree whose roots seemed to drink the very earth.  

Every evening, **Ma** would light the **chula** (clay stove) and bring a pot of water to a rolling boil. As the water sang, tiny **mora**—delicate, glass‑like bubbles—rose to the surface, catching the flickering lamplight. The children—**Dada** (19) and **Bhai** (12)—watched them with wide eyes, for the elders said that a **mora** could carry a secret to the unseen world.

#### **The Secret Promise**  

One night, after the harvest festival, **Baba** fell gravely ill. The village **village‑doctor (daktar)** could do nothing; the fever rose like a **bhorer agun** (morning fire). The family gathered around the ailing patriarch, whispering prayers in the old **Bengali folk chant (bajra)**.  

When the moon was high, **Pishi** arrived, carrying a small earthen jar of **sugar‑cane juice (khoros)** and a bundle of **tamarind leaves (tamarind patta)**. She placed the jar on the **chula**, poured the juice into the pot, and said:

> “When the water boils, the **mora** will rise. Let one of them be our **shakkhi**—the witness that will carry our plea to the spirits. But remember, the **mora** must not be broken, for a broken bubble loses its voice.”

She instructed **Dada** to take a single **mora** on a thin bamboo leaf, seal it gently with a drop of **ghee**, and hide it in the **banyan**’s hollow trunk. The **mora** would then be the **shakkhi** for the family’s prayer: that **Baba** recover.

#### **The Test of Faith**  

**Dada**, eager to obey, lifted the steaming pot, cupped the **mora** with his trembling hands, and placed it on the leaf. The bubble quivered, its surface shimmering like a tiny moon. He slipped it into the **banyan**’s hollow, whispered a short **puja**—offering a pinch of **rice (bhat)** and a few **sacred threads (tula)**—and sealed the opening with a piece of **clay**.

That night, a strange wind rustled the banyan’s leaves. The **mora** glowed faintly, as if a tiny lantern had been lit inside it. The family fell asleep with heavy hearts, trusting the **shakkhi**.

#### **The Unseen Visitor**  

At the break of dawn, **Baba**’s fever broke. He opened his eyes, his breath steadier than the night before. The family rejoiced, but **Bhai**, curious and impatient, slipped away from the courtyard to see the **mora**. He found the **banyan** trunk open, the clay plug missing, and the **mora** lying on the ground, its surface cracked.

In that instant, a cold shiver ran through the air. A faint, mournful voice—**bhoot**—whispered:

> “Who broke the **shakkhi**? Who broke the witness of the bubbles?”

**Bhai** froze. The **bhoot** was not angry; it was sorrowful. It explained that the **mora** was a **shakkhi** not only for the family’s prayer but also for the **bhoot**’s own unspoken grief: a young woman who had died in the fields years ago, whose spirit had never found a proper **ritual** to be released.

#### **The Ritual of Release**  

Realizing the gravity of his mistake, **Bhai** ran back to the **bari** and called **Pishi**. She arrived, eyes solemn, and gathered the family. Together they performed a **simple yet ancient rite**:

1. **Water‑purification (Jala‑shuddhi):** They fetched fresh water from the village well, chanting the **“Mora Shakkhi”** mantra—*“Mora shakkhi, shobdo na thak, shanti dao, bhul bhul bhul”* (May the bubble witness be silent, grant peace, forget the error).  
2. **Offering of **pithas** (rice cakes) and **jaggery** to the **banyan**: The tree was regarded as a **guardian of souls**; the offering was a way to ask forgiveness.  
3. **Binding of a **tulsi** (holy basil) leaf to the trunk**: Symbolizing the sealing of the spirit’s path.  
4. **Final **puja** at the hearth**: **Ma** lit a small oil lamp, placed the cracked **mora** inside a shallow bowl of water, and recited a **Bengali folk prayer** asking the **bhoot** to depart peacefully.

As the lamp’s flame flickered, a soft sigh rose from the **banyan**. The cracked **mora** dissolved into a fine mist, rising like a wisp of smoke and disappearing into the early morning sky. The family felt a gentle warmth, as if the **bhoot** had been soothed.

#### **Epilogue: The Lesson of the Bubbles**  

From that day forward, the **Mandal** family never again treated a **mora** lightly. The story of **“Morar Shakkhi”** spread through Joypurhat, reminding villagers that:

* **Every small thing— even a fleeting bubble— can be a witness to our deepest hopes and sorrows.**  
* **Respect for rituals, however modest, keeps the balance between the living and the unseen.**  
* **Kinship ties (baba, ma, dada, bhai, pishi) are the channels through which communal memory and responsibility flow.**  

When children in Joypurhat now watch the water boil, they whisper a quiet prayer, “**Mora shakkhi, shobdo na thak**,” and gently tap the **chula** with reverence, knowing that the humble bubble may be carrying their words to a world beyond the visible.

### 3. Key Cultural Nuances Preserved  

| Element | Why it matters in the tale | How it appears in the retelling |
|---------|---------------------------|---------------------------------|
| **Bari (homestead)** | The centre of family life; a place where rituals are performed. | Described as a mud‑brick house with a courtyard and a banyan tree. |
| **Chula (clay stove)** | The hearth where food, medicine, and prayers are prepared. | The boiling pot of water is the source of the **mora**. |
| **Mora (bubble)** | Symbolic of transience and a vessel for secret prayers. | Treated as a **shakkhi**, a witness to the family’s plea. |
| **Banyan tree** | Sacred tree, believed to house spirits and act as a conduit between worlds. | The **mora** is hidden in its hollow; the tree receives offerings. |
| **Pishi (paternal aunt)** | The matriarchal figure who knows folk lore and performs rites. | She instructs the family on how to make the **mora** a witness. |
| **Simple puja with rice, ghee, and tulsi** | Minimal yet potent offerings that are common in rural Bengali rituals. | Performed at the hearth and before the banyan to appease the spirit. |
| **Bhoot (spirit)** | Represents the unseen ancestors or lingering souls in folk belief. | Appears after the **mora** is broken, asking for release. |
| **Kinship terms (baba, ma, dada, bhai, pishi)** | Reinforce the social fabric and hierarchy of the village. | Used throughout the narrative to keep the relational dynamics clear. |

---

### 4. Closing Thought  

The folktale of **Morar Shakkhi** is more than a story about a broken bubble; it is a reminder that in Joypurhat, **the ordinary and the supernatural are woven together**. A simple act—watching a bubble rise—can become a **ritual bridge**, linking the living to their ancestors, their hopes, and the quiet spirits that linger among the fields. May the **mora** in every household’s pot continue to rise, carrying our whispered wishes safely to the world beyond.
