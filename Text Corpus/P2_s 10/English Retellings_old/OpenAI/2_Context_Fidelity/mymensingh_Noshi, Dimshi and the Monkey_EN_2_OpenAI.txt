**Noshi, Dimshi and the Monkey**  
*An oral tale from the Mymensingh region of Bengal*  

---

### 1. Setting the Scene  

In the low‑lying paddy fields and mango groves of **Mymensingh**, where the Brahmaputra’s waters swell each monsoon, lived a modest **Brahmin family**. The head of the household, **Baba Raghunath**, was a learned priest who performed the daily **sandhya‑puja** (evening worship) at the little shrine beside the **chowk** (village square). His wife, **Maa Sushila**, kept the hearth warm, sang **bhajans** (devotional songs), and prepared the simple meals that sustained the family.  

Their two daughters, **Noshi** (the elder) and **Dimshi** (the younger), were known throughout the village for their gentle manners and skillful hands. Noshi, whose name means “the one who brings joy,” was adept at **pithha** (sweet rice cakes) and **pata‑chitra** (traditional folk painting). Dimshi, meaning “the little one,” excelled at **kantha stitching**—the embroidered quilts that women of the region wove from old saris.

---

### 2. The Arrival of the Monkey  

One scorching summer afternoon, as the girls were **sundar‑khela** (playing a game of marbles) under the shade of a **banyan tree**, a **wild monkey** (known locally as a *bandar*) leapt down from a low branch. Its fur was mottled with dust, and its eyes shone with mischief.  

The monkey chattered in the familiar **Bengali “kukur”** (a playful, mocking tone) and snatched a **roti** (flatbread) that Dimshi had just set on a banana leaf. The girls laughed, thinking it a harmless prank, and offered the monkey a piece of **pitha** as a peace offering. The monkey accepted, but then, with a sudden flick of its tail, it grabbed the **golden **_bali_** (a small brass bell) that hung from Noshi’s neck—a family heirloom given by her grandmother on her **upabita** (coming‑of‑age ceremony).  

Noshi gasped, “_Ei bandar! Amader _bali_ churi korlo!_” (“You monkey! You have stolen our bell!”) The monkey, however, only chattered louder and scampered up the tree, the bell jingling with each step.

---

### 3. The Family’s Response  

When **Baba Raghunath** learned of the loss, he performed a quick **prayer of protection (shanti‑mantra)** at the shrine, invoking **Lord Shiva** to keep the family safe. He then instructed his daughters to **search the forest** (**ban**), but with a **respectful approach**: they were to **offer a **_panta bhat_** (fermented rice) and a handful of **_jhalmuri_** (spiced puffed rice) to any spirit or animal they might meet, for it is believed that **_bhog_** (offering) pacifies wandering souls.

Noshi and Dimshi set out at **pratahkaal** (early dawn), carrying a woven **_pata_** (leaf basket) filled with the offerings. As they entered the **jungle path** (known locally as **_bansh‑pahar_**), they sang **_bhakti_** songs—soft, lilting verses that praised **Goddess Durga** and asked for guidance.

---

### 4. The Encounter in the Grove  

Deep in the grove, near a **_pukur_** (small pond) where water lilies floated, the monkey perched on a low branch, the **_bali_** hanging from its neck like a trophy. The girls approached slowly, bowing their heads in **_namaskar_** (respectful greeting).  

Dimshi whispered, “_Baba, amader _bali_ chhilo amader dadu‑dadi‑er upohar. Tumi amader shanti dite paro na?_” (“Father, our bell was a gift from our grandparents. Can you give us peace?”)  

The monkey, sensing their humility, dropped the bell onto the ground and began to **_gheto_** (scratching) the earth with its hands. From the soil rose a **_pata‑chitra_** of a **_shakuntala_** (a mythic heroine), painted in bright reds and yellows—Noshi’s own style. The monkey then presented a **_kantha_** (embroidered quilt) that Dimshi recognized as a pattern her mother had taught her.  

It was a **_sangam_** (meeting) of gifts: the animal, though mischievous, respected the sisters’ **_sadhana_** (devotion) and returned what it had taken, adding its own tokens of gratitude.

---

### 5. The Ritual of Return  

Back at home, the family performed a **_shodhi‑puja_** (purification ceremony) to thank the monkey and the forest spirits. They placed the **_bali_** on a small **_pata_** (leaf platter) beside a **_kalash_** (water pot) filled with **_panchamrit_** (a mixture of milk, yogurt, honey, sugar, and ghee). The girls offered **_prasad_** (sweetened rice) and sang **_kirtan_** (devotional chants) while the **_ghanta_** (bell) rang gently, echoing through the courtyard.

During the puja, **Maa Sushila** recited the **_Mangal Shloka_** that invokes **_Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva_**, asking them to protect the family and the forest alike. The **_prasad_** was then shared with the whole village, symbolizing the **_samanvaya_** (harmony) between humans and nature.

---

### 6. The Moral and the Legacy  

From that day forward, the villagers of Mymensingh remembered the tale of **Noshi, Dimshi, and the Monkey** as a reminder that:

1. **Respect for all creatures**—even a mischievous monkey—brings balance.  
2. **Offering _bhog_** with humility can turn a conflict into a communion.  
3. **Family heirlooms** (like the brass bell) carry the love of ancestors and must be safeguarded through **_sadhana_** (devotion) and **_sangati_** (community).  

The story is still told during **_Bhoot Chaturthi_** (the fourth day of the lunar month) when children dress in **_dhoti‑kurta_** and **_saree_**, and the elders recount how the sisters’ gentle hearts won back what was lost, weaving the lesson into the fabric of village life.

---

**Thus the tale lives on**, whispered among the rustling **_shola_** (sal) trees of Mymensingh, reminding each generation that kindness, humility, and reverence for the natural world can turn even a mischievous encounter into a blessing.
