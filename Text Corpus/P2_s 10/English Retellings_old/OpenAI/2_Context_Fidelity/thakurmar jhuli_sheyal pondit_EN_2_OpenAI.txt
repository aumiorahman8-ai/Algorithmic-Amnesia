**Sheyal Pondit – A Retelling from *Thākurmār Jhuli***  

*The story is taken from the beloved Bengali folk‑tale collection *Thākurmār Jhuli* (Grandmother’s Bag of Tales).  The names of the characters, the kinship terms, and the ritual details have been kept as they appear in the original, so that the cultural texture of rural Bengal in the age of the zamindars may be felt by the reader.*

---

### 1. The Village and the Family

In a small, river‑lined village of Bengal lived **Bābā Mohan**, a humble but devout Brahmin priest.  He dwelt in a modest **chhāyā‑bari** (thatched house) with his **ma** (mother), his **dādī** (grandmother), his **bāi** (elder sister) **Madhuri**, and his **bāpu** (father) who had long since passed away.  The family survived on the modest rent they collected from the **pāni‑bāri** (water‑tax) of the nearby fields.

Mohan’s sister‑in‑law, **Pishima** (the elder sister’s husband’s sister), was a sharp‑tongued woman who often chided him for his “*bāṛi‑bāṛi*” (idle) ways.  Yet, despite the teasing, the whole household respected the **Pondit** (priest) for his knowledge of the **Vedas**, the **Puranas**, and the many **śruti** (rituals) that kept the village’s spiritual life in balance.

---

### 2. The Invitation of the King

One monsoon evening, a royal **messenger** arrived at the village gate, his **pagri** (turban) dripping with rain.  He bore a royal **patra** (letter) from **Rājā Mahendra**, the king of the neighboring kingdom.  The king, it read, was to hold a grand **śrāddha‑pūja** (ancestral rite) for his departed father, and he desired the most learned priest in the land to conduct the ceremony.  

The messenger announced that the king’s court would travel to the village to select the priest.  All the villagers gathered at the **pāṭh** (village square) and whispered: *“Who among us can match the king’s expectations?”*  

Mohan, though modest, felt a stirring of **dharma** (righteous duty).  He consulted his **ma**, who placed a **tilak** of sandalwood paste on his forehead and said, *“If the king seeks a true **Pondit**, the gods will guide you.”*  

---

### 3. The King’s Test

The next morning, a splendid **royal procession** entered the village.  The king rode a **shikhara‑horse**, his **sena** (troops) flanking him, and his **raj‑purohit** (court priest) carried a golden **sankha** (conch) and a **danda** (staff).  

The king announced his test: *“Whoever can keep a **sheyal** (a clever fox) from escaping the **mandap** (sacred pavilion) during the **aarti** shall be my priest for the śrāddha.”*  

The villagers gasped.  The **sheyal** was notorious for outwitting men, slipping through the smallest cracks, and stealing the **prasad** (offering) from the altar.  The king’s **panchayat** (council) warned that any failure would bring a curse upon the village.

Mohan stepped forward, bowing low.  *“Maharaj, I shall try,”* he said, his voice steady as the **ghor** (river) that ran nearby.

---

### 4. The Ritual Preparations

The **mandap** was erected in the centre of the **pāṭh**, its roof of **bamboo** and **jute** draped with **marigold garlands**.  A **kalash** (sacred pot) filled with water, topped with a coconut and a **sugarcane** stalk, stood at the centre.  The **priest** (Mohan) arranged **sandalwood paste**, **kumkum**, **turmeric**, and **incense** (agarbatti) on a low **pata** (plate).  

He performed the **prāṇāyāma** (breathing) and **mantra‑japa** (chanting) to invoke **Ganesha**, the remover of obstacles.  The **sheyal**, a sleek russet creature with bright eyes, was released from a bamboo cage by the king’s **senapati** (general) and set free inside the mandap.

The **aarti** began: the **diyas** (oil lamps) were lit, the **shankha** was blown, and the **prasad**—sweet **payesh** (rice pudding) and **pitha** (steamed rice cakes)—was placed on a silver **thali**.  The **sheyal** sniffed the air, its nose twitching.

---

### 5. The Clever Pondit’s Strategy

Mohan remembered a **purāṇa** story of a sage who trapped a mischievous spirit by offering it a **kāḍi** (bamboo stick) that seemed to be a **sādhana** (spiritual tool).  He whispered a **mantra** of **Māyā** (illusion) and gently placed a **piece of dried **kāḍi** near the **sheyal**.  

The fox, curious, approached the stick.  Mohan then began a **chant** of **Vāyu** (wind) while sprinkling **sindoor** (vermilion) around the stick.  The **sheyal**’s eyes widened; it felt an invisible force pulling it toward the stick.  

Mohan, with a swift motion, lifted the **kāḍi** and, as the **aarti** bells rang, he whispered, *“May you be bound by the sacred fire of truth.”*  

A sudden **flame**—the **aarti**’s own light—seemed to wrap around the **sheyal**, not burning it but holding it in a **halo** of **prāṇa** (life‑force).  The fox, now entranced, sat still, its tail curled around the stick, as if it had become a **devotee**.

The villagers gasped in awe.  The **king** smiled, his eyes shining with respect.  He declared, *“This Pondit has turned a cunning fox into a humble disciple. He shall be my priest for the śrāddha‑pūja.”*

---

### 6. The Śrāddha‑Pūja and the Blessings

The **śrāddha‑pūja** was performed the following day.  Mohan, now **Sheyal Pondit**, led the rites with the **sheyal** perched obediently on the **kalash**.  He offered **tīrtha** (holy water) to the ancestors, recited the **ṛk** (sacred verses), and performed the **tarpana** (libation) while the **sheyal** watched with bright eyes.  

During the **pradakshina** (circumambulation) around the **mandap**, the **sheyal** followed the priest, its paws making soft thuds on the **pata‑chowk** (earth).  The **king’s** court marveled at the harmony between man and beast, a sign of **sāmānya** (balance) between the material and the spiritual.

When the ceremony concluded, the **king** presented Mohan with a **golden **kora** (bracelet) and a **pouch of **sikri** (rice).  He also granted the village a **land grant** (bigha) for a **pāni‑bāri**, ensuring prosperity for generations.

---

### 7. The Moral and the Legacy

Back in the village, the **sheyal** was released into the nearby **bāṛi‑bāṛi** (forest).  It never again stole the **prasad**, but would sometimes sit at the edge of the **mandap**, watching the villagers perform their daily **puja**.  

Mohan’s **ma** whispered, *“A true Pondit does not rely on force, but on wisdom and the subtle power of **Māyā**.”*  

The tale of **Sheyal Pondit** spread far beyond the riverbanks, reminding all who heard it that cleverness, humility, and devotion can turn even the craftiest of creatures into a companion on the path of dharma.

#### Cultural Notes (for the reader)

| Bengali term | English approximation | Context in the story |
|--------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Pondit** | Priest, learned scholar | Central figure, performs rituals |
| **Sheyal** | Fox (often symbolic of cunning) | Test animal in the king’s challenge |
| **Ma, Dadi, Bapa, Bhabhi, Pishima** | Mother, Grandmother, Father, Sister‑in‑law, Elder sister’s sister‑in‑law | Kinship terms that locate the family structure |
| **Mandap** | Sacred pavilion | Site of the aarti and the test |
| **Aarti** | Ritual of light with lamps | Central ritual that creates the binding flame |
| **Kalash** | Sacred pot with water, coconut, sugarcane | Symbol of abundance and purity |
| **Prasad** | Blessed offering (sweet rice pudding, pitha) | Food offered to deities and later shared |
| **Shankha** | Conch shell blown to start ceremonies | Signifies auspicious beginning |
| **Panchayat** | Village council | Advises the king on the test |
| **Śrāddha‑pūja** | Ancestral rites for a departed father | The king’s purpose for inviting a priest |
| **Māyā** | Illusion, subtle power of the divine | Used by the Pondit to bind the fox |
| **Tarpana** | Libation to ancestors | Part of the śrāddha ceremony |
| **Pradakshina** | Circumambulation around the sacred space | Symbolic act of devotion |

These terms have been retained to preserve the story’s authentic Bengali flavor, allowing the listener to hear the rhythm of the original folk tradition while still understanding the narrative in English.  

---  

*Thus ends the retelling of “Sheyal Pondit,” a tale of wit, devotion, and the gentle triumph of dharma over mischief, as it lives on in the pages of *Thākurmār Jhuli* and in the hearts of Bengali storytellers.*
