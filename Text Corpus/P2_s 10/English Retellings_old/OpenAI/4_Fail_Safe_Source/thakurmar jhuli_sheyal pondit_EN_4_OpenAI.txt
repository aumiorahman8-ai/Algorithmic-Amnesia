**The Tale of the Jackal Pundit and the Crocodile**

In the Shanti forest a jackal, the son of a man who had once built a wall, decided to become a teacher.  Twirling his long whiskers, he opened a grand school that soon filled with every kind of forest creature—grasshopper youngsters, tortoises, millipedes with a thousand feet, earthworms, scorpions, dung beetles, cockroaches, frogs, crabs, spiders, and countless other legs.  The school buzzed day and night with study and the pundit’s calls.

A crocodile, hearing the uproar, worried that his own offspring would be left behind.  He brought his seven sons to the jackal’s school, hoping they too would become learned.  The pundit promised the crocodile that within seven days each son would turn into a “titan of knowledge, a master archer.”  Overjoyed, the crocodile returned home.

During those six days the pundit ate one of the crocodile’s young each day as a snack.  On the seventh morning the crocodile, eager to see his sons’ transformation, dressed in a tattered jute cloth, a torn fishing‑net shawl and a fisherman’s hat, and went to the school.  He asked to see how his boys were doing.

The pundit hurried him in, called for tobacco and snuff, and then disappeared into his den.  He held up the last remaining young crocodile and showed it seven times, declaring that all the boys were now great scholars and would become master archers if they stayed one more day.  The crocodile, satisfied, left happy.

That night the pundit finished his final snack with the last young crocodile, dismantled the school and fled.  When the crocodile returned the next day he found the forest empty, the school gone, and the pundit nowhere to be seen.  Realizing the trick, he swore revenge, promising the pundit would never again eat crabs or wander the canal.

The crocodile hid in the canal water.  Days later the pundit, hungry and seeing crabs dancing on the opposite bank, could not resist and leapt into the water.  The crocodile seized what he thought was the pundit’s leg.  In the struggle the pundit broke a reed and laughed, claiming the crocodile had only caught a stick.  The crocodile, believing it, bit the reed; the pundit escaped, leapt onto the bank and declared he would reopen his school and welcome the crocodile’s sons again.

Time passed, and the crocodile could not catch the jackal’s tail.  Finally, the crocodile pretended to be dead on the canal bank, lying perfectly still with his mouth open to the sun.  The pundit, suspicious, examined the “corpse,” noting that a dead creature’s ears should twitch and its tail should slap.  The crocodile, trying to obey, twitched his head and slapped his tail, drawing the attention of nearby cowherds.  The cowherds, recalling a crocodile that had eaten a calf, hurled sickles, sticks, bricks and stones at him.  The pundit fled, shouting “Hokka Hua!” and ran into an egg‑plant field.

There the hungry pundit ate the eggplants, but a thorn pierced his nose.  He sneezed, bled, and, weak and desperate, went to a barber’s house asking for help.  The barber, compassionate, tried to remove the thorn, but in the process the pundit’s nose was cut off.  Angrily the pundit demanded the barber give him his nail‑clipper; the barber, terrified, complied.

Carrying the nail‑clipper, the pundit reached a potter’s house.  The potter, curious about the clip, examined it, but it snapped.  The pundit, furious, demanded a replacement.  When the potter could not provide a new clip, the pundit demanded a pot instead; the potter gave him one.

While the pundit carried the pot, a wedding procession passed by.  A firecracker from the celebration landed in the pot and shattered it.  The pundit blamed the groom and demanded compensation.  The groom, pleading, offered the bride to the pundit.  The pundit accepted the bride and continued on his way.

He then went to a drummer’s house, asking the drummer to lend all his drums for his own wedding, promising the bride would stay with the drummer while he went to the priest.  The drummer obliged.  While the pundit was away, the drummer’s wife, chopping vegetables, accidentally cut the bride in two with a knife; frightened, she hid the two halves of the bride in a haystack.

When the pundit returned with the priest, the bride was missing.  He demanded the drummer’s wife return the bride.  Terrified, she begged for mercy and offered the drummer’s dhol (large drum) instead.  The pundit took the dhol, climbed a palm tree and began to sing and play:

```
Tak duma dum dum!
In the egg‑plant field a thorn pricked me,
Tak duma dum dum!
Removing the thorn, I lost my nose,
Tak duma dum dum!
For my nose I got a nail‑clipper,
Tak duma dum dum!
For the nail‑clipper I got a pot,
Tak duma dum dum!
For the pot I got a bride,
Tak duma dum dum!
For the bride I have now got a dhol,
Tak duma dum dum!
Dagum dagum dug duma dum!
Dum duma dum dum!!
```

Just as he was dancing in sheer joy, his foot slipped… and the story ends there.
