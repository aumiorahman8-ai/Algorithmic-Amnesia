**The Tale of the Seven Queens and the Lost Children**

A king ruled a great kingdom with seven queens. The five older queens were haughty and proud, walking as if their feet barely touched the ground. The youngest queen, by contrast, was gentle and kind, and because of this the king loved her most.

For many years the king had no children, and the kingdom felt empty and sorrowful. At last the youngest queen became pregnant. Overjoyed, the king announced throughout the land that the royal treasury was open—anyone could take sweets, gems, and jewels as they wished. The older queens, burning with jealousy, watched the celebration with spite.

Before the birth, the king fastened a golden chain to his own waist and to the youngest queen’s waist. He told her, “When the child is born, tug this chain and I will come to see the baby.” Then he returned to his court.

When the time of birth came, the older queens claimed they would attend the birthing chamber themselves. As soon as they entered, they pulled the chain. The king, hearing the tug, left his court with drums, pipes, and priests bearing treasure, only to find the room empty. He returned to his court, and the chain was tugged again. He rushed back, again to find nothing. Angrily, the king swore, “If the chain is pulled again before a child is born, I will have all the queens beheaded,” and he left the chamber.

The youngest queen gave birth to eight children—seven sons and one daughter—who shone like moon‑light dolls, their tiny hands and feet waving, filling the room with light. She asked her sisters, “Sisters, show me the child!” The older queens mocked her, gesturing and shouting, “Not a child—an elephant! A few rats and crabs!” Their cruelty drove the youngest queen into a faint.

The older queens then concealed the newborns in earthen pots, sealed them, and buried the pots in the ash heap. After they returned, they pulled the chain once more. The king again arrived with music and treasure, only to be presented with tadpoles and baby mice that the queens had hurriedly produced as a false “child.” Enraged, the king drove the youngest queen from the palace. The older queens laughed, their anklets jingling, and lived happily while the palace fell into sorrow.

The youngest queen, now reduced to a dung‑gathering maid, roamed the streets. The kingdom grew bleak: flowers failed to bloom, the king’s daily worship could not be performed, and the king himself felt no joy.

One day the royal gardener reported, “Your Majesty, I cannot find flowers for worship, but on the ash heap a seven‑Champa tree and a Parul tree have each produced perfect flowers—seven Champa blossoms and one Parul blossom.” The king ordered the gardener to bring them.

When the gardener approached the trees, the Parul blossom called to the seven Champa blossoms, “Sisters, wake up!” The Champa blossoms answered, “Why do you call us, sister?” The Parul flower replied, “The gardener has come. Will you give your flowers?” The Champa blossoms rose, refusing, “We will not give them now; we will rise a hundred leagues higher. Let the king come first, then we will give them.”

The gardener ran to tell the king. The king and his court hurried to the ash heap. When the king arrived, the Parul flower again called, “Sisters, wake up!” The Champa blossoms responded the same way, refusing to give their flowers until the king’s eldest queen arrived. The eldest queen came, but the blossoms still refused, asking for the second queen. One by one the second, third, fourth, fifth, and sixth queens arrived, each met with the same refusal. The blossoms rose higher, blooming in the sky like stars.

At last the blossoms addressed the “disgraced queen.” They said, “Only if the king’s dung‑gathering maid comes will we give our flowers.” The king sent a palanquin; guards fetched the youngest queen, now covered in cow dung and wearing torn clothes. She went to the blossoms, and at once the Champa flowers descended from the sky, the Parul flower joining them. From within the blossoms emerged seven handsome, moon‑like princes and one princess, all crying, “Mother, Mother!” They leapt into the arms of the dung‑gathering maid.

The king wept, the elder queens trembled with fear. The king ordered the six older queens to be buried alive in a pit of thorns, piled high with more thorns. He then took the seven princes, the daughter Parul, and the youngest queen back to the palace. Victory drums rang throughout the royal palace, and the kingdom was restored.
