A long time ago, in a certain country, a husband and wife lived with their only daughter, Bilkis Banu. Bilkis was extraordinarily beautiful, and as she grew older her beauty only increased. The family of three had everything they needed, but when Bilkis reached marriageable age the parents could find no suitable groom. Suddenly the father died, and the neighbors buried him. After his death the household consisted only of mother and daughter, and the mother—named Sona Banu—became even more anxious to marry off her beautiful child. Sona Banu was described as extremely greedy and cunning.

The family’s house stood on the edge of a hill, surrounded by dense jungle. One cold winter morning at the end of the month of Poush, the mother and daughter were sitting in the courtyard trying to warm themselves in the sun when a huge python, which had taken shelter in the bushes during the night, emerged and slithered toward them. The sunlight reflected off the snake’s scales, startling the two women, who fled into the house and bolted the door. The python stretched itself across the doorway and remained motionless all day and into the afternoon.

Because pythons are non‑venomous but can swallow large prey when hungry, Sona Banu, believing the creature could not be a real snake—“if it were a snake it would have left by now”—concluded that a prince must be disguised as a snake. Greedy for a marriage alliance, she decided to give Bilkis to the “Snake Prince.” She dressed her daughter and sent her to the python.

The hungry snake began to swallow Bilkis piece by piece. As each part entered the snake’s mouth, Bilkis cried out in pain, and her mother tried to soothe her with false reassurances:

* When the snake took Bilkis’s feet, the girl shouted, “Oh mother, my feet are hurting.”  
  Sona Banu replied, “Oh daughter, no, no, he is putting anklets on your feet.”

* When the snake reached her waist, Bilkis said, “Oh mother, my waist is hurting.”  
  Her mother answered, “Oh daughter, no, no, he is putting a golden waist‑chain on you.”

* When the snake swallowed her hands, Bilkis cried, “Oh mother, my hands are hurting.”  
  Sona Banu soothed, “Oh daughter, no, no, he is putting golden bangles on you.”

* When the snake reached her neck, Bilkis exclaimed, “Oh mother, my throat is hurting.”  
  Her mother replied, “Oh daughter, no, no, he is putting a necklace on you.”

* When the snake took her ears, Bilkis said, “Oh mother, my ears are hurting.”  
  Sona Banu answered, “Oh daughter, no, no, he is putting golden earrings on you.”

* When the snake finally swallowed her head, Bilkis wailed, “Oh mother, my head is hurting.”  
  Her mother comforted, “Oh daughter, no, no, he is putting a head ornament on you.”

At last the snake had swallowed Bilkis completely. Sona Banu imagined that her new son‑in‑law was now content and might later emerge in human form, perhaps returning her daughter’s gold jewelry. She waited through the fading daylight and the night, hoping the snake would release Bilkis. Eventually she fell asleep.

When dawn arrived, the snake, having digested its meal, heaved a sigh, regurgitated Bilkis’s clothes, and slipped back into the jungle. Sona Banu rushed to the door, only to find the snake gone and her daughter missing; only the discarded garments lay on the ground. Realizing that the “prince” had been a real python, she wept bitterly for the loss of Bilkis, who had been the only companion she had left.

Grief-stricken and alone, Sona Banu left her hill‑top home, went to a populated area, and took shelter in the courtyard of another house.
