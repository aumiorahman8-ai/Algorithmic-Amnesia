**The Tale of the Dice‑Bound Queen, the Ogress, and the Eight Princes**

A king ruled a palace that held a palm tree. He had a queen and a single son, the prince. The queen’s life was bound to a pair of dice. An ogress who lived in the palace’s palm tree knew this secret, but she had never found a chance to use it.

One day the king went hunting. While the king was away, the prince was playing dice with five companions. Disguised as a beggar woman, the ogress approached the prince and asked for the dice. The careless prince handed them over. With three breaths the ogress sent the dice—containing the queen’s life—to another kingdom. In her chambers the queen fainted; the ogress then ate the queen, took her form, and sat in the queen’s place.

The king returned as usual and was served by the impostor queen. The prince, while his “mother” served food, saw a drop of saliva fall from her tongue. Horrified, he stopped eating, rose silently and left the palace, and no one else noticed.

Years later the king fathered seven more sons. At the celebration of their births the prince alone observed that the palm tree’s top was withering and no bird perched there, but he kept silent. The seven younger princes grew, underwent the usual rites—first rice‑eating, tonsure, sacred thread—and then asked to travel the world. The king refused, saying the eldest son had not gone, and sent word to him.

The eldest prince answered by mounting a winged horse, flew to his brothers, and invited them to travel together. All eight princes, dressed in finery, set out from the palace with their retinue.

From the palace rooftop the ogress‑queen, now in the queen’s form, saw the princes depart and feared for her plans. She rushed to her chambers, opened a casket, and released the tiny, thread‑thin Sutashankha snake. She commanded it:

> “Sutashankha, tell me where the prince’s life lies.”

The snake answered in a conch‑like voice that the Pomegranate Prince’s life lay in a pomegranate seed. The ogress then sent the snake on a wind‑borne mission to the kingdom of Yama‑Yamuna, to deliver a missive to Pashabati, demanding seven daughters for her seven sons and threatening to have the snake devour any rival who came.

The ogress also summoned a Pakshiraj (a great bird) to carry a pomegranate to Pashabati’s kingdom. She then went to the palace’s thousand steps, asked them whose they were, and, after they replied “whoever walks on them,” she split the steps in two and hid the pomegranate seed in the crack, sealing it forever. She fell asleep on a milk‑white bed.

At that moment, in the forest where the eight princes rode, the eldest prince’s eyes were blinded by a sudden crack. He cried out that a scorpion had bitten him and that he was doomed. A storm fell, darkness covered the forest, and the seven younger princes rode on, their horses ahead of the storm.

The Sutashankha, exhausted after traveling through trees, rested in a king’s garden, coiled inside a fruit. The princess of that kingdom ate the fruit daily; the snake and the ogress’s missive entered her stomach.

The seven princes, unaware of any missive, continued their journey. When they reached a dawn‑lit plain they realized their eldest brother was missing. Assuming he had fallen behind, they slowed, then after days of no sign they concluded the horses must have gone ahead. They spurred their winged horses forward and arrived at the city of Pashabati.

Pashabati, seated in a decorated house with a banner, declared that anyone who could defeat her at dice would win her hand and those of her six sisters. She demanded a missive as proof of demonic identity; the princes had none, so she forced them to play. They lost; the seven sisters sliced the princes and their winged horses into pieces, ate them, and then resumed their beautiful forms.

The blind eldest prince, carried on a winged horse, flew through the storm, lost his grip, fell, and the horse turned to stone on a mountain. The prince fell into a city where, after dusk, drums and torches rang, banners flew, and a great celebration began. At dawn silence fell, grief spread, and the king’s state elephant later seized a person, lifted him onto a throne, and proclaimed him king. The new king was soon found dead in the princess’s chamber; the cycle of kings being eaten repeated, each day a new king was needed.

During this chaos the blind prince regained consciousness. The state elephant lifted him, set him on the throne, and the people shouted “The King!” The prince, now king, spent a sleepless night in a silent palace. Suddenly the princess screamed, a lightning flash cracked the walls, and a thin snake emerged from her nose, growing into a massive python. The prince drew his sword, struck the snake’s thirty‑two hoods, shattered a chandelier, and the snake split into seven pieces. At that instant the palace’s thousand steps collapsed, and from the prince’s life‑force a golden pomegranate tree with a thousand branches sprang up. The ogress‑queen, terrified, turned into a mouse and fled. The real queen, who had fainted earlier, awoke. The people rejoiced at the return of a true king and burned the snake, finding the ogress’s missive inside it.

Reading the missive, the prince realized his seven brothers had been taken by the ogress and declared he must go after them. The people lamented his departure.

The prince, now called the Pomegranate Prince, found his winged horse on a mountain; touching it revived the horse. He rode it across the dark land of Yama‑Yamuna, through a mountain of cowrie shells, a mountain of bones and a river of blood, crushing each obstacle beneath the horse’s hooves. At last he reached the gates of Pashabati’s city, where a banner proclaimed that whoever defeated her at dice would win the garland of the seven sisters.

He challenged her. The dice he used were his own, the ones that once held his mother’s life. He lost the first round and offered his winged horse in exchange for another chance. The sisters devoured the horse. The next day he brought a kitten, played again, and this time a mouse fled the dice. He invoked the dice, won the game, and demanded his horse back. The ogress returned it.

He kept playing, each victory restoring a brother and his horse. Finally he had reclaimed all seven brothers, their horses, and Pashabati’s kingdom. He demanded the dice and the mouse; the kitten killed the mouse. The dice spoke, urging him home. The eight princes mounted their eight winged horses and flew back.

Back at the palace, the queen awoke, asking where her prince was. The eight princes arrived, bowed to their mother, and the palace filled with victory drums. The people of the night‑king’s land, who had long searched for their king, found him and moved their court to his realm. The king was astonished at the sight.

At sunrise a thousand flowers bloomed on the golden pomegranate tree. Later, the palace’s palm tree, for no reason, tore from its roots, fell, and shattered into a thousand pieces. Thus the story of the dice‑bound queen, the ogress, and the eight princes came to its close.
