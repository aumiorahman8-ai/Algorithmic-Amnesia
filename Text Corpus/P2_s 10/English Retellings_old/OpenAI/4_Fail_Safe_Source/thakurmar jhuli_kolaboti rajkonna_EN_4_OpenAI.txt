**The Tale of the Seven Queens, Their Children, and the Monkey‑Owl Brothers**

Once a king ruled a great kingdom, his palace filled with elephants, horses, treasure and a bustling court of ministers, soldiers and guards. He had seven wives:

1. The Eldest Queen  
2. The Second Queen  
3. The Third Queen  
4. The Fourth Queen  
5. The Fifth Queen  
6. The Unfavored Queen  
7. The Youngest Queen  

Although the king possessed everything, his heart was empty: none of the seven queens had ever borne a child, and the whole realm lived in sorrow.

---

### The Hermit’s Gift

One day the queens went to the river to bathe. A hermit appeared, placed a tree‑root in the Eldest Queen’s hand and told her:

> “Grind this and share it among the seven queens; each will have a son as beautiful as the golden moon.”

Excited, the queens hurried home, changed, and went to the kitchen. Their chores were divided:

* Eldest – cook the rice  
* Second – cut the vegetables  
* Third – cook the curries  
* Fourth – draw water from the well  
* Fifth – assist  
* Unfavored – grind the spices (and the hermit’s root)  
* Youngest – clean the fish (she sat by the ash heap)

The Eldest Queen asked the Unfavored Queen to grind the root first. While grinding, the Unfavored Queen ate a small piece, then placed the paste in a golden bowl, covered it with a silver plate and handed it to the Eldest Queen. The paste was passed from queen to queen:

* Eldest ate some, gave it to Second.  
* Second ate some, gave it to Third.  
* Third ate some, gave it to Fifth.  
* Fifth ate the rest.  

When the Fourth Queen arrived, only a tiny residue remained; she ate that. Nothing was left for the Youngest Queen.

The Youngest, after cleaning the fish, ran to the kitchen only to find the bowl empty. She fainted. The queens began to quarrel and blame each other. The Youngest’s tears flooded the courtyard.

The Fourth Queen, feeling pity, offered the Youngest a drop of water washed from the grinding stone, saying it might still grant a “golden‑moon” son. The other queens whispered that drinking such water would only produce a “monkey‑moon” son, but the Youngest drank it anyway.

---

### Births and Fates

Ten months and ten days later five of the queens each gave birth to a son—each child shining like a golden moon. The Eldest Queen’s prophecy was fulfilled for the other two:

* The Fourth Queen gave birth to an **owl**.  
* The Youngest Queen gave birth to a **monkey**.

The kingdom celebrated the five princes—named **Hira, Manik, Moti, Shankha** and **Kanchan**—with drums and cymbals. The owl and the monkey were ignored; their mothers were reduced to a menagerie maid and a dung‑cake‑collecting servant, living in hardship.

The five princes grew up riding winged Pakshiraj horses, accompanied by soldiers and guards. The owl (named **Bhutum**) and the monkey (named **Buddhu**) lived with their lowly mothers, playing together on a bakul tree, gathering food, and sometimes walking in the southern forest.

---

### Capture and Escape

One day the princes rode to the menagerie, saw the owl and the monkey, and ordered them captured as pets. Soldiers netted the two, placed them in a cage and took them to the palace.

When the owl and monkey learned of their royal lineage, they asked the princes to bring their mothers too. The princes laughed, saying a human could not have an owl or a monkey as a child. A soldier, however, recalled the story of the Fourth and Youngest Queens and explained that the owl and monkey were indeed the king’s sons. The princes, disgusted, ordered the cage thrown away and rode off.

Bhutum and Buddhu, now aware they were princes, decided to seek their father.

---

### The Shukpankhi Princess

At the palace a Shukpankhi boat arrived, carrying a fair‑skinned maiden with dark, cloud‑like hair—**Princess Kalabati**—who spoke with a golden parrot. The queens rushed to see her, but the boat sailed away. They shouted verses, inviting her to send a son to her kingdom, promising a “flower of pearls” and a drum as a reward.

The king prepared Mayurpankhi boats for his five princes to travel to Kalabati’s land. Meanwhile, Buddhu and Bhutum entered the royal court, announced themselves as the “Monkey Prince” and the “Owl Prince,” and sat on the king’s lap and shoulder. The king wept, embraced them, and dismissed the court.

When the princes left on the Mayurpankhi boats, Buddhu and Bhutum begged to join. The queens, enraged, slapped and ridiculed them, calling them “the maid’s brat” and “the dung‑collector’s brat.” The king, silenced, could not intervene. The two brothers fled, built tiny areca‑nut‑sheath boats, and set them afloat with offerings of paddy, grass and cowries.

---

### The Princes’ Misfortune and the Brothers’ Rescue

The five princes’ boats reached the land of three old crones. The crones’ guards seized the boats, stuffing the princes, sailors and soldiers into sacks and swallowing them. Inside the crones’ bellies the princes whispered in despair until a voice called, “Hold my tail, hold my feathers.” The brothers, still afloat, pulled the princes out through the crones’ nostrils.

The rescued party set sail again, but the Mayurpankhi boats were blown into the Red River, then out to sea, where they drifted for seven days and nights. Exhausted, the princes lamented that only Buddhu and Bhutum could save them. The brothers tied their tiny boats to the prow, guided the fleet into a clear river lined with mango and jackfruit trees, and fed the princes.

Later, the princes, fearing the owl and monkey, threw Buddhu and Bhutum overboard. The brothers survived, dived into a mysterious tunnel, and emerged in a magnificent, empty palace where a hundred‑year‑old crone tossed a quilt at Buddhu. Soldiers bound him, locked him in a dark cell, and he heard the cries of his brothers.

Buddhu pretended to be dead; a maid discarded his “dead” body. He later escaped, found Princess Kalabati in the palace, lifted the pearl flower from her hair, and promised to free his brothers. The princess, bound by a vow that whoever passed the crones, crossed the Red River, escaped the crone’s quilt and retrieved her pearl would become her husband, agreed to accompany Buddhu.

Using a magical dhol‑dogor drum, Buddhu created and vanished a marketplace, seized the princess’s casket, fought a python, gathered fruit, and, by pulling a thread tied around his waist, summoned the rescued princes, sailors and boats back to the surface. The fleet set course for home.

---

### Return, Betrayal and Redemption

Back at the kingdom’s riverbank, the five princes, the queens and the king celebrated the return of the princess. The queens demanded to know to whom the princess now belonged; she answered, “I belong to whoever owns the dhol‑dogor.” When each prince denied ownership, the queens threatened to cut her to pieces. The princess invoked a one‑month vow, buying time.

Meanwhile, the two lowly mothers, having wept for years, went to drown themselves. At that moment Buddhu and Bhutum called out, “Mother!” Their mothers turned, saw their long‑lost sons, and embraced them, their tears flooding the earth.

A miraculous marketplace sprang up near the maid’s and servant’s hut; trees bore fruit on every leaf; a hundred thousand soldiers guarded the huts. News of these wonders reached the king.

Princess Kalabati announced her vow was over and asked the king to end her life if he wished. The king finally understood the whole story. He ordered the Fourth and Youngest Queens—who had been banished—to be brought back, accompanied by the sound of the dhol‑dogor. The palace drums roared, the princess, now dressed and with her vow‑offerings in her hair, went to welcome the two queens.

The five queens locked themselves in their chambers; the five princes barred their gates. With a hundred thousand soldiers and the beating dhol‑dogor, Princess Kalabati, the Fourth Queen and the Youngest Queen were escorted to the palace. Buddhu and Bhutum paid respects to the king.

The next day, a grand ceremony married **Buddhu (now Budhkumar)** to **Princess Kalabati**, and **Bhutum (now Rupkumar)** to **Princess Hirabati** of another kingdom. The five queens and five princes were sealed away behind walls of thorns and earth, never to open their doors or gates again.

Later that night the two princesses, curious about the empty beds, discovered a monkey’s skin and an owl’s feathers. Thinking them mere disguises, they burned them. The burning scent awakened the two brothers, who revealed that the hermit’s spell had turned them into an owl and a monkey; the disguise was broken. The princesses, delighted, praised the brothers.

Thus Budhkumar and Rupkumar, once a monkey and an owl, were restored to their true princely forms. The kingdom rejoiced; the king, the Fourth and Youngest Queens, Budhkumar, Rupkumar, Princess Kalabati and Princess Hirabati lived happily ever after.
