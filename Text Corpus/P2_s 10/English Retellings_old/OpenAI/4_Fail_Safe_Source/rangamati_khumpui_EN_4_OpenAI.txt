**The Tale of Naran, the Snake‑Husband, and the Seven Children**

In a remote village called Naran lived about five hundred families. The priest Darbasing Ochai was constantly busy, travelling from house to house to perform worship for every family. He had two daughters: the elder, **Khumpuiti**, who was plain‑faced, and the younger, **Khumbarti**, whose round, moon‑like face made her look like a living idol.

Each year the villagers cleared a patch of jungle for **jhum** (shifting cultivation). While everyone else worked the fields, Darbasing never helped; his duties were the rituals. The two sisters, however, went every day to the jhum to dig, sow paddy, cotton, sesame and to clear weeds. They had no hut, no shelter, and endured rain, sun, and storms while laboring.

When the harvest approached, the sisters worried how to store the grain and protect it from wild animals and birds. One day, while Khumpuiti sat under a tree lamenting their helplessness, she swore that if any creature of the forest would help them, she would take it as a husband. At that moment a huge, multicoloured snake appeared, its hood raised. When Khumpuiti tried to flee, the snake transformed into a man, gesturing that she should not be afraid and that he had come to help.

The snake set conditions:

1. **Never tell anyone** that he could take human form.  
2. **Every day Khumpuiti must give him rice** to eat; only then will he work for them.

Khumpuiti promised, and the snake slipped into a hole by the stream. The sisters kept the encounter secret.

The next day the snake emerged, again in human form, and began cutting timber, bamboo and other material to build a **gairing** (a jhum hut). The sisters helped. He also cleared a ten‑to‑fifteen‑cubit strip of jungle around the field, erected a bird‑scare device (wakhrab), stretched a rope to the hut, and made a path and steps to the bathing ghat. With these works finished, the sisters could work from the hut’s platform, pull the rope to frighten birds, and live much more comfortably.

Meanwhile, Darbasing grew anxious as Khumpuiti’s health waned. She was the family’s main laborer, yet she grew thinner each day. When the village prepared a Lakshmi puja that required a communal sacrifice of pigs, chickens and goats, Darbasing pressed his younger daughter, Khumbarti, for the cause of Khumpuiti’s decline. Khumbarti, bound by her sister’s oath, could not reveal the secret. After much pleading, she finally told her father the whole story: the snake’s appearance, the conditions, the daily feeding of rice from Khumpuiti’s share, and how this was draining her sister’s strength.

Determined to rid the family of the snake, Darbasing plotted to kill it. He asked Khumbarti to prepare a feast of aromatic rice, fish, meat and cool spring water to lure the creature. While she arranged the food on a banana leaf on the hut’s veranda, Darbasing secretly sharpened a sacrificial sword. When Khumbarti called the snake‑husband in a Tripuri chant, the snake approached, climbed the stairs, and at the moment its head reached the top, Darbasing struck, killing it. The father fled, and Khumbarti, weeping, scattered the severed pieces of the snake in places her sister frequented—the flower garden, the path to the ghat, the rice container—then left.

Returning home, Khumpuiti found only the family’s puppy. While bathing at the ghat she discovered the snake pieces, recognized them as belonging to her husband, and fashioned them into a garland which she placed on a leaf, lit incense and a lamp, and chanted a prayer to **Dungui**, the snake’s name, calling him back. As she prayed, the river rose in monstrous waves and began to engulf her. She urged her sister to flee, but Khumpuiti surrendered to the water, asking Khumbarti to remember her and promising a bright future.

Khumbarti obeyed, walking southward across a vast field until she reached a crossroads where a huge banyan tree stood. She climbed a branch and waited. That evening the king’s hunters arrived, hunting birds. They were struck by the girl’s extraordinary beauty and asked who she was. She answered that she was a human maiden, not a demon or spirit. The hunters reported her to the king, who rode to the tree, saw her, and, impressed by her beauty and composure, brought her to the palace. She was made queen, replacing the six older queens who had borne no children.

Khumbarti soon became pregnant. While the king fetched a golden cradle, the six jealous queens conspired: they forced Khumbarti to give birth at the bathing ghat, blindfolded her, and ordered the midwives to cut the newborns’ umbilical cords, throw the infants into the river, and replace them with puppies and kittens. The king, seeing only animals, was mocked by the queens and, believing the deception, relegated Khumbarti to a hut by the river where she was made to herd palace ducks, living in rags and misery.

The river, however, carried the true children—six boys and one girl—who played on its banks. Villagers repeatedly heard the sound of a cradle rocking and a lamenting chant about a mother’s suffering. Rumors spread, and the king, curious, ordered his fishermen to net the river for the mysterious children, but they could not catch them.

Years later, the children grew. Their aunt, the dead Khumpuiti, appeared to them in spirit and recounted their mother’s story. She instructed them to climb the seven branches of the banyan tree at the crossroads and await the king’s hunters. When the hunters arrived, the children answered that they would reveal their identity after meeting their mother, and that their mother was “the woman who can squeeze her breast and make seven streams of milk flow to us.” The hunters reported this to the king.

The king summoned every woman in the kingdom, ordering each to try to feed the children with milk. None succeeded—except the youngest queen, Khumbarti, still dressed in torn rags. Remembering her sister’s name, she pressed her breast and seven streams of milk poured into the children’s mouths. The children descended, embraced their mother, and the court witnessed the miracle.

The eldest child narrated the whole tragedy to the king. Filled with remorse, the king rescued Khumbarti, restored her to her rightful place as queen, and exiled the six deceitful queens to the deep jungle. He brought the seven children to the palace, where they lived happily with their mother and father.

Thus the story ends with the reunion of Khumbarti, her seven children, and the king, after a long series of hardships, betrayals, and supernatural aid.
