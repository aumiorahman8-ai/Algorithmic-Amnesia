Two sisters, Nasi and Dimsi, were walking along a deserted road when they began to hear a faint voice calling their names. They ignored it and kept walking, but the voice returned, saying, “Hey Nasi, hey Dimsi, will you marry me?” The younger sister remarked that someone seemed to be talking to them, yet when they looked around they saw no one.

After they walked a little farther the voice sounded again. This time, when they looked more closely, they spotted a monkey leaping from tree to tree, following them and playing tricks from behind the foliage. The monkey tried to tempt the sisters, promising gifts—“If you marry me, I’ll give you this, I’ll give you that, I’ll give you a water pot”—but the sisters paid it little attention and continued on their way.

Soon they reached a wide, deep canal that was impossible to jump across or wade through. Worried about how to get to the other side, they turned to the monkey and said, “Monkey, if you can get us across this canal, one of us will marry you.” The monkey was delighted. He climbed down from the tree, jumped into the water, and swam across easily. He told the sisters that they would have to cross one at a time: “First one of you gets on my back and holds on tight; I will swim you over, then I will bring the other.” The sisters agreed. With great effort the monkey carried one sister across, then the other, and they all reached the far bank. The monkey was happy, thinking his marriage prospect was now secured.

But once they were safely across, the sisters said, “Who would marry a monkey like you? We only promised to marry you to get across the canal.” The monkey’s hopes were crushed, yet he did not give up and kept following them.

Later the sisters heard the chirping of baby birds from a woodpecker’s nest high in a tree and felt a sudden desire to raise the chicks. This time they told the monkey, “Can you get us the baby birds? This time we’re telling the truth—we will marry you.” The monkey, eager to be sure, asked, “Really?” and they answered, “Really.” He climbed the tall tree, reached the nest, and stuck his hand into the hole where the chicks were.

The sisters waited below, expecting the monkey to bring the chicks down. When he did not, they asked why he hadn’t come down. He replied, “Let me count the chicks first.” A long time passed and he still did not descend. The sisters pressed again, “What happened?” He said, “I still haven’t been able to count them.” He began counting, saying, “This one is for Nasi, this one is for Dimsi,” and continued dividing the chicks between the sisters. Time dragged on; the sisters grew impatient and finally said, “Forget it! We don’t need the baby birds anymore. We’re leaving.”

Hearing this, the monkey became distressed and begged, “Wait a little longer! This one for Nasi, this one for Dimsi.” Meanwhile, his hand was trapped in the small opening of the nest. As the sisters prepared to leave, the monkey, desperate to free himself and still hoping for a marriage, pulled forcefully on his trapped hand. Despite his effort, the hand would not come out. In a final, impossible burst of strength, his arm tore from his body, remaining lodged in the hole while his body was flung down into the thick mud below. Stuck in the mud, the monkey could not get out, no matter how hard he tried.
