**The Tale of Sheyal Pondit (The Clever Scholar)**  

*Setting the scene*  
In the lush, river‑lined countryside of Bengal, where paddy fields sway like green seas and the air is scented with mango blossoms, there lived a modest village schoolmaster named **Sheyal Pondit**. “Sheyal” means “fox” in Bengali, and the nickname was given to him not because of his looks but because of his sharp wit—just as a fox is famed for its cleverness. He taught the children of the village, helped the elders settle disputes, and was known far and wide for solving problems with a twinkle in his eye and a clever turn of phrase.

---

### 1. The King’s Challenge

One scorching summer, the king of the nearby kingdom—King **Ranjit**—decided to test the wisdom of the realm’s scholars. He sent a royal messenger to every village with a proclamation:

> “Whoever can answer my three riddles shall be rewarded with a chest of gold, a fine silk robe, and a place at my court. Fail, and you shall be cast out of the kingdom.”

The messenger arrived at Sheyal Pondit’s humble mud‑thatched school. The villagers gathered, murmuring anxiously. The king’s riddles were known to be notoriously tricky, and many scholars had already tried and failed.

Sheyal Pondit smiled, stroked his white beard, and said, “Let us go to the palace together. I will answer the king’s riddles, and the village will share whatever reward we receive.”

The villagers, trusting their beloved teacher, escorted him to the royal palace.

---

### 2. The Three Riddles

In the grand hall, the king sat on a throne of polished teak, flanked by courtiers and a glittering array of jewels. He raised his scepter and announced the riddles:

1. **“What walks on four legs in the morning, two legs at noon, and three legs in the evening?”**  
2. **“I am not alive, but I grow; I have no lungs, yet I need air; I have no mouth, yet water kills me. What am I?”**  
3. **“A man builds a house of bricks. He builds it on a hill, but the house never falls. How is this possible?”**

The courtiers whispered among themselves, each trying to guess. The king’s eyes glittered with anticipation.

---

### 3. Sheyal Pondit’s Answers

**Riddle 1 – The Human Life Cycle**  
Sheyal Pondit bowed slightly and answered, “It is a human being. In the morning of life, a baby crawls on all fours; at noon, an adult walks on two legs; in the evening, an old person uses a cane, making three legs.”  

The king nodded, impressed. This was the classic answer, but many had forgotten it.

**Riddle 2 – The Fire**  
The second riddle made the courtiers frown. Sheyal Pondit raised his hand, closed his eyes, and said, “It is fire. It is not alive, yet it spreads; it needs oxygen to keep burning; and water extinguishes it.”  

A murmur of admiration rippled through the hall.

**Riddle 3 – The Brick House on a Hill**  
The third riddle puzzled even the most learned scholars. Sheyal Pondit smiled, tapped his chin, and replied, “The house is built on a hill of sand, but the bricks are glued together with mortar made of rice‑starch paste. The paste hardens when the sun dries it, so the house never falls because the hill itself is not a solid rock but a mound that the house rests upon, held together by the paste.”  

The courtiers stared, then burst into applause. The king, delighted by the unexpected twist, clapped his hands.

---

### 4. The Reward and the Lesson

True to his word, King Ranjit presented Sheyal Pondit with a chest of gleaming gold coins, a silk robe embroidered with golden threads, and an invitation to join his court as the royal advisor. The villagers cheered, but Sheyal Pondit gently placed the gold on the floor and said:

> “My reward is not the gold or the robe, but the knowledge that we have shown the kingdom that wisdom does not belong only to the palace. It lives in every mud‑brick house, in every field, and in every child’s curious mind. Let this gold be used to build a school for all the children of our village, so that they may learn to think, question, and solve riddles of their own.”

The king, moved by the teacher’s humility, agreed. The gold was used to construct a modest but sturdy schoolhouse beside the river, and the silk robe was hung in the village shrine as a reminder of the day their humble teacher outwitted a king.

---

### 5. The Enduring Legacy

From that day forward, the story of **Sheyal Pondit** traveled across Bengal. Parents would tell their children:

> “Remember the clever Sheyal Pondit, who taught us that true wisdom is shared, not hoarded, and that a sharp mind can turn any challenge into an opportunity.”

Even today, in many Bengali villages, you’ll hear an elder say, “Don’t be a *sheyal* in the sense of being sly for selfish gain; be a *sheyal* in the sense of being clever, kind, and always ready to help the community.”

Thus, the folktale lives on—an evergreen reminder that wit, humility, and generosity are the true treasures of any kingdom.
