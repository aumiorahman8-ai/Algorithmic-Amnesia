A prince and a shepherd were friends. The prince promised that when he became king, he would make his shepherd friend his minister.

The shepherd said, “Alright.”

The two lived in great happiness. The shepherd would return from grazing his cattle in the fields, and the two friends would sit under a tree, arm in arm. The shepherd would play his flute, and the prince would listen. In this way, their days passed.

The prince became king. Now a king, he had Queen Kanchanmala and a treasury full of jewels—what was some shepherd from who-knows-where to him! The prince completely forgot about his shepherd friend.

One day, the shepherd came to the palace gate and pleaded, “My friend, you never even showed me your queen.” The doorman shooed him away, shouting, “Away, away with you!” Heartbroken, the shepherd disappeared, and no one knew where he had gone.

The next day, upon waking, the king could not open his eyes. “What has happened? What has happened?” The queen saw, and everyone else saw, that the king's face was covered in needles, his body was covered in needles—even his hair had turned into needles. What could have happened! Wailing filled the royal palace.

The king could not eat, sleep, or speak. In his heart, he understood that he had broken the promise made to his shepherd friend, and this was the punishment for his sin. But he could not tell anyone what was in his mind.

The Needle-King's royal household came to a standstill. The Needle-King sat with his head bowed in sorrow, while Queen Kanchanmala, through her grief and hardship, somehow managed to run the kingdom.

One day, as the queen went to the river ghat to bathe, a girl of great beauty approached her and said, “If the queen wishes to buy a handmaiden, then I shall be one.” The queen replied, “If you can remove the needles from the Needle-King, then I will buy you.”

The handmaiden agreed.

So the queen bought the handmaiden with the bangles from her wrists.

The handmaiden said, “O Queen Mother, you look so weary. I wonder how long it has been since you have eaten or bathed properly. Your ornaments are loose, and your hair is matted. Please, take off your jewelry, and let me bathe you well with a cleansing paste.”

The queen said, “No, my dear, what is the point of bathing—let it be.”

The handmaiden would not listen. She removed the queen's ornaments and rubbed the cleansing paste on her. Then she said, “Mother, now immerse yourself in the water.”

The queen went into the neck-deep water and submerged herself. In the blink of an eye, the handmaiden put on the queen's clothes, adorned herself with the queen's jewelry, and climbing onto the ghat, she called out—

“Oh handmaiden, handmaiden, where can you be?
A radiant bride stands on the bank for all to see!
The king's queen is Kankonmala;
How much longer will you stay submerged?”

When the queen emerged from the water, she saw that the handmaiden had become the queen, and she herself had become the maid. Striking her own forehead in despair, the queen, shivering with wet hair, followed Kankonmala.

Upon reaching the palace, Kankonmala created an uproar. She said to the minister, “I am returning from my bath, why have you not prepared the elephants and horses?” She said to a courtier, “I was to return from my bath, why did you not send a palanquin?” The minister and the courtier were beheaded.

Everyone was startled. What was this? But out of fear, no one dared to say a word. Kankonmala took her place as queen, and Kanchanmala remained a handmaiden. The king knew nothing of this.

Kanchanmala would sit by the rubbish heap, cutting fish and crying—

“With the bangles from my wrist, I bought a maid,
Now she is the queen, and I a servant made.
For what sin is my golden king's kingdom laid to waste,
For what sin is the fate of Kanchanmala erased?”

The queen wept, drowning in her own tears. The king's suffering knew no bounds. Flies buzzed around him, his body and face tingled with the sting of the needles. There was no one to fan him or give him medicine.

One day, Kanchanmala went to the river ghat to wash the coarse clothes. There, she saw a person sitting under a tree with a pile of thread, saying,

“If I get a thousand needles,
Then I'll eat a watermelon!
If I had five thousand needles,
Then I'd go to the market!
If I get a hundred thousand—
Then I'll take over the kingdom!!”

Hearing this, the queen slowly approached and said, “My child, who are you that wants needles? I can give you some! But will you be able to remove them?”

Hearing this, the person silently picked up the bundle of thread and went with the queen.

On the way, Kanchanmala told the person the entire story of her sorrow. After listening, the person said, “Alright.”

Arriving at the palace, the person said to the false queen, “O Queen Mother, today is the festival of Pitha-Kuduli, and rice cakes must be distributed throughout the kingdom. I shall dye the red and blue threads, while you go and decorate the courtyard with alpana designs and arrange the ceremonial seats. And should that handmaiden not see to the other arrangements?”

The false queen, overjoyed, said, “Why certainly! What if she is a handmaiden? Let the handmaiden make rice cakes today as well.” So both the false queen and the handmaiden went to make the cakes.

But oh! The cakes the false queen made were lumpy cakes, clumsy cakes, and grassy cakes! The handmaiden, however, made Chandrapuli, Mohanbanshi, Kheer-murli, and Chandanpata—all exquisite varieties.

The person understood who was the queen and who was the handmaiden.

After finishing the cakes, the two went to paint the alpana. The false queen ground a huge mound of rice, mixed it into seven pitchers of water, dipped a coarse jute brush into it, and began to smear the entire courtyard, slapping a daub here and a daub there.

The handmaiden, however, swept a small corner of the courtyard clean, mixed a pinch of rice flour with a little water, dipped a small rag in it, and gently began to draw. She drew a lotus vine, and beside it, seven golden pitchers. She drew spires atop the pitchers, stalks of paddy on either side, and then added peacocks, dolls, and the sacred golden footprints of the goddess Lakshmi.

Then the person called out to Kankonmala, “O, you maidservant! With skills like these you call yourself a queen?”

“You serpent-maid, bought with a bangle!
She became the queen, and the queen became a handmaiden!
If you know what's good for you, speak the truth.”

Kankonmala felt a blaze of fury. She roared, “Who are you, you wretched creature? Get out of my sight!” Calling the executioner, she commanded, “Take the heads of this handmaiden and this vagabond. I will bathe in their blood—only then will I be worthy of the name Kankonmala.”

The executioner went and seized the handmaiden and the person. Then the person opened the bundle and said—

“Thread, oh thread, so full of tricks!
In the king's kingdom, a fine fix!
Thread, oh thread, now do as I say,
Bind the executioner and hold him at bay.”

A hank of thread flew out and bound the executioner hand and foot.

The person spoke again, “Thread, whose are you?”
The thread replied, “I belong to the owner of this bundle.”
The person said, “If you are truly my thread, go into Kankonmala's nose.”

Two balls of thread flew up and blocked Kankonmala's nostrils. In a panic, she rushed into her room, her voice nasal and distorted, crying, “Shut the door! Shut the door! That madman, the handmaiden has brought a madman!”

The person was then chanting a spell—

“Thread, oh thread, so fine and true, where is your home?
Go now, and thread yourselves through the needles of the Needle-King.”

Before their very eyes, a hundred thousand threads swished through the air and threaded themselves through the hundred thousand needles on the king's body.

Then the needles spoke: “The thread's soul is to stitch and sew; where shall we place our first pierce?”

The person said, “The eyes and face of the serpent-maid Kankonmala.”

The hundred thousand needles rose from the king's body and flew to stitch shut the eyes and mouth of Kankonmala. How she writhed in agony!

The king opened his eyes and saw—his shepherd friend!

The king and the shepherd embraced. The king's tears drenched the shepherd, and the shepherd's tears drenched the king.

The king said, “Friend, do not blame me for my fault. Even with a hundred lifetimes of penance, I could never find a friend like you. From this day forward, you are my minister. I suffered so much after forsaking you; I will never let you go again.”

The shepherd said, “Alright! But I seem to have lost that flute of mine; you will have to give me a new one!”

The king had a golden flute made for his shepherd friend. After that, writhing day and night from the pain of the needles, Kankonmala died. Kanchanmala's sorrow came to an end.

Then, the shepherd would perform his duties as minister all day. At night, when the sky was full of moonlight, he would take the king to the riverbank, and sitting under that same tree, he would play his golden flute. The king would listen, arm in arm with his minister-friend. The days of the king, the shepherd, and Kanchanmala passed in happiness.
