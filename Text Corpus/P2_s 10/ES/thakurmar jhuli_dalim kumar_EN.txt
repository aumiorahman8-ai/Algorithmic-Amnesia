There was a king, and the king had a queen and a prince. The queen's life was held within a pair of dice. In the royal palace's palm tree, an ogress knew this. But the ogress had never found an opportunity. One day, the king went hunting, and the prince was playing dice with five of his companions. Seeing this, the ogress, disguised as a beggar woman, went to the prince and asked for the pair of dice. What did the prince know? He carelessly gave the pair of dice to the beggar woman. With three puffs of her breath, the ogress sent the dice containing the queen's life to what kingdom, who knows? In her chambers, the queen fainted! The ogress quickly went and ate the queen, then took on the queen's form and sat there.

As always, the king returned that day; as always, the queen served him with care. Only the prince noticed that as she was serving food, a drop of saliva fell from his mother's tongue with a plop! His skin crawled! The prince ate no more; he rose silently and left. No one else knew of this.

Some years passed, and the king had seven more sons. The king celebrated with great pomp and circumstance. Only the prince noticed that the top of the palm tree was withering day by day, and no bird would sit on it. The prince remained silent.

The seven sons grew up. At the proper times, the king had all their ceremonies performed—the first rice-eating, the tonsure, the sacred thread ceremony. Then the princes said, "Now we shall go and travel the world."

The king said, "The eldest prince did not go, so how can you?" The king sent word to the eldest prince.

Upon receiving the news, the eldest prince mounted a winged horse and went to his brothers. "Why, my brothers! Had you forgotten your older brother? Come, let us now travel the world." The eight brothers, dressed in their finest, set out from the royal palace with their retinue.

From the rooftop, the ogress-queen saw them—a great danger—the prince was gone! Thrashing about, the ogress went into her room and opened a casket; inside the casket was the Sutashankha snake. The ogress said:

"Sutashankha, Sutashankha, with your conch-shell voice!
Tell me today, wherein lies the prince's life?"

The Sutashankha was as small and thin as a thread, but its voice was like a conch shell's. Raising its slender hood, the Sutashankha said in its conch-like voice:

"Wherein lies your life, O Queen, and wherein lies mine?
The Pomegranate Prince's life is in a pomegranate's seed."

The ogress said:

"Go now, O Sutashankha, riding on the wind,
Past the kingdom of Yama-Yamuna, to where Pashabati's house you'll find!
Give this missive to Pashabati,
For my seven sons, I want her seven daughters.
If my rival's son, my enemy, goes there, Sutashankha, chew him up and eat him,
So that the stepson cannot bring back the dice."

Taking the missive, the Sutashankha, riding the wind, traveled on from treetop to treetop!

The ogress, with a pomegranate in her hand, chanted another spell:

"Pakshiraj, Pakshiraj, rise and fly away,
Go to Pashabati's kingdom, and there graze and stay."

After chanting the spell, the ogress hurried to the thousand steps of the royal palace and said, "Steps, whose are you?"

The steps replied, "We belong to whoever walks on us!"

The ogress said, "Then, steps, split in two, and let this pomegranate seed remain in your crack." The pomegranate seed was sealed beneath the thousand steps forever. The ogress went and fell asleep peacefully on her milk-white bed.

At that very moment, in whatever forest the eight princes were in, the eldest prince's eyes went blind with a sharp crack. The eldest prince cried out, "Oh, brothers! A scorpion's bite—I am done for, I am done for!!"

The sun set, and a storm with rain and darkness descended. Nothing could be seen or heard in the forest. Where the eldest prince fell, where the retinue went—the seven princes' horses galloped on, ahead of the storm.

The ogress dreamed that the Sutashankha had by now crossed the land of Yama-Yamuna! But on the other side, the Sutashankha, exhausted from traveling through the trees all day, stopped as night fell. Who would travel further? It was a well-kept king's garden. The snake entered a fruit on one of the garden's trees, coiled up neatly, and fell asleep.

The princess of that kingdom ate the fruit from that tree every day. The gardener brought the fruit as usual; the princess ate the fruit as usual. Along with the fruit, the Sutashankha and the ogress's missive went into the princess's stomach.

What did the princes know of missives and such things? Flying and racing, where their winged horses went and how, no one knew. They reached a place at dawn; they all saw that their older brother was not there! They thought he must have fallen behind. Loosening the reins, the seven brothers halted their winged horses for their brother.

But no—days passed, nights passed, there was no sign of their brother! Then, one brother said, "What if the horses went ahead!"

"Right, right!!" they all cried, and spurred their winged horses forward.

The enchanted winged horses arrived directly at the city of Pashabati!

In the city of Pashabati, Pashabati herself sat waiting, adorned, in her decorated house with a banner flying at the door. Whoever could come and defeat her at dice, she, along with her six sisters, would accept as their husband. Seeing the princes, Pashabati said, "Who are you?"

The princes replied, "We are the princes of such-and-such a country, traveling the world."

Pashabati said, "No! You look like yakshas or rakshasas. Do you know my wager?"

"We do not."

"My wager is on dice. If you are demons, yakshas, or rakshasas, I will test you; if you are human, you must play.

He who wins gets the garland,
He who loses goes into our stomachs!"

The princes said, "Test us!"

Pashabati asked to see a missive. "If you are demons, yakshas, or rakshasas, you will have a missive."

The princes said, "A missive for what? We have none."
"Then play."
The princes played and lost. The seven sisters of Pashabati chopped the seven princes and their winged horses into tiny pieces and gobbled them up. Then, they resumed their beautiful forms and sat waiting. What the ogress-queen dreamed, and what fate befell her sons! Whether realization had dawned on the ogress, who knows? So be it!

Carrying the blind prince on its back, the winged horse flew through the storm and darkness, high in the air. The prince lost his grip on the reins and fell somewhere, at some point. The winged horse fell upon a mountain and turned to stone. Where the prince fell was a city! In that city, in the royal palace, after dusk, a hundred thousand kettle-drums, a hundred thousand shehnais, and all manner of drums would sound. Torches would be lit in every house, on every spire, and along every path. Banners would fly, and a great clamor of joy would erupt.

At dawn, all would be silent! Then, only weeping, screaming, wailing, beating of chests, and running about—the country would be flooded with tears, the kingdom shrouded in grief.

Again, after midday, when the king's state elephant emerged, decorated and ready, the people of the kingdom would breathe a sigh of relief and go to eat and drink. After that, all the city's people would stand in rows along the paths.

The state elephant would run and run, seize one person, and lift him onto the throne. At once, with the beating of drums and the blowing of horns, the soldiers, sentries, ministers, and courtiers would take the chosen person and make him the king of the realm. He would be married to the princess. And again, the festival of joy would begin.

The next day, only bones would be found in the princess's chamber; not a trace of the king remained! In this way, many kings came, and many kings went. But a kingdom cannot exist without a king; so, a new king was needed every day! The princess did not know, no one could understand, what ate the king!

The state elephant was running. A cry of "Make way!" went up in the city; everyone was shouting, "Clear the path, clear the path, form a line."

The prince had regained consciousness. Hearing the sound, he sat up. What path, where had he come—the prince knew nothing, understood nothing; he remained bewildered.

The elephant did not touch anyone in the line; it rushed past all the paths, came to the prince, lifted him, and set him on the throne. The people of the kingdom, shouting "The King! The King!", hailed the blind prince and made him their king.

Pomp and circumstance, coronation, splendor, justice and rituals, court, and durbar—after everything, night fell. In the king's land, all were asleep. Not a sound in the city or town, no guards at the doors—what would be the use? Everyone knew what would happen tomorrow, so the guards no longer kept watch! The princess was deep in sleep.

On that fateful night, only the prince was awake. The house and its surroundings were silent, not a peep in the whole world, not even an insect or a bird made a sound; everything seemed to be veiled in the dark slumber of that ominous night.

The lamp in the room flickered, the prince's heart pounded; there was no sound, no stir.

Suddenly, in her sleep, the princess screamed and fainted. A flash of lightning lit up the room, the walls cracked with a loud noise, and all around, tassels and leaves from the decorations came crumbling down. The prince's hair stood on end. Gripping the hilt of his sword tightly, he knelt and said, "Who is it?"

The prince could see nothing. The light in the room, the flash of lightning—the princess's body was as stiff as wood. From inside the princess's nose, a snake, as fine and thin as a hair, emerged! In moments, that hair became a thread, a rope, a thick cable, and then a gigantic python! With a roar like a conch shell, the python rose up. The palace trembled violently! The sword in his hand hummed—the prince shouted, "I know not who you are, yaksha, rakshasa, or demon! But if I am a prince, if my body is sinless, I swing my sword unseen—may this sword touch you!"

No sooner said than done—the Sutashankha had spread its thirty-two hoods, fire spitting from its venomous fangs as it rose, hissing. The prince's sword, with a great clang, shattered the room's chandelier and struck the Sutashankha's thirty-two hoods! At that instant, the prince saw—a snake! The room was a blinding maze of electricity, smoke everywhere! The prince swung his sword with a whoosh and said, "I have regained my sight!!!" The sword cut the python into seven pieces. At that very moment, in the ogress-queen's palace, the thousand steps crumbled with a thunderous crash. The prince's life-force sprouted as a golden pomegranate tree with a thousand branches. An earthquake shook the royal palace with a deep rumbling sound! In terror, the ogress turned into a mouse and fled, squeaking "chee-chee." The real queen's body, which had been in a swoon, fell to the ground again. In the kingdom and the palace, there was an outcry—"What is all this!"

The people of the night-king's kingdom had come, weeping as they did every day, and saw—Glory! Glory! The King! The king is alive today!!! The people's joy knew no bounds! They saw the seven pieces of the thousand-hooded snake lying on the floor!! "What a horror!"—everyone understood that this snake had been eating so many kings for so long!—"Burn the snake."

While burning it, they found the missive in the snake's belly! The missive was brought to the king. After reading it, the prince said, "Princess! I can stay no longer—I fear my seven brothers have been eaten by an ogress! I must go!" The people of the kingdom were disheartened—"The one king we finally found is also leaving." Everyone waited, watching the road for the king's return.

The Pomegranate Prince journeyed on and on, and on a mountain, he saw his winged horse. The moment he touched it, the winged horse came back to life and neighed, "Chih-hee-hee!" The prince said, "Pakshiraj, let us go now."

The land of Yama-Yamuna—the darkness was tangible, stones flew in the wind. The prince paid it no heed. 'What is the speed of a storm to one who rides a Pakshiraj.' The winged horse flew on like an arrow, like lightning.

Some distance away was a mountain of cowrie shells. The winged horse's hooves could not get a grip; it struggled with a clattering sound. The prince said, "Pakshiraj! Do not stop; fly on." With the speed of an arrow, a thunderbolt—all through the night, the mountain of cowries was crushed to dust under its hooves. After that came a mountain of bones. Below the mountain of bones, a river of blood rushed noisily; waves of blood, tides of blood! Skulls with bared teeth grinned "Hee! Hee!", and bones clattered against bones with a sharp, cracking sound—it was deafening. The prince said, "Pakshiraj! Have no fear, close your eyes and go on." Under its hooves, the mountain of bones was ground to dust with a clattering, crackling, crunching sound. Then the night ended. The prince saw, in the distance, the city of Pashabati. At the gate of Pashabati's city was a banner; on the banner was written:
"Whoever defeats me at dice, the seven sisters will garland!"

The prince shouted, "I will play dice!"

Sitting down to play, the prince was startled—these were his own dice! As he played, the prince lost. He saw a mouse turning the dice over. The distracted prince sat and thought.
Pashabati said, "Prince! Forfeit your stake."

"Take my winged horse; I will play again tomorrow," said the prince, and got up. The Pashabatis immediately devoured the winged horse in great mouthfuls.

The next day, the prince went into a village and brought back a kitten. He said, "Come, let us play today."

They sat down to play—today, the mouse hesitated, as if seeing something, and fled. The prince threw the dice:

"You were in this hand, O dice, and to this hand you have returned,
After all this time, on whose milk and rice have you been nurtured?"

And the dice fell in his favor. In the blink of an eye, Pashabati lost. The prince said, "Give me back my winged horse."

The ogress gave back the winged horse.

They played again. The ogress lost again. The prince said, "Give me a horse like my horse, and a prince like me." Pashabati brought a prince and a horse. The prince saw it was his brother and his brother's horse! The prince played again. He played and played until he had won back all seven brothers, their seven horses, and all of Pashabati's kingdom, realm, house, and city. Finally, he said, "What will you give now? Give me these dice and that mouse." Was Pashabati going to give up the dice just like that? Then the prince released the kitten. The kitten pounced on the mouse with a growl, tore it to shreds, and ate it. The lamp in the room went out. Where did the kingdom and realm go? With the dice in his hand, the prince saw that the seven Pashabatis had turned into seven dead earthworms!

The dice said, "Prince, prince, let's go home."
The eight princes spurred their eight winged horses and flew off with a great rush.

In the royal palace, the queen sat up. "How long have I slept! Where is my prince?"

"Where is the prince!" Victory drums beat all around. Kicking up clouds of dust, the eight princes returned to the kingdom in a line on their eight winged horses. The prince came and asked, "Where is mother, where is mother?" The eight princes surrounded the queen and bowed to her. The empty palace once again became a golden, bustling marketplace.

"Our one living king went in search of his brothers so long ago, and still has not returned." The people of the night-king's land searched and searched, and finally found him. "Our king is here!" Then the princess moved her entire court and kingdom to that place.

The king was astonished to see it all.

The next morning, a thousand flowers bloomed on the golden pomegranate tree. And at noon, the palm tree in the royal palace, for no reason at all, tore itself from its roots, fell with a thud, and shattered into a thousand pieces.
