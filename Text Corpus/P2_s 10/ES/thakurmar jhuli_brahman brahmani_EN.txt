Once there was a Brahmani, and she had a husband. The Brahmani was a pot of wisdom, the Brahmin exceedingly foolish! Consequently, all the household chores had to be done by the Brahmani. The Brahmin just sat and ate, while the Brahmani toiled to death. And what a Brahmani she was—a veritable battle-goddess! So fierce, a flick of her nose-ring could tear her nose. There was no oil in her hair, no nourishment for her body; it was a life of 'daily begging to keep body and soul together,' and on top of that, the Brahmin's incessant, irritating demands. Utterly vexed, the Brahmani would winnow the paddy—who knows if she was throwing away the husk or the grain!

Just then, the Brahmin went and said, “Brahmani, I suppose you’ll be making pitha today, won’t you?”

Tossing aside the winnowing fan, the Brahmani grabbed a broom and roared, “Yes, I was just about to make pitha! The rice is gone, the pot is empty—you haven’t the means to buy a single cowrie, yet the son of a pitha-glutton wants to eat pitha! Get out of my house!”

Her roar made the courtyard tremble, the trees shake, and the birds fly away. The Brahmin thought—
“What? Shall I endure such insults from the Brahmani?
That will not do!”
And in a rage, he went off to the forest!

He wandered through the woods, and at one point, the Brahmin met a sage. After hearing the whole story, the sage took the Brahmin to his own ashram.

At the ashram, the Brahmin began to learn reading and writing from the sage.

The old Brahmin with his wobbly ears,
How did he study with the sage?

In the morning, he would read, “Ka-cha-pa-a-a-a.”
In the afternoon, he would read, “Kha-cha-ka-a-a-a!”
By day, he would read, “Hagarang dagarang bag bag bagaram.”

At night, he would read, “Chang, chhang, krrram—ghuru-r gharam!” The night would pass with the sounds from his nose and throat!

In this way, the Brahmin learned a great deal of knowledge. Having learned and dried up, the Brahmin

thought to himself—I’ve become someone!
My fame and fortune will now spread far and wide because of my learning!
Then—I won’t have to see that venomous face of the Brahmani anymore.

Ha! Ha!
Where will I be then, and where will she be!

He was overjoyed. Why bother telling the sage anything!

He tied his satchel, manuscript, staff, and mat into a bundle,
cried “Victory to Jagadamba!” and the Brahmin set off for his homeland.

The Bhadra sun was scorching, ripening the palm fruits, cracking the earth and stone. In the evening, the Brahmin reached the edge of his own village. “Right! I will certainly go to the king’s palace, but whether she’s dead or alive, I might as well go and see that Brahmani once.” It had gotten a little late when the Brahmin stepped into his courtyard.

A sizzle, sizzle sound reached the Brahmin’s ears.

“The Brahmani is frying taler bora, I guess!”

The Brahmin crept quietly to a corner and pricked up his ears.

“How many sizzles was that? Note it down in your mind.
Four, five, seven, eight—one score and one.”

Then the sizzling stopped. As soon as the Brahmani washed her hands and feet and came outside,

The Brahmin called out in a loud voice, “Brahmani, are you home?
This time I have returned after learning profound, profound knowledge!”

Startled, the Brahmani ran out and saw—the Brahmin had arrived, his entire body covered in tilak marks! In a flurry, the Brahmani said, “Where have you been all these days?”

The Brahmin said, “Brahmani! I have returned after learning very profound knowledge, and I have come to tell you just that!”

The Brahmani said, “You madman!”
The Brahmin replied,

“You say that because you do not know, otherwise by now
You would have served me twenty-one fritters as an invitation.”

“Eh? How did you know?”

The Brahmin said, “Brahmani!
That is the very mother of knowledge! I said it by calculating;

Wherever anyone fries fritters, it is all in my mind!”

Hearing this, the Brahmani was astonished! “Oh my, oh my, is it true, is it true?” In her joy, the Brahmani

ran and told all the neighborhood folk,
“The Brahmin has returned, having learned such knowledge as has never been seen.”

The neighbors were amazed! They came and saw—

Opening many manuscripts, the Brahmin shakes his topknot vigorously
and spouts long speeches of “hang lang bang chang.”

What did it all mean? Did he even understand it? Everyone was dumbfounded.

In no time, the news spread throughout the village
that the Brahmin had returned with marvelous knowledge.

(4)

His days passed in great pomp. He read one person’s palm, another’s fortune, and from country to country, the Brahmin’s knowledge was hailed with cries of victory.

One day, Moti the washerman’s donkey went missing. Moti came to the Brahmin’s door and began to plead—
“Tell me, O divine one, what is my fate—
I have lost my only treasure, my lame little donkey.”

The Brahmin said,
“Be quiet—I will first perform the Chandi puja,
then I will come and tell you. Wait there at the door.”

Without food or water, Moti remained lying at the door. The Brahmin went inside and said, “Brahmani, what do I do now? Give me
that umbrella.”
Taking the umbrella, the Brahmin roamed the entire field under the scorching sun but could not find the donkey.

Then,
panting, his spirit crushed,
he said, “O Moti! Listen to what I tell you—
You will not find the donkey today, go.
The goddess Chandi is very angry for some reason;
Come tomorrow and take your donkey home.”

At the mention of the goddess’s anger, Moti
went away in fear.
By then the sun had set,
and night had fallen.
The Brahmin was deeply worried.

“I fear this time
alas, my whole charade will be shattered.”

Night fell; sitting with his head in his hands, the Brahmin began to think—
“All my knowledge, my satchel and manuscript, are now useless.
O Jagadamba! What have you done!—A terrible predicament!”

Mulling it over and over, the Brahmin fell asleep.
Late at night, a sound came from a corner of the outer courtyard! The Brahmin woke with a start—
“Brahmani, Brahmani, do you hear that? What was that sound?”

The Brahmani—
“Yes, yes—a thief must have come—he must be taught a lesson.”

The Brahmin, in turn, was terrified of thieves; in a tearful voice, he said, “Brahmani, then I shall hide!”

The Brahmani said, “Is that so! And you such a great scholar? Enough of showing off your scholarship. I’ll hold the lamp, you go and catch the thief.
You go around from house to house divining other people’s thieves,
but when a thief sneaks into your own home, you panic.”

What could the Brahmin do? Caught between a rock and a hard place, he curled up in fear. If he stayed inside, Ravana would kill him; if he went outside, Rama would. Wrapping his sacred thread around his ten fingers and chanting “Durga, Durga, Jagadamba,” the Brahmin went to catch the thief.
“There’s the thief, catch him!” The Brahmani gave the Brahmin a shove, pushing him forward!
“Hee-haw-hee-haw-hee-haw-hee-haw!”
“Oh my! What is that!”

Bringing the lamp closer, the Brahmani saw—
Oh my goodness, it’s not a thief at all—
There lay the huge donkey, all sprawled out!

A tempest of trembling between Brahmin and donkey, a tangled mess!
When the Brahmin stumbled and fell on top of it,
the rope around the lame donkey’s neck had become a noose.

The donkey’s throat gurgled, the Brahmin shuddered violently—
his eyes rolled back, and the Brahmin fainted dead away.
The Brahmani screamed, “Alas! What has happened, oh my!”

The neighbors came running. “What, what, what happened? Don’t be afraid!”

The Brahmani said, “No, no, it’s nothing. We were just looking at this donkey.”

Quickly, the Brahmani took the donkey and tied it to a post, and carried the Brahmin
and laid him on the bed. Oil, water, fanning, blowing—everyone came and
asked, “What, what, what has happened?”

The Brahmani said,
“It’s nothing much—Thakur was sitting in meditation,
and having brought back Moti’s donkey through his calculations, he is now resting.

To bring back a lost donkey by calculation is no small feat, you know?
That is why the great astrologer is a little exhausted.”

How astonishing! A lost donkey had appeared by the power of his mantras!
Everyone was amazed!!!

So much oil, water, and fanning! As soon as he regained consciousness, the Brahmin sat up, shouting, “Thief! Thief!” The Brahmani said,
“Thief, my foot!
Look there, Moti’s donkey is tied to the post.”

The Brahmin said, “The donkey? Where, where? Call Moti!”

The Brahmani quickly said, “Hush, hush! Moti at this hour? My dears, the night is over, you all go home now. Let the Brahmin sleep.” Everyone left. The Brahmin asked, “So, Brahmani, what did happen?”

The next day, Moti came and saw—the donkey! Moti prostrated himself at full length, eating up half the dust of the courtyard!

From then on, Moti washed the Brahmin’s clothes for free. Then Moti,
embellishing this astonishing story with even more flair,
went and spread it in all the villages.

Then,
praises for the Brahmin rang out across the land.
Eventually, this tale reached the king’s ears.

The princess’s hundred-thousand-taka necklace could not be found. Many astrologers, many scholars came and admitted defeat. All the great experts had failed. Finally, the Brahmin was summoned.

Lanky guards with their spears! The Brahmin thought,
“I was better off as a fool, who knows what fate has in store.” Like a sacrificial goat under the blade, the Brahmin went, trembling, to the royal court.

The king’s order:
“If you can find the necklace by calculation, you will get a reward,
otherwise, Brahmin, your final home will be the prison.”

To hell with offerings, to hell with worship, the Brahmin said, “Your Majesty, I need two days’ time.”
“Very well.”

The day passed as days do, and night came.

Alone in a room, the Brahmin was confined.
He drank pot after pot of water, groaning in distress.
“Alas, Mother Jagadamba, you have put me in peril,
You have ruined me completely, taken my life and fortune.
What can I do, Mother, what is the way out—
O Jagadamba! Was this your intention, alas!”

The royal gardener’s wife, Jaga, whose name was Jagadamba,
was passing by that very spot.
Jaga stopped abruptly, her heart pounding.

Without another word, “I beg you, Thakur, I beg you, Baba! I will do whatever you say, Baba—just don’t mention my name to the king!” Jaga ran and fell, clutching the Brahmin’s feet.

The Brahmin was astounded! “What is this! Who are you, who are you! What have I done? Why me?”

“No, Baba Thakur, you know everything. I will never do such a thing again.

I beg you, Baba, save me. I took the princess’s necklace out of greed. I beg you, Baba, I fall at your feet, Baba!”
Then the Brahmin understood what had happened—
By taking the name ‘Jagadamba,’ Jaga had given herself away!

Then, life returned to the Brahmin’s body. Composing himself into a great scholar, he said, “What you have done is done. You have nothing to fear. Make sure the necklace is inside a pot; take it and place it in the mud of the rear pond; see that there is no mistake.”

Shedding tears from both eyes, Jaga was saved. She immediately took the necklace and placed it in the rear pond.

The next day—looking like a pampered priest, his body covered in tilak marks like a leopard’s spots, wearing three shawls, with three more around his neck, large rudraksha beads, his topknot drooping under the weight of flowers, with his satchel, manuscript, umbrella, and staff, the Brahmin appeared in the king’s court.

He shakes his topknot, recites mantras, with so many gestures and poses,
he opens this manuscript and that, hundreds of manuscripts!

He counted until his fingers were worn, drew hundreds of chalk lines, sketched hundreds of figures on the ground. After a long time,

“Listen, listen, Your Majesty! I have found the necklace.
It is certainly in your pond.”

“Search, search!” The pond was drained, but where was the necklace? The king said,
“Ha! You have spoken with cunning words,
you have no fear for your life, what kind of Brahmin are you!”

“Mercy, Your Majesty!” The Brahmin was about to burst into tears.

“I have made no mistake, Your Majesty. Truly, this is all the work of Jagadamba!”

The king said, “Right! It could be a turn of fate. Very well, search again! But tie up the Brahmin, so he cannot escape.”

Again, search, search—
From beneath the mud, a pot was found;
breaking it, they saw the glittering necklace inside.

It was found, it was found! The Brahmin’s bonds were untied.
The king left his throne and fell at his feet—
“From this day, you are the chief scholar of my court.”

The Brahmin fainted from joy. But this time, it was not the faint from catching a thief. Be that as it may, it was a good thing. And after that? After that?
Wealth, gems, pearls, and jewels were showered upon him.
Every day the Brahmin went and sat in the king’s court.
Great scholars came from all directions,
and at the name of our scholar, they trembled with fear.
The king gave him offerings, the queen worshipped him,
Jaga supplied flowers daily,
and Thakur worshipped the ten-armed goddess.

Then—
In a three-storied palace, that same Brahmin
now lies upon a golden bed.

And—
The pantry overflows with oil, her body cannot hold all the jewelry.
The Brahmani is overjoyed—she does nothing but smile.

Now—
The Brahmin eats pitha every day—
“Ah, what a goddess of fortune!”

Hearing this, the Brahmani beams with laughter, and with a happy heart,
the devoted wife began to serve her husband joyfully.
