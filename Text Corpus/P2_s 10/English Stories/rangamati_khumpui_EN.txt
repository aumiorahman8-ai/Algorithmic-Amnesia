In the past, in a certain place, there was a large village called Naran. Crows and kites could not easily fly over that village. About five hundred families lived in that village. In the village, there was a priest named Darbasing. He was always very busy. He did not have a single day of leisure. Every day, going from house to house, he had to perform the worship of gods and goddesses for each family. Every year, the people of the neighborhood would collectively cut down some jungle and cultivate Jhum with a few measures of paddy seeds. Although everyone cultivated the Jhum together, Darbasing Ochai never went to work in the Jhum fields. Because he was busy with worship and rituals. He had two daughters. The elder daughter's name was Khumpuiti and the younger daughter's name was Khumbarti. Khumpuiti was not particularly beautiful, but her younger sister Khumbarti was very beautiful. Seeing her round face, like a full moon, she looked like a living idol.

In the months of Poush and Magh, the jungle for Jhum is cut and left to dry. In the months of Chaitra and Baishakh, that cut jungle is set on fire and burned. After clearing the burnt trees, bamboo, etc., of the Jhum, when it rains in the month of Baishakh, the soil is dug with the tip of a sharp dao, and the Jhum paddy is sown. Khumpuiti and Khumbarti went to the Jhum every day to do that work. There was no Jhum hut in the field. So they had to rest in the shade of trees; during rains and storms, they would get drenched, and drying in the sun, they had to work in the Jhum. The two sisters' hardship knew no bounds. The seedlings of Jhum paddy, cotton, sesame, and other crops gradually began to grow. The two sisters came every day and worked in the Jhum to the best of their ability. With great difficulty, the two sisters cleared the weeds for the first, second, and third time. Soon, the months of Sraban and Bhadra arrived. In a few more days, the Jhum harvest would be brought home. Abundant weeds had grown all around the Jhum. The weeds had not yet been cleared to make a path to the bathing ghat. There was no Jhum hut. All this time, they had worked relentlessly, getting drenched in the rain and drying in the sun. Now, they were worried about where they would store the paddy, cotton, and sesame after harvesting. How could the crops be protected from wild animals and birds, such as pigs, monkeys, and bears? Khumpuiti sat under a tree, thinking about all this. At one point, she said to herself, "Alas! Seeing our misery, no human will come to help us." She thought to herself, if any creature from the animal society of the jungle came forward to rescue us from our sorrow, I would accept him as my husband. Thus, in her sorrow, Khumpuiti sat under the tree and began to cry.

Just then, a huge, multi-colored snake came and stood behind her with its hood raised. The snake's skin glistened in the sunlight. When Khumpuiti saw the snake behind her and was about to flee in fear, the snake slowly took human form and gestured to her, saying, "Do not be afraid, I have come to help you."

"But listen! You must abide by a few conditions of mine. The condition is this: you can never tell anyone about this form of mine. Every day you will bring me rice to eat, and I will help you with all your work." Khumpuiti listened carefully to everything the snake said. She promised not to tell anyone about the snake taking human form and to feed the snake rice every day. Hearing Khumpuiti's words, the snake went down to the stream and entered a large hole beside it. Khumpuiti and Khumbarti returned home without telling anyone about the incident.

The next day, Khumpuiti and Khumbarti arrived at the Jhum as usual. At that time, the snake also came out of its hole, took human form, and began cutting down trees, bamboo, and other things from the jungle with the purpose of building a Gairing, or Jhum hut. He brought thatch cut from the top of the hill. The two sisters helped the snake with the work. In this way, the snake came every day and began to work with the two sisters. After the work was done, Khumpuiti would give the snake its share of the rice she had brought with her. This was because it was not possible to bring rice separately for the snake from home, for fear that people might find out about the snake's secret. In the meantime, the snake finished building the Gairing, or Jhum hut. He cleared the jungle and weeds for a distance of ten to fifteen cubits around the Jhum. And to scare away birds, he set up a Wakhrab (bird-scarer) and stretched a long rope to the Gairing, or Jhum hut. He cut a path and built steps to go to the bathing ghat. Now, when they came to the Jhum, the two sisters could enjoy great happiness. Standing on the open platform, or Chakereng, of the high stilt house on the hill, they could keep an eye on the entire Jhum. By pulling the rope, they could also scare away the birds. In this way, they spent quite some time in great happiness.

Meanwhile, Khumpuiti's father, Darbasing Ochai, became very worried seeing Khumpuiti's health decline day by day. Khumpuiti was his first daughter. Their family depended on her hard work. He himself could go to the villagers' houses every day to perform worship and eat good food, including wine and meat, but he received no other help. According to the rituals of some pujas, he could bring some raw meat home. Other than that, there was no particular benefit for him. Therefore, he became quite worried as his elder daughter grew thinner.

Towards the end of the month of Bhadra, one day, the village decided to arrange a puja for Goddess Lakshmi with the Jhum paddy, to please the goddess by sacrificing pigs, chickens, and goats, and to invite and feed all the villagers. So, a week in advance, one woman from each family was called and requested to help with the work. Khumpuiti was also told to help with that work. At home, there were only the father and her younger sister, Khumbarti. Getting such an opportunity, Khumpuiti's father, Darbasing Ochai, called his younger daughter Khumbarti with great affection and asked her a question. "Well, my dear Khumbarti! There is something I have been meaning to ask you for a long time, but never have. I have no one else; I live depending on you two sisters. Tell me the truth, my child! Why is your elder sister Khumpuiti's health declining day by day? What is the reason? She hasn't fallen ill, has she?" In this way, Darbasing Ochai kept asking his younger daughter again and again. But his younger daughter Khumbarti would not easily reveal that secret to her father. She feared that some danger might befall them if she told the truth, because the snake-man had strictly forbidden the two sisters from revealing the details of the incident. Besides, if anyone found out, the snake would be killed. For that reason, despite her father's repeated insistence, Khumbarti said nothing. Later, her father explained further to his daughter, "Look, my child, I never wish any misfortune or harm upon you. I cannot even imagine any harm coming to you. Because my future depends on the livelihood of you two sisters. My child, do not distrust me. Tell me everything."

Finally, seeing her father's pleading, Khumbarti said through her tears, "Father! My sister has forbidden me to tell anyone about this. If I tell, my sister will be angry with me." Hearing his daughter's words, the father said, "I assure you, my child, tell me the real story. No harm or misfortune will come to you."

At her father's repeated requests, Khumbarti finally said, "One day, while resting from her work in the Jhum, my sister said, 'The jungle around the Jhum has grown thick; when the paddy ripens, the birds will eat it. Since we have no Jhum hut, where will we keep the paddy after it is harvested? There is no path to the bathing ghat, no arrangement to scare birds from the paddy field, and no person will come to help us with the work. If any creature from the forest comes to help us with our work, whoever it may be, I will accept him as my husband.' As my sister was saying these things and crying, a huge snake with its hood raised was staring at her from behind. The color of the snake's chest was glistening in the sunlight. My sister suddenly turned around, saw the snake, and tried to run away in fear. Then the snake slowly took human form and gestured to my sister, saying, 'Do not be afraid, you don't have to run away. I have come to help you.' The snake also said, 'You must never tell anyone about me. If you do, great harm will come to me. You must feed me rice every day, only then can I do all your work for you.' My sister accepted all the snake's conditions and also forbade me from ever telling anyone. Since then, the snake has come to the Jhum every day and does the work. My sister feeds the snake from her own share of rice. In this way, the snake built the Gairing or Jhum hut, cleared the jungle around the Jhum, set up a Wakhrab to scare birds from the paddy field, and stretched a rope to the Gairing or Jhum hut. He cut beautiful steps to bathe in the spring water. Because she feeds the snake from her share of rice every day, my sister's health is declining day by day. She cannot bring a separate packet of rice from home for the snake, for fear that people might find out everything. She thinks that if people find out, great harm will come to the snake."

After hearing everything from his younger daughter, Darbasing had a strong desire to see the snake. He thought to himself, "There is a proverb that one should not nurture a snake with milk and bananas, as danger can strike at any time. If anyone finds out the secret, it will bring misfortune. Besides, if the snake keeps eating from my daughter's share of rice, she will surely die." After thinking about various things, Darbasing finally decided that the snake must be killed. Thinking this to himself, Darbasing Ochai said to his younger daughter, "My child, I have a great desire to see this snake who is like a husband to your sister. I will not harm him. How can I bring him here? Trust me, my child, I will never do him any harm. I will do whatever you say."

Hearing her father's sweet words, Khumbarti explained to him that to bring her sister's husband, they must first arrange for good food. Aromatic rice, good fish and meat, and cool water from the spring must be arranged. Hearing this, Darbasing Ochai quickly arranged for everything. Khumbarti slowly cooked and arranged everything. Meanwhile, Darbasing Ochai secretly sharpened his sacrificial sword well with dry sand. Khumbarti arranged all the food on a banana leaf, stood on the open platform 'Chakereng' or veranda of the stilt house, and began to call her sister's snake-husband in a rhyming chant. She called out, "Oh 'Kumui' or brother-in-law, for you, aromatic rice and good fish and meat have been cooked and packed. Come and eat these. I am waiting for you." She began to say in the Tripuri language:

Kumui yoyoi kumuiye
Kumui maichani phay
Kumui toi ni phay.
Maichu mai mutum chugoi tontitha
Tilok toi kachang dagoi tontikha
Kumui maichani phay.

As she stood on the Chakereng calling her brother-in-law snake in a mournful tone, the snake began to approach. Coming near the house, it started to climb up the stairs of the Chakereng or veranda. When its head reached the top of the stairs, it glittered in the sunlight. Just then, her father-in-law, Darbasing Ochai, ran up and cut the snake with the sword in his hand. The snake then fell writhing to the ground. After cutting the snake, Darbasing Ochai left the house. Later, his younger daughter Khumbarti, crying, picked up the severed pieces of the snake one by one. Then she began to place each piece in a different location. First, she placed a piece in the Khumpui flower garden. The next she placed on the path to the bathing ghat, another at the bathing ghat itself, a piece in the rice container, and in this way, she placed the severed pieces of the snake in all the places her elder sister Khumpuiti frequented, and then Khumbarti also left the house.

Her elder sister Khumpuiti, after finishing her work in the village, returned home to find that neither her father nor her younger sister was there. Only their little puppy was there. The puppy was just running about here and there, whimpering. Khumpuiti came into the house, took her Rinai Richa or traditional attire, and went to the ghat to bathe. On the way, thanks to the puppy, she saw a severed piece of a snake, the color of which was like her husband's skin. After bathing, when she went to the garden to pick flowers for her hair bun, she saw another severed piece there as well. When she opened the rice container to cook rice, she saw a severed piece of the snake there too. She understood that all these severed pieces belonged to her snake-husband. Then, she carefully strung the severed pieces of the snake together into a garland and wore it around her neck. She gathered a lamp and incense from the house, went to the riverbank, and cleaned a spot. Then she plastered the spot neatly, placed the garland of flesh on a leaf, and decorated it with flowers. Lighting the incense and the lamp, she began to chant in a prayerful tone. The snake's name was 'Dungui'. Addressing Dungui, she began to say, "Oh, Dungui! Come, take your unfortunate wife with you! I am calling you again and again." Which in the Tripuri language is said as:

Dungui yoyoi dungui ye
Ano tilangni phay,
Ano tilangni phay.
Nukhui dukhiya jukma phaitiyoi
Rikhondong dungui phay
Ano tilangni phay.

In this way, Khumpuiti cried and called for her snake-husband. Just then, waves as high as mountains rose in the river and began to crash upon the sandy bank. And gradually, the river's waves began to engulf and carry Khumpuiti away. At that moment, her younger sister Khumbarti ran up and said, "Sister, don't go! If you leave, who will I live with? Father has abandoned me, how will I live alone?" But her sister continued to sink with the river's waves. As she was sinking, she told her younger sister, "Don't you worry, you are exceptionally beautiful! Everyone will want to accept you as their wife. You will have nothing to worry about. To the south of the river, there is a vast field. At the far end of the field, at a crossroads, there is a huge banyan tree. You go there! Go there and sit on a branch of that banyan tree. Every evening, the king's hunters come there to hunt. Seeing your beauty, they will ask you: Are you a demoness, a monster, or a human? Or some goddess or fairy? Then you will tell them that you are not a demoness, a monster, a goddess, or a fairy. You are a human maiden. Then the king's hunters will take this news to the king. The king will come and ask you the same question. You will give the king the same answer. Pleased with your words, the king will take you to his kingdom and make you his queen. You will have no more worries. If for any reason you fall into trouble, remember me. I will come to help you. Never forget this, whether in sorrow or in happiness." As she said these words, Khumpuiti, in rhythm with the waves, merged into the river's water.

Meanwhile, following her sister's words, Khumbarti began to walk alone across the vast field south of the river. After walking a long distance alone, she reached the banyan tree at the crossroads and then climbed up and sat on a branch. In the evening, the king's hunters arrived under that banyan tree with bows and arrows to hunt birds. They were astonished by Khumbarti's exquisite beauty and stared at her. Later, a hunting party leader gathered his courage and asked, "Who are you? What is your identity? Are you a demoness or some evil spirit?" In reply, Khumbarti said, "I am not a demoness, a monster, or an evil spirit. I am a human maiden."

Then everyone, delighted by her words, went to the king and said, "Your Majesty! We have seen a maiden of such exquisite beauty! In the light of her beauty, even the banyan tree has become as bright as moonlight. We went to hunt birds near the banyan tree at the crossroads and saw that stunningly beautiful girl sitting on a branch of the tree. After we asked her everything, she replied that she is not a demoness, a monster, or an evil spirit; she is a human maiden. Come, Your Majesty! You will only believe it when you see her with your own eyes." Hearing the hunters' words, the king, along with his ministers, friends, and courtiers, rode in a palanquin to the banyan tree. Arriving there, he saw that the girl was even more beautiful and lovely than the hunters had described. The king himself asked the same question and saw that Khumbarti gave the same answer.

Then the king ordered his ministers and friends to take the stunningly beautiful girl to the palace in the palanquin. Khumbarti was taken to the royal palace. The king ordered preparations for a wedding. Upon receiving the order, the king's courtiers, ministers, friends, soldiers, and guards all began to arrange the wedding with great enthusiasm and joy. In due time, Khumbarti was married to the king with great pomp and ceremony. Previously, the king had married six other queens. But they had no children. The king did not have much of a relationship with them either. So Khumbarti now became the king's most beloved queen and began to spend her days in great joy. The king fulfilled all of Khumbarti's needs, and he was always ready to fulfill her every wish. In this way, Khumbarti's days passed in great happiness.

Thus days, months, and years passed, and Khumbarti became pregnant. The king was overjoyed. A child would be born into their house. After ten months and ten days, Khumbarti's time of delivery duly arrived. The king, extremely happy, went to buy a golden cradle, and as he left, he told the servants to inform him when the child was born. For ease of communication, a long rope was stretched from the palace to the goldsmith's shop. If the rope was pulled, the king would know that the youngest queen had given birth. He could then come back immediately.

Meanwhile, what happened! When the king went to get the golden cradle, the other six queens began to taunt Khumbarti in various ways. "What a beautiful queen! Her feet barely touch the ground for all her dazzling beauty. What a display of beauty! Now if she has a child, her influence will increase even more." Thinking this, they began to harass Khumbarti with all sorts of hurtful words. Meanwhile, Khumbarti was restless with labor pains. The other queens did not let her give birth in the palace. The other queens said in one voice that if the youngest queen gave birth in the palace, the palace would become impure. Be it in the flower garden or the rest house. Saying this, the six queens ordered that she be taken beside the bathing ghat to give birth, and that her eyes be blindfolded during the delivery. "When the babies are born, whether alive or dead, cut their umbilical cords and throw them into the river, and replace them with puppies or kittens. So that it can be shown to the king that the youngest queen has given birth to puppies and kittens." This was the queens' order! They informed all the servants.

On the orders of the six queens, the servants took the youngest queen, Khumbarti, near the bathing ghat to give birth. There she was kept blindfolded. In due time, Khumbarti gave birth to six sons and one daughter in succession. Following the instructions of the other six queens, the midwives cut the babies' umbilical cords, threw them into the river, and replaced them with puppies and kittens. And they pulled the rope to inform the king of the birth.

In the meantime, the king arrived with the golden cradle and saw that the youngest queen had given birth to several puppies and kittens. Then the six queens, laughing, said to the king, "His Majesty's beloved wife has given birth to puppies and kittens. It's a good thing; now the royal treasury will have to be spent to raise them. And not just one or two, but seven of them!" Unable to bear the queens' ridicule, the king had a hut built for the youngest queen, Khumbarti, on the riverbank and sent her to live there. And he ordered her to herd the palace ducks. From then on, Khumbarti, wearing torn Rinai and Richa or traditional attire, began to herd ducks every day from morning till evening. In this way, Khumbarti's days passed in great sorrow and hardship.

Having driven the youngest queen Khumbarti from the palace through various wicked schemes, the six queens were overjoyed. They once again became the king's favorites. They began to enjoy all the king's favors and privileges anew. Meanwhile, when the subjects went to bathe in the river, they would hear the sound of a baby's cradle rocking in the river. Along with the sound of the cradle, they would hear someone saying, "My dears, grow up quickly. Your mother is living in great hardship, herding ducks. You are the ones who must relieve your mother's sorrow and suffering. Now you sleep," and so on. Every day, people coming to bathe would get this kind of hint. In the Tripuri language, it is said in a rhyming chant:

Tutu tui tuya yang khaitidong aparak
Rangchakni wayinggo boktiye
Rupsthini buduk bai naltidong
Khuthutidoi aparak thutidoi amarak.

Namalai rajani takhum beyama
Onggoi ma tongkha, hani dukhuno
Kharai ma tongkha, daktui tordinok
Aparak daktui tordi nok
Amarak hoi hoi thutidi.

Every day, the sound of a rocking cradle could be heard like this. Sometimes downstream from the ghat, sometimes upstream. Gradually, this news reached the king's ears. From then on, everyone in the kingdom became eager to know this secret. When no one was at the ghat, the seven siblings would come up to the riverbank and run around and play. If they saw people coming, they would run and disappear into the river. This kind of scene was witnessed by one or two of the subjects. They went and told the king about it. They said, "Your Majesty! You may not believe our words. For so long, we have heard the sound of a baby's cradle while bathing in the river. This time, we have seen six beautiful little boys and one girl playing on the riverbank. Not just once, we have seen this two or three times in a row! The boys look just like princes, and the girl looks like a princess. When they see people, they jump into the river with a splash and disappear. Your Majesty! This is a wondrous event; an investigation is needed." Hearing their words, the king became deeply worried. He became eager to see the event with his own eyes. On another day, that scene was witnessed by one of the king's trusted servants. The king was quickly informed of the sight. The king came and watched from a distance, from a hiding place, and saw six beautiful boys like princes and a girl like a princess playing on the riverbank. The king was very astonished to see the scene. If anyone went near, they all jumped into the river and disappeared. To uncover this mystery, the king summoned all the fishermen of the kingdom. And he called them and ordered, "From tomorrow, you will cast your nets at the river ghat and pull them to the shore, so that no aquatic creature, not even a small stone, is missed. Everything must be caught in the nets and brought up." By the king's strict order, all the fishermen of the kingdom began to cast large nets from one end of the river to the other and pull. Then the fishermen also began to get a similar hint: if they pulled from upstream, the sound was heard downstream, and if they pulled from downstream, it was heard upstream. For seven days and seven nights, without eating, the fishermen cast their nets up and down the river, but they could not catch the children in their nets in any way. In the end, everyone returned, having failed. But that lullaby continued to ring in everyone's ears. The king fell into great worry over the matter. Even after discussing it with his courtiers, ministers, and friends, he could find no solution.

The seven siblings grew bigger day by day. One day, their aunt Khumpuiti called them and said, "Listen, my children! Your mother's name is Khumbarti. She was a very beautiful girl. I am the elder, and she is the younger. We two sisters used to go to work in the Jhum every day. But we had no Gairing or Jhum hut in our field. We had to work in the Jhum, getting drenched in the rain and scorched by the sun." From then on, she told them the entire story of what had happened, one event after another. In the end, Khumpuiti told her niece and nephews, "You will all do as I say. Tomorrow, you will go under that banyan tree, climb it, and the seven of you will sit on seven different branches. When the king's bird hunters see you, they will ask you various questions. They will ask you the same kind of questions they asked your mother. You too will give answers like your mother did. And if they ask for your identity, say, 'First, we will meet our mother, then we will give our identity.' If the king's hunters ask for your mother's identity, then you will say, 'The woman who can stand on the ground, squeeze her breast, and make seven streams of milk reach our mouths is our mother.' When you find your mother, come down from the banyan tree, touch her feet in respect, and embrace her."

After explaining everything to the seven siblings in this way, their aunt Khumpuiti seated them on seven branches of the banyan tree at the crossroads and took her leave of them. They also took leave of their aunt and, as she had instructed, climbed and sat on seven branches of the banyan tree. Just then, the king's hunters arrived and were astonished to see them. Each boy was like a full moon. And the girl was like the light of the full moon. When the bird hunters asked for their identity, they said they would only give their identity after they found their mother. Then the hunters asked for their mother's identity. In reply, they said, "The woman who can squeeze her breast and make seven streams of milk fall into the mouths of us seven siblings is the mother who gave birth to us."

Hearing the words of the seven siblings, the hunters ran again to the king and reported the details. Hearing everything from the hunters, the king, along with his courtiers, ministers, friends, and companions, came to the banyan tree, bringing the six queens with him as well. Seeing the six boys and one girl, the king was very happy and enchanted. To find their mother, the king ordered all the mothers of the kingdom, including the six queens, to squeeze their breasts and try to get milk into the mouths of the seven siblings. But no milk came from the breasts of the six queens, and although other women produced milk, it could not reach the branches of the banyan tree. In this way, the task of testing the milk of every woman was completed one by one. Now, only the king's unfortunate youngest queen was left. The king summoned the one whom he, deceived by the other six queens' conspiracy, had falsely accused of giving birth to puppies and kittens and had appointed to herd all the palace ducks, and ordered her to squeeze her breast and get milk into the mouths of the seven siblings. Receiving the king's order, Khumbarti, dressed in torn rags like a destitute woman, came under the banyan tree and, remembering her sister Khumpuiti's name, said to herself, "Sister! I lost my mother in childhood, I lost my father, and finally, I lost you too. Now, in my trial by fire, if you do not help me, I will have to suffer even more sorrow. So please free me from this ordeal, save me." Saying this, she moved the end of her sari from her chest, and when she squeezed her breast, immediately seven streams of milk gushed out like a fountain and fell into the mouths of the seven siblings. Then the seven siblings came down from the branches of the banyan tree, bowed to their mother in respect, and embraced her. The king, queens, ministers, and all the subjects present there were utterly overwhelmed by this incredible sight.

Then, the eldest of the seven siblings, the eldest brother, explained all the events concerning their mother in detail to the king. The king, now suffering from remorse, took them all to the palace in a palanquin with great love and care. For the crime of deceit and jealousy, the king exiled the six queens to the deep jungle. He restored the youngest queen, Khumbarti, to her status as queen and brought her back into the palace. The king began to live in great happiness with Khumbarti and their sons and daughter.
