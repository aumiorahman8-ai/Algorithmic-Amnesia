There once was a jackal,
His father had built a wall;
He was his son, how could he be any less?
He too had a whim!
Twirling his great big whiskers, the jackal pundit opened a grand school in the Shanti forest.

Chirping insects, buzzing insects, the young of the grasshoppers,
Tortoises, millipedes with a thousand feet,
Earthworms, scorpions, dung beetles, cockroaches, frogs,
Crabs and spiders—so many legs!
The jackal pundit's school had so very many students.

With the students studying,
and the pundit's calling,
the Shanti forest was in an uproar day and night.

Seeing and hearing all this, a crocodile thought, “Is that so! Everyone's children are getting an education, will my sons remain fools?” The crocodile took his seven sons to the jackal pundit's school and had them initiated into their studies.

The sons were now learning their ABCs. The jackal said, “Mr. Crocodile, just you watch, before seven days are up, each of your sons will become a veritable titan of knowledge, a master archer.” Overjoyed, the crocodile returned home.

The pundit taught, and every day he ate one of the crocodile's young for a snack. Six days passed in this manner.

The crocodile thought, “Tomorrow my sons will return as great scholars and master archers, I should go and see them today.” With this thought, he told the mother crocodile, “My dear, prepare the ilish-kholse chachchari, the rui-katla gorgori, the chitol-boal mormori; the boys will eat when they arrive.” Saying this, the crocodile, wearing an old piece of jute cloth, a torn fishing net for a shawl, and a fisherman's boat-hat, went to the pundit, chewing a mouthful of moss and stroking his potbelly. “Pundit sir, Pundit sir, let me see, let me see how well my sons have learned their lessons.”

Getting up quickly, the pundit said, “Come in, come in, sit down, sit down! Hey, Gube, prepare the tobacco! Hey, Phoringe, bring the snuff box!—Hey, where have the handsome crocodile boys gone?—Sit down, sit down, I will call them.”

Going inside his den, the jackal pundit held up the last remaining young one and showed him seven times. He said, “Mr. Crocodile, I have worked so hard, why leave a flaw for such a small thing? All the boys have become great scholars; if they stay just one more day, they can go home as absolute master archers.”

The crocodile said, “Alright, alright, very well, so be it.”

The foolish crocodile went away happy.

The next day, the jackal pundit finished his final snack with the last young one, dismantled his school, and—fled!

He ran and ran—the crocodile arrived to find the students weren't studying, the jackal pundit was not at home, the Shanti forest was empty. The crocodile then understood everything. Slapping his cheeks and his head, crying with eyes streaming, the crocodile said, “Alright, pundit, you just wait—
Won't you eat crabs anymore?
Won't you go to the canal anymore?
You'll come to that very canal to eat crabs—
Let's see how you
escape the clutches of this crocodile.”

The crocodile hid silently in the canal water.

A few days passed; the jackal pundit roamed the banks of the canal, but wouldn't touch a foot to the water even if his life depended on it. Finally, the pangs of hunger became too great; moreover, on the sandbank on the other side, crabs with their young were coming out in droves, showing their claws and dancing 'dhiring dhiring'; how could he bear it any longer? Forgetting everything, come what may—he jumped into the water!

And where could he go—the crocodile, with his multitude of teeth, caught the pundit's leg!

There was a tug-of-war, a great struggle—the pundit was pushed up against a thicket of reeds. At once, he broke off the tip of a reed and said with a laugh, “Ha! I didn't know you were so foolish, Mr. Crocodile! You've mistaken a stick for my leg! Let go, let go, you should have let go of the stick and grabbed my leg instead!”

The crocodile thought, “Eh? I've caught a stick?” Catch it, catch it!—Letting go of the leg, the crocodile bit down on the stick.

Freed from the reed, the pundit crossed over in three leaps! “Mr. Crocodile, Hokka Hua! I'll open my school again, do send your sons.”

Again the days passed; the crocodile couldn't even lay a finger on the jackal's tail.

Finally one day, after much plotting and planning, with his tail stretched out straight and his mouth gaping towards the sun, the crocodile lay sprawled on the canal's sandbank, perfectly still as if dead. The jackal pundit was passing that way. He saw it and thought, “Well now! The crocodile is dead! I should go and invite the missus for the feast.”

But, the pundit was suspicious. Twirling his whiskers three times and licking his chops, he said, “Alas, he was such a good fellow! What happened to him! How did he pass away! Well, what are the signs that a creature is truly dead?
The ears will twitch sharply,
The tail will slap down smartly,
Only then is it a corpse!—This fellow
isn't dead yet then!”

The crocodile thought, perhaps what he says is true. Though he had no ears to speak of, the crocodile moved his head to twitch them, and slapped his tail with a 'chat chat chat'.

Some cowherds were nearby. “Hey! There's that crocodile on the bank, the one that ate the calf the other day!”

Sickles, sticks, bricks, and stones rained down. Shouting and yelling, the group of cowherds came and set upon the crocodile.

The jackal pundit fled in three bounds. “Hokka Hua, Mr. Crocodile! Greetings! I'm off this time!”

After coming a long way, the jackal pundit entered an eggplant field.

His stomach rumbled with hunger, he happily ate the eggplants;
While eating, a thorn suddenly pierced his nose,
“Hachoo hachoo hachoo! Phnyach phnyach phnyach!”
Nothing worked, and his body was drenched in blood. Finally, weak and distressed, the jackal went to a barber's house.

“O barber, handsome among men, are you home?
Come outside for a moment, brother, with your nail-clipper.”

The barber was a very good man; he came out with his nail-clipper and said, “Who is it, brother, the jackal pundit? Goodness me, what is this! Oh dear, your nose is ruined!”

Shedding a couple of tears and sobbing, the jackal said, “That is the sorrow I weep for, brother, how can I be at peace? I have no one else to turn to but you, so I have come to you.”

The barber was very kind, his heart melted; he said, “Sit, sit, I will remove the thorn.”

One thing led to another,
The jackal's nose got cut off, while trying to get the thorn out!

“Waa, waa! Hua, hua! Kyak kyan!!! Oh you wretched, wicked, villainous barber! Look, just look what you've done. You scoundrel, first fix my nose, or else I'll show you!”

The good-natured barber, stammering in fear, said, “Brother! I've made a terrible mistake; forgive me, brother, or this poor man will die.”

The jackal said, “Alright, go. What's done is done. But give me your nail-clipper, and then I will let you go.”

What could he do? The barber gave the jackal the nail-clipper.

Receiving the nail-clipper, the jackal said, “Alright, I'll be on my way then.”

The jackal was passing in front of a potter's house; seeing him, the potter said, “Who goes there, friend? What's that in your mouth?”

The jackal said, “Is that you, brother potter? Oh, I'm just carrying a nail-clipper.”

The potter also had great need of a nail-clipper. He said, “Well, brother, let me see, what is your nail-clipper like?”

While he was examining it, the nail-clipper broke with a snap; the potter said, “Oh dear!”

Getting angry, the jackal said, “Alright, you son of a potter, that won't do! If you know what's good for you, replace my nail-clipper!”

There was no blacksmith in that village. Helpless, the potter said, “What can I do now, brother, if you don't forgive me, this poor man will die!”

The jackal said, “Then give me a pot!”

The potter gave him a pot and breathed a sigh of relief. Taking the pot, the jackal went on his way again.

A wedding groom was passing by! Everyone was moving along, setting off firecrackers and fireworks.

In the dark, who knows how? A firecracker flew out and landed in the jackal's pot. The pot shattered.

Rolling his eyes, the jackal came up and said, “Who are you, mister groom, on your grand procession? Couldn't you find any other place to set off fireworks? If you know what's good for you, give me back my pot!”

The groom was flabbergasted. Everyone said, “Forgive us, brother, forgive us, brother, or we will all die.”

The jackal said, “That won't do. Give me the bride, then you can all go wherever you please.”

What else could they do? The groom gave the bride to the jackal.

Receiving the bride, the jackal went on his way from there.

Going to a drummer's house, the jackal said, “Brother drummer, brother drummer, how many of you are there? I am getting married, I want to book all your drums. The bride will stay here with you, I am off to the priest's house.”

The drummer went to arrange for the drumming, and the jackal went to the priest's house.

The drummer's wife was sitting down to chop vegetables. The bride, dozing off, fell on the boti and was cut in two.

In fear, the drummer's wife took the two pieces of the bride and hid them in a haystack.

The jackal returned with the priest to find the bride was gone! “If you know what's good for you, drummer's wife, bring me the bride!” Frightened, the drummer's wife went inside her hut and cried, “Oh mother, what will happen now!”

The jackal said, “Never mind all that, give me the drummer's dhol and I'll let you go!”

The drummer's wife thought, “I'm saved!” She quickly brought the dhol, gave it to him, and went inside, bolting the door.

Taking the dhol, the jackal climbed a palm tree and began to play and sing—
“Tak duma dum dum!
In the eggplant field a thorn pricked me, tak duma dum dum!
Removing the thorn, I lost my nose,
Tak duma dum dum!
For my nose, I got a nail-clipper,
Tak duma dum dum!
For the nail-clipper, I got a pot, tak duma dum dum!
For the pot, I got a bride, tak duma dum dum!
For the bride, I have now got a dhol, tak duma dum dum!
Dagum dagum dug duma dum!
Dum duma dum dum!!”
