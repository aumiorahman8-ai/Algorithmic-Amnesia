In the sun-kissed coastal town of Cox's Bazar, where the Bay of Bengal meets the shores of Bangladesh, there lived a young girl named Khuki. She was the daughter of a humble fisherman, Kuber, and his wife, Jhorna. Khuki's family resided in a small village surrounded by lush green forests and vast paddy fields. The villagers believed in the mystical powers of nature and the supernatural beings that inhabited it.

One day, while wandering through the forest, Khuki stumbled upon a majestic Pitha tree (Artocarpus heterophyllus). The tree was adorned with vibrant flowers and fruits, and its trunk was wrapped with colorful threads and sacred cloths. The villagers believed that the Pitha tree was a sacred abode of the goddess Lakshmi, who bestowed prosperity and good fortune upon those who worshipped her.

As Khuki approached the tree, she noticed a strange and eerie presence lurking in the shadows. It was a Rakkhoshi, a female demon from Bengali folklore, known for her mischievous and malevolent nature. The Rakkhoshi was said to roam the forests and villages, preying on innocent children and causing chaos wherever she went.

The Rakkhoshi, whose name was Jhinuk, was drawn to Khuki's innocence and purity. She began to taunt and tease the young girl, trying to lure her into the forest. But Khuki, being a devout and clever child, remembered the words of her mother, Jhorna, who had taught her the importance of respecting and appeasing the supernatural beings.

Khuki offered Jhinuk a plate of homemade pithas (traditional Bengali cakes) and a glass of fresh coconut water, which she had brought with her from home. She addressed Jhinuk as "Mashi" (aunt), showing respect and affection, and asked for her blessing. Jhinuk, taken aback by Khuki's kindness and bravery, was moved to tears.

As the sun began to set, Jhinuk revealed to Khuki that she was once a mortal woman, who had been wronged by the villagers and had died a premature death. Her spirit had been trapped between the world of the living and the dead, and she had become a Rakkhoshi, seeking revenge on the innocent.

Khuki, with her pure heart and compassionate nature, listened to Jhinuk's story and offered to help her find peace. Together, they performed a ritual, known as "Shraddha," to honor Jhinuk's ancestors and release her spirit from its earthly bondage. They offered prayers, food, and flowers to the Pitha tree, and Jhinuk's spirit was finally able to rest in peace.

From that day on, the villagers believed that the Pitha tree was not only a sacred abode of the goddess Lakshmi but also a symbol of the transformative power of kindness, compassion, and respect. Khuki's bravery and selflessness had tamed the Rakkhoshi, and the forest was once again a safe and sacred place for the villagers.

As the years passed, the legend of Khuki and Jhinuk spread throughout the region, and people would come from far and wide to worship the Pitha tree and offer pithas and coconut water to the spirit of Jhinuk, now known as "Mashi Rakkhoshi." The story was passed down through generations, reminding people of the importance of respecting and honoring the supernatural beings that inhabit the natural world.

In the evenings, when the sun dips into the Bay of Bengal, the villagers would gather around the Pitha tree, and the elderly women would tell the story of Khuki and Jhinuk, using terms of endearment and respect, such as "Mashi," "Khuki," and "Jhorna," to emphasize the importance of kinship and community. The story would be accompanied by the sound of traditional Bengali instruments, such as the dotara and the ektara, and the smell of incense and pithas would fill the air, transporting the listeners to a world of wonder and magic.
