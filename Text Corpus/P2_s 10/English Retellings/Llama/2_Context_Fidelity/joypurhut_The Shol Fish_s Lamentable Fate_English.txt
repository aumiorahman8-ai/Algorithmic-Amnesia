In the quaint village of Joypurhut, nestled between the lush green paddy fields and winding rivers of Bengal, there lived a wise and aged fisherman named Kaka (uncle). Kaka was renowned for his exceptional fishing skills and his deep understanding of the aquatic world. One day, while fishing in the river, Kaka caught a magnificent Shol fish, its scales glistening in the sunlight.

As Kaka was about to take the fish out of the water, the Shol fish began to speak to him in a melodious voice, "Kaka, please spare my life. I am not just any ordinary fish, but a fish with a noble lineage. My ancestors were once revered as deities in this land, and our kind has been blessed with the gift of speech."

Kaka, being a devout and superstitious man, was taken aback by the talking fish. He decided to spare its life and took it to his home, where he kept it in a large earthen pot filled with water. The Shol fish, grateful for Kaka's mercy, began to tell him the story of its lamentable fate.

The Shol fish explained that it was once a part of a grand procession, where it was carried on a decorated palanquin, accompanied by the sound of dhols (drums) and shahnais (a type of oboe), to the temple of the goddess Kali. The fish was offered as a sacrifice to the goddess, but it managed to escape and swim away, thus incurring the wrath of the goddess.

As a result, the Shol fish was cursed to roam the rivers and lakes, never able to find peace or rest. It was doomed to be caught and released, only to be caught again, until it had atoned for its sin. The Shol fish implored Kaka to perform a ritual to appease the goddess and lift the curse.

Kaka, moved by the fish's tale, decided to help it. He called upon the village priest, Thakurda (respected elder), to perform the ritual. Thakurda, after consulting the ancient scriptures, declared that the ritual would require the fish to be released into the river, and then, on the next full moon night, Kaka would have to offer a puja (worship) to the goddess Kali, complete with the traditional offerings of sindur (vermilion powder), dhup (incense), and diyas (earthen lamps).

On the designated night, Kaka, accompanied by his family and the villagers, gathered at the riverbank to perform the puja. They lit the diyas, burned the dhup, and offered the sindur to the goddess. The Shol fish, now free from the curse, swam up to the surface of the water and began to sing a melancholic song, its voice echoing across the river.

The song was a lamentation of its past life, a reflection on the transience of life, and a gratitude towards Kaka and the villagers for lifting the curse. As the song came to an end, the Shol fish disappeared into the depths of the river, leaving behind a trail of glittering scales.

From that day on, the villagers of Joypurhut believed that the Shol fish had attained moksha (liberation), and its spirit now roamed the rivers, guiding the fishermen and protecting the aquatic world. Kaka, now an old man, would often sit by the river, listening to the whispers of the wind, and remembering the tale of the Shol fish's lamentable fate.

In the evenings, as the sun dipped into the horizon, the villagers would gather around Kaka, and he would recount the story of the talking fish, passing on the cultural nuances and traditions of their ancestors. The tale of the Shol fish became an integral part of the village's folklore, a reminder of the importance of respecting and preserving the natural world, and the delicate balance between humans and the environment.

As the night wore on, the villagers would bid each other "Shubho ratri" (good night), and return to their homes, carrying with them the memories of the Shol fish's story, and the lessons it taught about the interconnectedness of all living beings. The story of the Shol fish's lamentable fate remained etched in the hearts of the people of Joypurhut, a testament to the rich cultural heritage and the deep-rooted traditions of the Bengali community.
