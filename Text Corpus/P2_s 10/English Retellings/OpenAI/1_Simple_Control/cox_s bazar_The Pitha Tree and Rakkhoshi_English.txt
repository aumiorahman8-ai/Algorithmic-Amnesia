**The Pitha Tree and the Rakkhoshi**  
*(A Bengali folktale retold)*  

---

### 1. Setting the Scene  

In a quiet village tucked between the emerald fields of Bengal and the winding banks of the Ganges, there once stood a small, humble garden. In the middle of that garden grew a curious little tree, no taller than a child’s waist, whose branches were always heavy with **pithas**—the soft, fragrant rice‑cake sweets that the villagers made for festivals, weddings, and every occasion worth celebrating.

The pithas on this tree were not ordinary. They were sweet as honey, scented with the perfume of jaggery and coconut, and they never seemed to run out. Whenever a hungry child reached for one, another would appear, as if the tree knew exactly how many were needed.

Because of its generosity, the villagers called it **“Pitha‑Bari”**—the Pitha Tree—and they treated it with reverence, offering prayers and a few drops of oil each evening so that the tree might keep giving.

---

### 2. The Rakkhoshi’s Hunger  

Beyond the village, in the dense, shadowy forest, lived a **rakkhoshi**—a fearsome female demon. She was known for her insatiable appetite and for stealing food from unsuspecting travelers. Though she possessed great strength, the rakkhoshi was also clever; she could change her shape, whisper sweet lies, and disappear into the night like a wisp of smoke.

One moonless night, the rakkhoshi prowled the forest, her stomach growling louder than the rustling leaves. She had tried to feast on the crops of the village, but the villagers, warned by the old priest, had set up protective charms that kept her at bay. Frustrated and famished, she decided to search for a new source of food.

As she crept toward the village, a faint, sweet aroma drifted on the wind—**the scent of fresh pithas**. Her nose twitched, her eyes widened, and she followed the fragrance straight to the garden where the Pitha Tree stood, its branches glowing faintly in the moonlight.

---

### 3. The First Attempt  

The rakkhoshi approached the tree, her claws clicking on the soft earth. She tried to pluck a pitha, but as soon as her fingers touched the golden cake, it slipped away, landing gently on the ground and then rising back onto the branch as if nothing had happened.

“**What trickery is this?**” she snarled, shaking the tree. The branches swayed, and a cascade of pithas fell, landing in a perfect circle around her feet. She tried to eat them, but each bite turned to ash the moment it touched her tongue.

The rakkhoshi’s anger boiled over. She raised her hands and shouted a terrible curse, trying to uproot the tree. Yet the roots held fast, and the tree’s bark glowed brighter, as if warning her that she was not welcome.

---

### 4. The Villagers’ Wisdom  

The next morning, the villagers found the rakkhoshi’s footprints around the garden and the scattered ash of the pithas. The village elder, **Baba Babu**, gathered the children and the women and said:

> “The tree is a gift of the river goddess, and it will not feed those who come with greed in their hearts. The rakkhoshi’s hunger is not for food alone; it is for the emptiness inside her. We must fill that emptiness with kindness, not with more sweets.”

Baba Babu instructed the villagers to prepare a **special pitha offering**—not for themselves, but for the rakkhoshi herself. They mixed rice flour with jaggery, added a pinch of cardamom, and shaped the dough into tiny, heart‑shaped pithas. Then, they placed them on a leaf mat at the edge of the forest, beside a small lamp.

---

### 5. The Rakkhoshi’s Change of Heart  

When night fell again, the rakkhoshi returned, drawn by the same sweet scent, but this time she saw the heart‑shaped pithas and the gentle glow of the lamp. She felt a strange tug at her chest—a feeling she had not known for centuries: **curiosity mixed with a faint, unfamiliar warmth**.

She approached cautiously, sniffed a pitha, and—against all her nature—took a bite. The moment the soft rice cake melted on her tongue, a wave of **sweetness spread through her whole being**, not just in her mouth but in her heart. Memories of her own childhood, of a mother who once fed her with love, rose up like a distant song.

The rakkhoshi’s eyes filled with tears. She realized that the endless hunger she had chased for ages was not for food, but for **love and belonging**. The villagers’ offering was not a trap; it was an invitation.

She lowered her head in gratitude and whispered:

> “Thank you, kind humans. I have been a monster because I never knew how to be fed with kindness. Let me stay near your tree, not as a thief, but as a guardian.”

---

### 6. A New Guardian  

From that night onward, the rakkhoshi—now called **Rakkhoshi Rani** by the villagers—took up residence in the forest’s edge, watching over the Pitha Tree. Whenever a child was sick, she would bring a warm pitha and a gentle lullaby. When storms threatened the village, she would use her strength to shield the garden, ensuring the tree’s branches never broke.

The villagers, in turn, continued to offer a few pithas each evening, not out of fear of losing the tree’s bounty, but out of love for the guardian who had once been a monster. The Pitha Tree flourished even more, its sweet scent spreading farther than ever before, reminding everyone that **generosity, compassion, and a little sweetness can transform even the darkest heart**.

---

### 7. Moral of the Tale  

- **Kindness can tame even the fiercest of beasts.**  
- **True hunger is often for love, not just food.**  
- **Sharing what we have—no matter how small—creates bonds that protect us all.**  

And so, the story of the Pitha Tree and the Rakkhoshi lives on in Bengali households, told to children as they sit around the fire, savoring fresh pithas and dreaming of a world where even demons can become friends.
