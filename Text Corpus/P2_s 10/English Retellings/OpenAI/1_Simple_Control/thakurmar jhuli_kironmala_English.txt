**The Tale of Kironmala – A Bengali Folktale Retold**

*Setting the scene*  
In the rolling green fields of a small Bengal village, where the air always smelled of wet earth and ripe mangoes, lived a modest potter family. The potter’s wife, Lila, was known for her gentle smile; his husband, Gopal, for his steady hands on the wheel. Their only child, a bright‑eyed girl named **Kironmala**, was as luminous as the morning sun that spilled over the paddy fields. “Kiron” means “ray of light”, and “‑mala” means “garland”; together her name meant “a garland of sun‑rays”, a fitting description of the light that seemed to follow wherever she went.

---

## 1. The Girl Who Loved Stories

From the moment she could walk, Kironmala followed the village storyteller, *Baba Bairagi*, watching his fingers dance over the sand as he wove legends of gods and demons. She learned to read the world not by the strength of her arms, but by the sharpness of her mind. She could solve riddles that left the grown‑ups baffled, and she never shied away from asking questions—no matter how daring.

The village was ruled, as most places were then, by a **zamindar** (landlord) named *Raghavendra*. He was a man of wealth and power, but also of great vanity and a terrible temper. He liked to show off his riches by demanding impossible gifts from his subjects—most often as a test of their loyalty, but more often to flaunt his own superiority.

---

## 2. The Zamindar’s Challenge

One scorching summer day, the zamindar rode into the village on a golden horse, his entourage rattling like a chorus of bells. He announced a grand competition:

> “Whoever can bring me **the most beautiful garland ever made**, woven not of flowers but of something that no one can possess, shall be rewarded with a silver chest full of gold and the hand of my only daughter, *Madhuri*, in marriage.”

The crowd murmured. A garland made of *nothing*? A garland of “something that no one can possess” seemed a trick—an impossible task designed to humble the villagers.

The potters, the weavers, the fishermen—each tried to think of something priceless, but fear held them back. The zamindar’s eyes glittered with anticipation, and the villagers left the square feeling smaller than the dust under their sandals.

Kironmala, however, felt a spark of excitement. She went home, sat on the low stool by the hearth, and whispered to herself:

> “If I cannot make a garland of what no one owns, perhaps I can **borrow** that which belongs to everyone, yet belongs to no one.”

She thought of the sunrise that rose every day, the night’s cool breath, the songs of the river—things that belonged to the world, not to any single person.

---

## 3. The Clever Plan

That night, under a sky stitched with bright stars, Kironmala gathered her friends, the sparrows, the wandering fireflies, and the murmuring wind. She asked the river Brahmaputra, “If I were to gather your waters into a garland, would that be a garland of something no one can truly own?” The river gurgled with delight and replied, “Take my **dew‑drops** at dawn, for they are the finest droplets that slip through every hand.”

At first light, Kironmala slipped out of her home, her feet bare on the cool grass. She crouched in the thin mist that clung to the fields and began to **collect dew** in a handful of clean lotus leaves. As the sun rose, the droplets turned into tiny, glistening pearls.
