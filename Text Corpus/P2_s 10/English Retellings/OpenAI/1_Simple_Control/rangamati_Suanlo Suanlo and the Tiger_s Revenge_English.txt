**Suanlo Suanlo and the Tiger’s Revenge**  
*A Bengali folk tale retold*

---

### 1. The Forest of Whispers  

In a time when the Bengal jungle was still thick with the scent of mango blossoms and the rivers sang lullabies, every creature knew its place. The mighty tiger, *Bagh‑Bira*, ruled the shadows with a roar that could shake the tallest teak. The sly jackal, *Suanlo*—known to his friends as “Suanlo Suanlo” because he would always say his own name twice to announce his arrival—was the forest’s perpetual trickster, forever hunting for a quick meal or a clever escape.

The other animals respected the tiger, but they also whispered about Suanlo’s cleverness. “If there’s a knot that can be untied, Suanlo will find the end of the rope,” they’d say, half‑in awe, half‑in fear of what the jackjack—*suanlo‑suanlo*—might do next.

---

### 2. The First Encounter  

One scorching afternoon, the tiger prowled near the mango grove, his eyes fixed on the rustling underbrush where a family of small birds had taken shelter. Suanlo, ever the opportunist, crept close, hoping to snatch a fledgling for dinner.

Just as the jackal’s muzzle hovered over a trembling chick, the tiger leapt, his massive paw crushing the branch beneath. “Who dares to steal from the king of this forest?” Bagh‑Bira roared, his voice echoing through the hills.

Suanlo froze, his whiskers trembling. He thought fast. “O mighty tiger,” he said, bowing his tiny head, “I am no thief. I was only trying to warn you. A terrible fire has started on the far side of the river. It will spread, and if you do not flee now, the flames will consume the jungle and all its kings—including you!”

The tiger’s eyes narrowed. The notion of a fire was foreign; the jungle had never seen such a disaster. Yet the thought of losing his throne frightened him more than any hungry jackal.

“Very well,” Bagh‑Bira growled, “show me this fire, and I shall spare you.”

Suanlo gestured toward a dry patch of grass, quick‑lighting a small ember with a snapped twig. The flame flickered, sending a wisp of smoke into the air. The tiger, already convinced of the danger, turned and vanished into the deeper forest, leaving the jackal alone with the tiny fire.

When the flame died, Suanlo laughed a thin, delighted laugh. He had saved himself—by the skin of his teeth—and, unknowingly, had given the tiger a story to tell his own kin: “The clever jackal warned me of fire.”

---

### 3. The Tiger’s Promise  

Bagh‑Bira returned to his den that night, his mind churning. Though he had escaped the imagined blaze, he could not swallow the taste of humiliation. “The jackal has outwitted me with his words,” he muttered to the moon. “From this day forward, I shall make him pay for his deceit.”

He swore a fierce oath: *“If ever that Suanlo dares to mock me again, I will hunt him till his bones lie buried beneath the riverbank.”* The tiger’s pride had been pricked, and his heart beat with a cold, vengeful rhythm.

---

### 4. A Trick Too Far  

Weeks passed. The jungle healed, the birds sang, and Suanlo, feeling emboldened by his earlier escape, grew bolder. He loved to play tricks on the other animals, and, for a while, the forest seemed to enjoy his antics.

One dusk, Suanlo spotted a fresh set of footprints near a waterhole—large, deep, unmistakably those of the tiger. A wicked grin curled his muzzle. “Now,” he whispered, “I shall teach the tiger a lesson.”
