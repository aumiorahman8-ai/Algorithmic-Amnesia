**The Shol Fish’s Lamentable Fate**  
*(A Bengali folk tale, retold)*  

---

### 1. The Setting

In the quiet, winding backwaters of Bengal, where the water glistens like liquid silver and the mangrove trees sway with the monsoon winds, lived a humble fisherman named **Biren**. He owned a small wooden boat, a simple net, and a heart full of gratitude for the river that fed his village.  

Among the many creatures that called the river home, there was one fish that stood out: the **shol** (also known as the *shad*). The shol was a sleek, silvery fish, prized for its delicate flesh and the sweet, fragrant oil that could be extracted from its body. In the market, a single shol could fetch a price that would feed a family for weeks.

---

### 2. The Dream

One night, as the monsoon clouds gathered and the river swelled, Biren fell asleep under a thatched roof. In his dream, a radiant **river goddess**—the spirit of the water—appeared before him. She wore a crown of lotus blossoms and her voice sounded like the gentle rush of the current.

> “Biren,” she whispered, “the shol you cherish is more than a mere morsel. It carries the memory of the river itself. If you ever catch a shol, you must honor it, for its fate will echo in the waters.”

Biren awoke with a start, his heart pounding. He brushed off the dream as a fisherman’s fancy, but the goddess’s words lingered like the scent of rain on dry earth.

---

### 3. The Catch

A few days later, the river was unusually calm. Biren cast his net at the usual spot near a cluster of water lilies. The net sank, and after a short wait, he pulled it up—*the biggest shol he had ever seen* glimmered in the moonlight, its scales flashing like a thousand tiny mirrors.

The villagers gathered, eyes wide with awe. “This will bring us great wealth!” they exclaimed. Biren, however, felt a sudden pang of unease, remembering the goddess’s warning.

He lifted the shol out of the water and held it gently. The fish’s eyes, dark and soulful, seemed to look straight into his own. For a moment, the world fell silent, as if the river itself held its breath.

---

### 4. The Lament

Biren’s wife, **Mala**, urged him to bring the fish home. “We can sell it at the market, buy new tools, and store food for the winter.” Yet Biren hesitated. He placed the shol back into the net, but before he could release it, a sudden splash startled him—a **crocodile** lunged from the reeds, snapping its jaws.

In the chaos, the shol slipped from the net and fell into the crocodile’s jaws. The fish thrashed, its silver body shimmering even in the croc’s dark mouth. As the crocodile snapped shut, a soft, mournful sound rose from the water—a lament that seemed to echo the river goddess’s warning.

The villagers gasped. The shol’s lament was not a sound of pain alone; it was a sorrowful song that spoke of the river’s ancient balance, of countless generations of fish that had been taken without reverence, and of the river’s quiet plea for respect.

---

### 5. The Consequence

That night, the river rose higher than ever before. The monsoon rains poured down, and the water surged into the village, flooding fields and homes. The villagers, frightened, gathered in the highest part of the village, clutching each other and praying for safety.

In the midst of the flood, the river goddess appeared again, this time as a luminous figure standing upon the water’s surface. She spoke to the villagers:

> “The shol’s lament was a warning. When a creature of the river is taken without gratitude, the river remembers. The flood is not a punishment, but a reminder that we must live in harmony with the water that sustains us.”

She then pointed to Biren, who stood trembling but resolute. “You, Biren, have the heart to listen. Help the river heal.”

---

### 6. The Redemption

Biren, guided by the goddess’s words, gathered the villagers and led them to the highest ground. Together, they built a small **sacred platform** from bamboo and reeds, placing it at the edge of the water. On this platform they laid a simple offering: a handful of rice, a few mangoes, and a small bowl of water taken from the river’s pureest spring.

They sang a humble song of thanks, echoing the shol’s lament but turning it into a hymn of gratitude. As the song rose, the floodwaters began to recede, the river calming as if soothed by the villagers’ sincere reverence.

When the waters finally withdrew, the village was spared the worst of the damage. The fields, though wet, remained fertile, and the fish returned to the river in abundance. The shol, however, never reappeared in Biren’s net—its fate had become a legend, a reminder of the delicate bond between humans and nature.

---

### 7. The Moral

From that day forward, the villagers of the backwaters told the story of **“The Shol Fish’s Lamentable Fate”** to every child and traveler who passed through. The tale carried three simple, yet profound lessons:

1. **Respect the gifts of nature** – every creature, even a humble fish, holds a place in the larger web of life.  
2. **Listen to warnings, whether spoken or felt** – the river’s murmurs, the dreams of the night, and the sighs of the wind often carry wisdom.  
3. **Gratitude can calm even the fiercest storms** – when we honor what sustains us, the world responds with balance and protection.

And so, the river continued to flow, its waters sparkling with the memory of the shol that sang its lament, reminding all who lived along its banks to cherish and protect the living world that cradles them.

*May the story of the shol fish inspire you to listen to the quiet voices of nature, and may your own heart always beat in harmony with the rivers that sustain us.*
