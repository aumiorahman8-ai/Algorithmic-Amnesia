**The Stepmather’s Misguided Affection – A Bengali Folktale Retold**

---

*In a river‑lined village in the heart of Bengal, where the mangrove shadows stretched far into the fields, lived a widower named Bhairav. He had a small, cheerful house on the banks of the Hooghly, a son named Chandan, and a modest shop that sold tamarind paste and dried fish.*

*After his wife’s death, Bhairav married a woman from a neighboring village. The new bride, Lalita, was beautiful and sharp‑witted, and the villagers whispered that she had come to the village with “a heart full of love and a mind full of plans.” Lalita brought with her a young daughter, Nirosha, a clever girl of eleven, who had been orphaned when her own mother died.*

*Bhairav was delighted. He now had a girl to look after and a daughter‑in‑law to share his burdens. The little house grew louder, its courtyard filled with children’s laughter, and for a while the whole family seemed blessed.*

---

### The Seed of Misguided Love

Lalita, however, bore a secret longing. In the eyes of the village women, a step‑mother’s love was often expected to be “gentle” and “self‑sacrificing.” But Lalita’s affection for Nirosha was tinged with a selfish desire to possess the child entirely, as if Nirosha could fill the void left by her own lost mother. 

She began to dote on Nirosha in ways that startled the village folk:

* She let Nirosha eat the best pieces of rice from the family pot while her own son, Chandan, had to make do with the leftovers.  
* She taught Nirosha the art of weaving beautiful saris, a skill that would one day bring wealth, yet she kept the loom to herself, so that only Nirosha could practice.  
* She whispered to Nirosha that her “true” family was now only hers, urging the girl to distance herself from Chandan.

At first, Nirosha, being shy and eager to please, obeyed. She started to spend most of her time in the shaded verandah with Lalita, learning the way to pleat fabrics, the rhythm of the loom, and the secret songs Lalita sang when the moon rose. Chandan, who had grown up on modest fare and simple chores, felt the sting of neglect but kept his silence, fearing to upset his father.

---

### The Consequence of Over‑Protection

One sweltering summer, the monsoon clouds failed to appear. The fields turned brittle, the river receded, and the village’s well water turned brackish. People began to worry about the coming harvest.

Lalita, fearing for Nirosha’s future, decided that the girl must be “perfect” for the day she would be married off to a prosperous merchant from a distant town. She ordered Nirosha to practice the intricate braiding of a wedding sari every dawn and dusk, demanding absolute perfection. She would not allow the child any play, any rest, any time with her brother.

Chandan, seeing his sister’s hollow eyes, tried to intervene. “Sister,” he whispered one evening as they fetched water, “the river is low. You need to drink, to laugh, to rest. The loom will not break if you pause a little.” 

Nirosha stepped back, her cheeks flushed with tears. “I must not fail mother,” she replied, the word *ma* trembling on her tongue, though it was a step‑mother’s.

Lalita heard the whisper and, misinterpreting it as rebellion, tightened her grip. “You shall not speak to your brother,” she declared. “Your path is only the loom and my voice.”

Thus, the house fell into a silence broken only by the clack of looms and the occasional sigh of the wind. The step‑mother’s affection, once a bright lantern, became a scorching fire that held Nirosha captive.

---
