**The Tale of Kakonmala and Kanchanmala**  
*(A Bengali folktale retold for a modern ear)*  

---

### 1.  The Birth of the Two Blossoms  

In a small riverside village on the banks of the Hooghly, a poor potter named Bamdev lived with his wife, Gouri, and his aged mother. One night, as the monsoon clouds drummed the thatched roof, Gouri went into labour. The rain fell in a sudden, fierce downpour, and when the first cry of the newborn pierced the storm, the midwife declared:  

> “Two girls you have, each a flower of destiny—one called *Kakonmala* and the other *Kanchanmala*.”

The names were not random. ‘Kakon’ means “crab” and ‘Kanchan’ means “gold”. The midwife whispered that the girl who would later be called **Kakonmala** would be as humble and resilient as a crab, clinging to the mud and surviving the tide. The other, **Kanchanmala**, would be as radiant as a golden lotus, destined for a life of splendor.

The twins grew up side‑by‑side, their hair always twisted together like the twin vines of a creeper. Kakonmala was the quieter of the two, content to help her mother at the potter’s wheel, to fetch water from the river, and to care for the family’s few chickens. Kanchanmala, by contrast, loved to laugh, to sing, and to dress herself in the bright saris her mother could afford, dreaming of festivals and palace courts.

---

### 2.  The Wicked Stepmother  

When Bamdev died of fever, Gouri remarried a man named Bashir, a miserly trader whose heart was as cold as the winter night. Bashir brought with him a step‑daughter, **Rupali**, who was as beautiful as the moon but as selfish as a vulture. Bashir’s wife, **Shurupa**, saw Kakonmala’s gentle nature as a threat to her own daughter’s privileged future. She set a series of cruel tasks for the twins, hoping to break Kakonmala’s spirit.

*The first test* was to fetch water from the river before sunrise, using a cracked earthen jar. While Kakonmala walked barefoot into the moon‑lit water, a sudden whirlpool tried to drag her in. Remembering the stories her mother told of the river goddess Matsya Devi, she whispered a prayer. The water calmed, and a tiny crab—bright as a ruby—clung to the jar’s rim, guiding it safely to the shore. Bashir’s wife scoffed, but the water was brought home, and the jar, though cracked, held enough for the day.

*The second test* required the twins to spin a sack of paddy into a rope thick enough to tie a boulder to a mango tree. Kanchanmala, impatient, tried to cheat, using a spindle borrowed from a neighbour. The spindle broke, and the paddy scattered. Kakonmala, meanwhile, sat on the floor, humming the lullaby her mother used to sing, and began to spin. As she worked, a chorus of fireflies gathered, lighting the room with a golden glow. By dawn, a rope of shining threads stretched from the potter’s bench to the mango tree, strong enough to hold the boulder.

Shurupa’s envy grew. She whispered to Rupali that the sisters were a curse, that the river goddess had given them a secret: **the two sisters could exchange their fortunes, but only once, and only if they truly understood each other’s hearts**.
