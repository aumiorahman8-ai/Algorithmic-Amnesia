**The Silent Quarrow and the Polo Trap**  
*(A Bengali folktale retold for modern ears)*  

---

### 1. Setting the Scene  

In a sleepy village tucked between the emerald fields of Bengal and the winding banks of the Ganges, lived two neighbors: **Biren**, a clever but boastful potter, and **Mohan**, a quiet, diligent farmer. Their houses stood side‑by‑side, separated only by a low mud wall that was as thin as a whisper.  

Both men were respected in the village—Biren for his beautifully glazed earthenware, Mohan for his bountiful harvests. Yet, beneath the surface of their cordial greetings, a silent rivalry simmered. Each believed his own trade was the true backbone of village life, and each thought the other’s work was merely a supporting act.

---

### 2. The Spark of the Silent Quarrel  

One scorching summer afternoon, the village chief announced a grand fair in the nearby town. Artisans, musicians, and traders would gather to showcase their wares, and a handsome prize—golden bangles—would be awarded to the “Most Valuable Contribution to Village Prosperity.”

Biren, hearing the news, puffed out his chest. “My pots feed the stomachs of the whole village,” he declared to anyone who would listen. “Without my vessels, the grain cannot be stored, the water cannot be carried, and the children cannot play with their toys.”

Mohan, hearing the same news, replied in his calm, measured tone, “And without my fields, there is no grain to fill those pots. The earth gives us life; the pot merely holds it.”

The two men exchanged glances, each convinced that the other was underestimating his own worth. Yet, neither wanted to voice the growing irritation. In the Bengali tradition of *shanti* (peaceful restraint), they kept their thoughts to themselves, letting the tension linger like a thin veil of mist over the river at dawn.

---

### 3. The Polo Trap (the “Polo” is a local term for a small, shallow pit used to catch stray animals)  

In the village’s common ground, a shallow pit—known locally as a **polo trap**—had been dug years ago to catch wandering goats and stray dogs that threatened the crops. The trap was covered with a thin lattice of bamboo and straw, just enough to hide it from casual eyes but not from the keen ones.

One night, a mischievous monkey, attracted by the scent of ripe mangoes from Mohan’s orchard, slipped into the trap. The bamboo cover gave way, and the monkey tumbled into the pit, letting out a startled screech that echoed through the still night.

The next morning, the village awoke to the sight of the monkey perched on the edge of the pit, its tail curled around a bundle of Biren’s freshly fired pots that had been left to dry nearby. The monkey, frightened, clutched the pots as if they were a lifeline.

Villagers gathered, laughing at the absurd sight: a wild animal holding delicate pottery while perched over a muddy hole. The chief, ever the wise one, saw an opportunity.

---

### 4. The Unspoken Lesson  

The chief called Biren and Mohan to the center of the crowd. “Look,” he said, “the monkey has taken both your gifts—my pots and my fields—into its grasp. Yet it cannot use either. It is trapped because it tried to hold onto both at once.”

He pointed to the polo trap. “The pit was made to protect the crops, but when we forget its purpose and let it become a snare for ourselves, we suffer. Likewise, when we let pride become a trap, we lose sight of what truly sustains us.”

Biren felt a flush of shame. He realized that his pride had turned his beautiful pots into a symbol of vanity rather than service. Mohan, too, sensed that his quiet confidence had become a silent barrier, preventing him from acknowledging the value of others.

The chief continued, “True prosperity comes when the pot and the field work together—when the pot holds the grain that the field yields, and the field is irrigated by the water carried in the pot. Neither can thrive alone, and neither should be used to boast over the other.”

The villagers murmured in agreement, and the monkey, sensing the change in atmosphere, dropped the pots and scampered away, freeing itself from the trap.

---

### 5. The Resolution  

From that day forward, Biren and Mohan spoke openly. Biren began to craft special storage jars for Mohan’s grain, inscribing them with verses that praised the farmer’s hard work. Mohan, in turn, set aside a portion of his harvest each season to give to Biren’s family, ensuring the potter never went hungry.

When the fair arrived, the two men presented a joint exhibit: a beautifully painted pot filled with golden rice, placed atop a small mound of fresh soil. The villagers marveled at the harmony of the display, and the chief awarded them the golden bangles—not for individual brilliance, but for the lesson they embodied.

---

### 6. Moral of the Tale  

- **Pride can become a trap**—just as the polo trap was meant to protect the fields, unchecked ego can imprison us.  
- **Collaboration outshines competition**—the pot and the field are interdependent; so are people.  
- **Silent quarrels fester**—unspoken resentment grows like weeds; speaking the truth with humility clears the path.  

---

### 7. A Modern Take  

If you ever find yourself in a “silent quarrel” with a colleague, neighbor, or friend, remember the potter and the farmer. Ask yourself: *What am I trying to hold onto that isn’t meant for me?* And consider how a simple conversation—like opening the bamboo cover of a polo trap—might let both parties step out into the light together.

---

**Thus ends the retelling of “The Silent Quarrel and the Polo Trap,” a story that reminds us that humility, communication, and mutual respect are the true foundations of any thriving community.**
