**The Golden Stick and the Silver Stick  
(Shonar Kathi – Rupor Kathi)**  

*An old Bengali folktale, told by hearth‑side and by the river‑bank, that carries a timeless warning against greed and the restless desire for ever‑more.*

---

### 1. The setting

In a small, mud‑bound village clinging to the banks of the Hooghly, the monsoons came each year with a roar, turning the paddy fields into a shimmering sea of green. Most of the villagers were simple folk—farmers, potters, and thatchers—who lived hand‑to‑hand with the land and the river.  

Among them lived **Benu**, a thin, work‑worn thatcher, and his wife **Mori**, who wove mats from reeds to supplement the meagre earnings. Their little wattle‑and‑daub hut was always cold in winter, and the rice‑stores were often empty. Yet Benu never lost his easy smile; he believed that honesty and hard work were enough to keep a family afloat.

---

### 2. The storm and the discovery

One night, a fierce cyclone tore across the delta. Lightning cracked the sky, and the river rose like a rolling wall of water. The next morning, the storm had passed, leaving behind a strange stillness and a forest littered with fallen trees, broken branches, and—most peculiarly—two shining sticks lying side by side on a cleared patch of earth.

One stick gleamed with the warm luster of pure gold; it was smooth, light, and seemed to hum with a faint, melodic ringing when the wind brushed it. The other was a brilliant silver, cooler to the touch, its surface rippling like moonlight on water. Villagers whispered that the two sticks had been gifts of **Balur Chandi**, the river‑deity who sometimes dropped her ornaments into the world below when she was angry or delighted.

When Benu came to collect the fallen timber for his trade, his eyes fell upon the sticks. He knelt, lifted the golden one, and felt a strange warmth spread through his palm, as if the stick were alive. He lifted the silver one as well; a gentle chill ran up his arm, like a sigh of the river itself.

---

### 3. The powers revealed

That evening, Benu, unable to keep the secret to himself, showed the sticks to Mori. She laughed at first, calling it a “fairy‑tale” thing, but when Benu pointed the golden stick at a splintered piece of wood and said, “Turn into gold,” the wood glowed, melted, and re‑formed into a shining golden plank. Likewise, when he tapped a dull iron nail with the silver stick and whispered, “Be silver,” the nail turned silvery, fine as a moonbeam.

Word spread like wildfire. The village headman, **Kakababu**, came, then the village priest, then a gaggle of curious neighbours, each asking to try the sticks. Benu, wary but generous, allowed them a single touch each. Every villager who tried left with a small miracle—a copper pot turned to brass, a bamboo basket turned to polished silver, a handful of rice becoming golden grains.

The villagers cheered. “With these sticks, we shall never be hungry again!” they cried. Benu, however, felt a knot tighten in his chest. The sticks were beautiful, but their power seemed to be bounded by something he could not see.

---

### 4. Greed takes root

Soon the excitement turned to desire. Kakababu, the headman, demanded that the sticks be handed over to the village council so that *all* could benefit. “If the whole village shares the wealth, the name of our village will be sung from Dhaka to Darjeeling,” he declared.

Mori, seeing her husband’s growing anxiety, pleaded, “Benu, we have little. Let us keep them, just a little, to help our children.”  

But the storm had already awakened a deeper hunger. The gold‑stick’s warm glow seemed to whisper promises of riches beyond imagination, while the silver‑stick sang of status, of silvery bangles and beautiful saris. Benu found himself thinking not of his own needs but of how the sticks could *fix* all the village’s miseries.

One moonless night, he slipped out of the hut, took the two sticks, and walked to the market town of **Barasat**. There, under lantern light, he sold a single golden plank for a small fortune, and a handful of silver nails for a tidy sum. He returned home clutching sacks of coins, his eyes bright as the sticks he had left behind.

For a few weeks, everything changed. He bought a new roof for the hut, a loom for Mori, a small cart for his trade, and even a handful of fancy cloths for the children of the village. The men of the village, seeing his sudden rise, started calling him *Lakh‑Mohan*—the man with a lakh (one hundred thousand) of rupees.

But the more Benu spent, the more the gold‑stick and silver‑stick whispered. “Why keep just a few? Take more, share more, become a king!” they seemed to say, their voices mingling with the river’s rush.

---

### 5. The curse of the sticks

One sweltering afternoon, while Benu was bragging at the village square about his new possessions, a sudden, sharp wind whipped through the market. The golden stick, left on a low stool for a brief moment, slipped, fell to the ground, and shattered into a thousand tiny fragments—each piece just a glint of metal, useless on its own.

Panic surged through the crowd. “The sticks! The sticks are broken!” cried the children. Benu’s heart pounded; he scrambled to collect the shards, but they melted instantly in the heat, turning to dust.

The silver stick, too, felt the tremor. As Benu reached for it, the river’s current, which had been gentle that day, surged unexpectedly, flooding the market lane. The stick slipped from his grasp and was carried downstream, disappearing into the watery depths, never to be seen again.

The villagers fell silent. Their faces, once bright with hope, turned ashen. Benu, now stripped of his magical tools, felt a cold emptiness settle in his chest.

---

### 6. The lesson

That night, Benu sat alone on the roof, the sky a tapestry of stars. He heard the distant rush of the Hooghly and remembered the stories his grandmother used to tell—how the river goddess would test mortals with gifts, rewarding humility and punishing arrogance.

He realized that in his rush to grasp wealth, he had ignored the delicate balance that the sticks represented: **gold** for *desire* and **silver** for *appearance*. When those desires are unchecked, they crack and dissolve like the fragile metal fragments.

The next morning, Benu went to the riverbank, knelt, and offered a simple prayer to Balur Chandi: “Forgive my greed, O river‑goddess. Let my family learn again the value of honest work and the joy of enough.”  

The river’s surface rippled, and a soft voice seemed to echo back, “True wealth lives in a content heart, not in glittering sticks.”

From that day forward, Benu returned to his thatching trade, humble yet wiser. The village, having seen the rise and fall of his fortunes, remembered the tale. Mothers would tell their children, “If you ever find a golden or silver stick, keep it safe, use it sparingly, and never let greed drown your good sense.”

---

### 7. The folk version today

In modern Bengali gatherings, the story is still told with a bit of drama:

> *“Shonar Kathi, Rupor Kathi—dui jodi alo,  
>   Ekta age rakhle shanti, ekta bhule dori.  
>   Baṛo swarther ghure phele, shesh hobe shona o rupor dhooli.”*  

(“The golden stick and the silver stick—two beams of light.  
If kept with restraint they bring peace; if turned by big greed they end as dust of gold and silver.”)

The tale serves as a cheerful reminder that **miraculous gifts are only as good as the hearts that wield them**, and that the greatest treasure is a soul free from endless craving.
