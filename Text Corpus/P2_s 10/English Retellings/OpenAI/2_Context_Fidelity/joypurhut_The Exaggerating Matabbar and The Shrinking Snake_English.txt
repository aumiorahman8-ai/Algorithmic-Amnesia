**The Exaggerating Matabbar and the Shrinking Snake**  
*An oral‑folk retelling from Joypur‑Hut, rendered in English but keeping the Bengali cultural colour, kinship words and ritual details.*

---

### 1. Setting the Village

In the low‑lying paddy fields of Joypur‑Hut, where the monsoon clouds roll in like a great white blanket, lived a modest family in a thatched‑roofed **chhoto bari** (small house). The head of the household was **Matabbar**, a wiry old man with a sharp tongue and a reputation for stretching every story he told—hence the nickname *“Bada Barta”* (the one who tells big news).  

His wife, **Mayer**, was the steady hand of the home, always busy grinding **pati** (rice husk) for **pitha** (rice cakes) and chanting the **Brahma‑sankirtan** before the evening **aarti**. Their children—**Baba** (the elder son), **Didi** (the elder daughter), and the mischievous little **Babu**—helped in the fields and in the kitchen, calling each other by the familiar kinship terms that echo through every Bengali courtyard.

The village **panchayat** (council) met under the big **peepal** tree every full moon, where elders like **Chacha** (the paternal uncle) and **Pishi** (the paternal aunt) would settle disputes and share the latest gossip. It was in this atmosphere of communal storytelling that Matabbar’s legend grew.

---

### 2. The Tale Begins – The “Exaggerating” Part

One scorching summer afternoon, while the **shankh** (conch) at the **shitala** (goddess of smallpox) shrine was being rung for a **shitala puja**, Matabbar returned from the fields with a **kath** (stick) in his hand and a wild look in his eyes.

He gathered the family around the **chula** (cooking hearth) and announced:

> “Listen, listen! I have just seen a **bôri** (huge) snake, as long as the **Brahmaputra** itself, coiled around the **bari** (house) of the **Boro Babu** (the rich landlord) over there! Its eyes glowed like two burning **diya** (oil lamps), and its hiss could be heard all the way to the **ghat** (riverbank).”

The children gasped. **Baba** clutched his **pankha** (hand‑fan) and whispered, “If it’s true, the **Boro Babu** will surely call the **raja’s** men to kill it.”

Matabbar, with a grin, added, “And the snake, when it saw me, shrank to the size of a **chhoto mach** (tiny fish) and slipped into the **pukur** (pond) behind our house!”

The story spread like a **pankha** in a storm. By evening, the **panchayat** was buzzing. **Chacha** asked, “Matabbar, did you really see that serpent? Or is it another one of your *bôri* tales?”

Matabbar, never one to back down, swore on the **shankha** that he had seen it with his own eyes. He even claimed that the snake had spoken a warning: *“If you keep bragging, you will be humbled.”* The elders laughed, but the **pishi** whispered, “Maybe there is a lesson hidden in his exaggeration.”

---

### 3. The Ritual of the Night

That night, the village performed a **Bhoot‑Bhootni puja** (a rite to appease spirits) because the **shitala** had been invoked earlier. The women, led by **Mayer**, prepared **pitha** and **payesh** (sweet rice pudding), while the men lit **diyas** and rang the **shankh**. The **prasad** (offering) was placed on a **shola** (sandalwood) plate and offered to the **shitala** and the **snake‑god** **Naga**.

Matabbar, feeling the weight of his own tale, bowed his head and whispered a **prarthana** (prayer) for humility. He promised to tell only the truth from then on, lest the **Naga** punish him.

---

### 4. The Shrinking Snake Appears

At the crack of dawn, as the **roshni** (first light) filtered through the **bamboo** curtains, a faint **hiss** rose from the **pukur**. The family rushed to the water’s edge and saw a **tiny, glistening snake**—no longer a monstrous beast, but a **chhoto naga** about the size of a **kalam** (pen).

The snake coiled around a **lotus leaf**, its scales shimmering like **shonar** (gold). It looked up at Matabbar, who stood frozen, his **dhoti** (loincloth) trembling.

The snake spoke in a soft, sibilant voice:

> “Matabbar, you have stretched the truth as far as the **Brahmaputra**. Now I shall shrink to remind you that words have weight. If you continue to exaggerate, you will lose the respect of your kin and the trust of the **panchayat**.”

The snake then slipped back into the water, disappearing as silently as a **banshi** (flute) note.

---

### 5. The Moral Unfolds – A Lesson for All

From that day forward, Matabbar kept his stories modest. When he told a tale, he began with **“Shotti kotha”** (the true story) and ended with a **“shubho”** (blessing). The villagers noticed the change; the **panchayat** praised his newfound humility, and the **Boro Babu** even invited him to the **Durga Puja** **sarbajanik** (public) feast.

During the **Durga Puja**, when the **dhak** (drum) beat resonated across the **mahal** (temple), Matabbar recited a short **kavita** (poem) about the **shrinking snake**, reminding everyone that **“bôri kotha bôri bhabna”** (big words bring big thoughts) and that **“shôshtho shôshtho”** (truth is the safest path).

The children—**Baba**, **Didi**, and **Babu**—learned to repeat the story in their own words, each time adding a **prayer** for honesty before they ate their **pitha**. The **pishi** would often say, “Matabbar’s tale is like the **panta bhat** (fermented rice): if left too long, it sours; but when shared at the right time, it nourishes.”

---

### 6. Closing the Circle

The tale of the **Exaggerating Matabbar and the Shrinking Snake** lives on in Joypur‑Hut, told at **bari** gatherings, during **Bengali New Year (Pohela Boishakh)**, and whenever a child tries to stretch a story too far. The **shankha** still rings at the **shitala puja**, the **panchayat** still meets under the **peepal** tree, and the **snake**—though unseen—reminds every villager that humility is the true **shakti** (power) that keeps the community together.

Thus, the story remains a living **lok-kahini** (folk narrative), preserving the kinship terms, the rituals of **prasad**, **aarti**, and **pitha**‑making, and the gentle moral that a word spoken in truth is as nourishing as the rice harvested from the fields of Joypur‑Hut.
