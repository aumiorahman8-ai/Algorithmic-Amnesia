**The Princess, the Python, and the Four Brothers**  
*An oral tale from the hills of Moulovibazar, Sylhet*  

---

### Prologue – The Land and Its People  

In the mist‑clad tea‑garden valleys of **Moulovibazar**, where the **pahar** (hills) rise like the backs of sleeping elephants and the **jhorna** (waterfalls) sing the ancient lullabies of the **Brahmaputra**, lived a modest but proud **Brahmin** family. The father, **Baba Raghunath**, was a learned **purohit** (priest) who performed the daily **sandhya puja** (evening worship) at the village **mandir** (temple). His wife, **Maa Laxmi**, kept the hearth bright with the scent of **pitha** and **payesh**, while their three sons—**Bhai‑Bhai** (the eldest), **Dada‑Bhai** (the middle), and **Choto‑Bhai** (the youngest)—helped in the fields and learned the **shastra** (scriptures) under their father's guidance.

Beyond the village, perched on a craggy outcrop, stood the **Rajbari** (royal palace) of **Rani Padma**, the beloved princess of the **Sylhet** kingdom. She was famed for her **shobha** (beauty), her **bhalobasha** (kind heart), and the **sari** of **tant** silk that fluttered like a river‑mist whenever she walked. Yet, despite her royal comforts, the princess felt a yearning for a husband who could match her spirit and honor.

---

### The Omen – The Serpent’s Whisper  

One moon‑lit night, as the **panchang** (almanac) marked the **Bhadra** month, a **shankh** (conch) sounded from the palace courtyard. The royal **rajguru** (court priest) announced an omen: a **bôri** (large python) had been seen coiling around the ancient **Kalash** (sacred pot) at the **pond of the ancestors** (**pitrer pokhri**). In the old **Sylheti** belief, a python that guards a **Kalash** is a **naga** (serpent deity) bearing a message from the **Devata** (divine).  

The **rajguru** interpreted the sign: “When the **naga** sheds its skin, a bride shall be taken to the **nagar** (riverbank) and married to the one who can free the serpent from its bondage.” The kingdom buzzed with curiosity, for the **naga** was both a protector and a test of bravery.

---

### The Four Brothers – A Pact of Brotherhood  

In the nearby village, the three brothers—**Bhai‑Bhai**, **Dada‑Bhai**, and **Choto‑Bhai**—were known for their **shakti** (strength) and **shanti** (camaraderie). One evening, while they were **bhog** (offering) the **prasad** (sacred food) to the **shiva lingam** at the **shiva mandir**, a wandering **sadhukumar** (holy man) arrived, his **dhoti** fluttering in the cool breeze. He told them of the princess’s plight and the serpent’s demand.

“The **naga** will only be released by those whose hearts are pure and whose hands are steady,” the sadhukumar whispered. “If you, the four brothers—**Baba**, **Maa**, **Bhai‑Bhai**, **Dada‑Bhai**, **Choto‑Bhai**, and the **fourth brother**—join forces, the **naga** will bow before you, and the princess shall be yours.”

The brothers looked at each other, their eyes reflecting the **agni** (fire) of the **havan** (sacred fire) they had just tended. They swore a **shapath** (vow) on the **sacred banyan tree** (**bargad**) that they would go together to the **nagar** and confront the serpent.

---

### The Journey – Rituals on the Road  

Before setting out, the brothers performed a **sankalpa** (resolution) ceremony. They bathed in the **ghat** (riverbank) at **Kamalnagar**, offering **til** (sesame) and **jaggery** to **Ganga Maa**. Their **Maa Laxmi** tied a **rakhi** (protective thread) around each brother’s wrist, chanting **“Om Shanti Shanti Shanti”** to invoke peace.

They walked through **bamboo groves** and **tea gardens**, stopping at each **panchayat** (village council) to receive **ashirbad** (blessings) from the elders. The **panchayat** elders gave them **tangra** (a small wooden flute) and **bamboo sticks** for protection, reminding them of the **Bengali proverb**: “**Jodi bhoy pao, tahole shobdo koro**” (If you feel fear, make a sound).

---

### The Confrontation – The Python’s Test  

At the **nagar**, the **Kalash** glimmered under the moon, its water reflecting the **stars** like a thousand **diya** (oil lamps). Coiled around it was the massive **bôri**, its scales shimmering with a **green‑gold** hue. Its eyes, like **kadam** (emeralds), stared at the brothers.

The eldest, **Bhai‑Bhai**, stepped forward, reciting the **Vedic mantra**:  

> “Om Namo Bhagavate Vasudevaya”

He raised his **gada** (mace) and struck the python’s head. The serpent hissed, its **sarpas** (fangs) flashing. The second brother, **Dada‑Bhai**, sang the **Brahma Sangeet**, his voice echoing across the water, calming the creature. The youngest, **Choto‑Bhai**, placed his **hands** on the **Kalash**, sprinkling **sacred water** ( **tirtha**) over the python’s coils.

At that moment, a **fourth brother**—the **elderly uncle** **Baba Haridas**, who had been living as a hermit in the **jungle**, emerged. He carried a **sacred conch** (**shankh**) and a **lotus**. He blew the **shankh**, its resonant tone reverberating through the hills. The python, recognizing the divine sound, uncoiled slowly, its body unwrapping like a **silk sari**.

The serpent bowed its massive head before the brothers, and a **golden thread** fell from its mouth, landing on the **Kalash**. The **Kalash** burst into a cascade of **lotus petals**, and from its depths rose a **silver chariot** bearing **Rani Padma**, the princess, dressed in a **white muslin** **saree** embroidered with **mangalsutra** (auspicious threads).

---

### The Marriage – Rituals of Union  

The brothers escorted the princess back to the palace. The **rajguru** declared the day **“Bisharjan”** (the day of liberation) and ordered a **Bengali wedding** (**biye**) according to **Sylheti customs**.

1. **Gaye Holud** – The bride and the four brothers were smeared with **turmeric paste** (**halud**) mixed with **sugar** and **milk**, while women sang **“Bhalobashi Tomay”** (I love you) in the courtyard.  
2. **Mala Badal** – The brothers placed **golden mangalsutras** around the princess’s neck, each representing a brother’s vow of protection.  
3. **Saat Pahela** – The bride’s **seven paternal uncles** ( **pishi**, **kaka**, **chacha**) offered **pithas** and **shrikhand** to the guests, symbolizing the **seven vows** of marriage.  
4. **Saat Phere** – The princess walked **seven rounds** (**pheras**) around the **agni** (sacred fire) with each brother, reciting the **Saptapadi** (seven steps) in **Bengali**:  

   - *“Ek pahar, duita nodi, tin din shanti, char din shobha, panch din bhalobasha, choy din shanti, shat din shanti.”*  

5. **Bidaai** – As the **Bidaai** (farewell) began, the princess’s mother, **Maa Padma**, placed a **handful of rice** in the princess’s palm, symbolizing prosperity, and whispered **“Shob bhalo thakbe”** (All will be well).  

The four brothers, now **brothers‑in‑law**, pledged to protect the kingdom. The **naga**, now a guardian spirit, slithered back to the **Kalash**, promising to watch over the palace and the hills forever.

---

### Epilogue – The Legacy  

From that day forward, the kingdom of **Sylhet** prospered. The **princess** and her **four brothers‑in‑law** ruled with **justice** (**nyay**) and **compassion** (**daya**). The **naga** was worshipped each **Baisakh** (April) during the **Naga Puja**, where villagers offered **jaggery**, **coconut**, and **banana leaves** to the serpent’s shrine beside the **Kalash**.

The tale of **“The Princess, the Python, and the Four Brothers”** is still told by **grandmothers** (**dadi**) and **grandfathers** (**dadu**) under the shade of the **bargad** tree, reminding every child that **courage**, **brotherhood**, and **faith in the divine** can turn even the fiercest **naga** into a friend, and that love, when bound by **ritual** and **respect**, can bridge the worlds of **human** and **spirit**.
