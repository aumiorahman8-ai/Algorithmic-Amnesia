**Kakonmala‑Kanchonmala**  
*From* **Thākurmār Jhūlī** (Grandmother’s Folktales)

---

### 1. The Village and the Two Sisters  

In a small **pāṛa** of Bengal, beside the sluggish **bāi** (river) and a mango‑grove that swayed with the monsoon wind, lived a potter named **Bābujī** and his wife **Maitrī**. Their household was modest, but its heart was the pair of daughters they loved as their own **śiśu**.

The elder girl was named **Kākonmālā** – “the one whose hair shone like the black‑tinted, glossy pearls of a night‑flower”. She was gentle, ever‑obedient, and liked to help the village **sādhus** (clerics) by lighting the **diyā** (earthen lamp) in the **mandir** (shrine) each evening. The younger sister, **Kānchanmālā**, whose name meant “the one whose beauty sparkled like a crystal”, was proud, quick to anger, and liked to hear the **ẏālī** (folk songs) that praised valor and riches.

The two girls were as different as the **kānchā** (crystal) and the **kāk** (crow‑feather). Yet both were beloved by their parents, and the village folk called them **dui chotto bāṛi‑pāṭi** (the two little blossoms).

---

### 2. The Arrival of the Stepmother  

One bitter **sharat** (autumn) evening, a shrill knocking sounded on the **darwājā**. A woman named **Mashi Rukhmā**, a distant relative of the potter, entered with a child, **Bābā‑bāi** (her own son). She claimed that her husband, a merchant, had perished in the **bādal’s** (storm) flood, and that she sought shelter.

Bābujī, being generous, welcomed them. Yet Mashi‑Rukhmā was a woman of **bhīṣa** (sharp temper). She brought with her a **kāṭha** (tinder‑box) of jealousy, and soon began to compare the two sisters. “*Kākonmālā, you are a *piyāra* (dear) but you spend your days sweeping the courtyard, while *Kānchanmālā* is the one who brings glory to the house with her voice.*”

Mashi‑Rukhmā's son, **Bābā**, also entered the fold. Though a **sāl‑bābā** (young boy), he was drawn to the kind‑hearted sister. He would often fetch water from the **kunā**, saying, “*Kakon, you are the *shrestha* (best) of all*.”  

The stepmother seized the chance. One night, while the household slept on **ṭhākur** (cot) mats, she whispered to *Kānchanmālā*:

> “*If you want a **bṛhat** (grand) marriage, you must rid the house of this *Kākon* who steals the attention of all.*”

Thus, the seeds of envy took root.

---

### 3. The First Test – The **Pabni** (Feast of the New Moon)

The village’s **naba‑chandrā** (new‑moon) festival was approaching. It was custom to prepare a **pabni**, a grand feast where families placed **pūjaṇa** (offering) of **panch‑phoron** (five spices) and **pāla‑pāni** (sweet water) on a white **chādar** (cloth) before the deity **Lakshmi**. The host family would also arrange a **khela‑danga** (playground) for the children and perform **aarti** with a **sārka** (coconut).

Mashi‑Rukhmā commanded Kānchanmālā to *cook* the **pabni**, while she ordered Kākonmālā to *clean* the **dhūli** (platform) and arrange the **diyas**. The girl with the crystal name, eager to prove herself, *took great pride* in the delicate **chhā** (rice pudding) she prepared, adding *kesar* (saffron) bought from the market.
