**The Cunning Mother’s Strategic Upbringing of Her Son and Stepson**  
* A folktale from the villages around Mymensingh, Bengal *

---

### Opening Scene – The Village Strip

In a low‑lying hamlet on the northern bank of the **Brahmaputra**, thatched‑roofed **chau‑chalas** stand shoulder‑to‑shoulder like old friends. The smell of **panta bhāt** and frying **pithas** wafts from the open courtyards every evening, while the river’s susurrus carries the soft murmur of women chanting **‘aṅkurey‑āri’** (the invocation to the field’s newborn sprouts).

Here lives **Mala**, a wiry, dark‑eyed woman of fifteen winters, married to **Bhairav**, a modest **kshatriya** farmer. From a previous marriage, Mala already has a son, **Sarbajit** (the **jutho**— “younger son”), but Bhairav has a grown‑up son from his first wife, **Ratan**—the **sôtpôtr** (step‑son). The household therefore contains two boys: the **ninety‑day‑old infant** Sarbajit, and the **strength‑built lad** Ratan, who is already ten seasons old.

The village wise‑woman, **Dadi Maa**, warns Mala in the first few **panchāyāt** (community meetings): *“Two sons, two fates. You must treat them both with the same *bhalobasha* (love), lest the karmic scales tilt against you.”* But Mala, who learned early the art of **parikalpana** (strategic planning) from watching the market women bargain for **bhat** and **dal**, has other designs.

---

### The First Test – The Naming Ceremony (*Naamkaran*)

When Sarbajit reaches the **nava‑tola** (the seventh day after birth), the family gathers for the **Naamkaran** at the village **pukur** (pond). The priest recites **‘Namokar Mantra’**, sprinkling **tamarind water** over the infant, and the village women chant the **‘Boddho Bickho’** (an auspicious rhyme).  

Mala seizes the moment. She asks the priest to give Sarbajit the name **“Bikram”**, after the great warrior‑king, hoping the name will bring him **shōk** (courage) and a **bṛhat** (large) share of the family's future.  

In the same ceremony, Ratan—who already bears his name—receives a **new title**: **“Ratan‑Babu”**, a gentle way of distancing him from the ancestral **‘Ratan’** of old. The subtle re‑naming is the first of Mala’s **camouflaged moves**.

---

### The Second Test – The Harvest Offering (*Borsha‑Bhrata*)

The monsoon arrives early, turning the paddy fields into a glistening sea of **kancha** (young shoots). In the **Borsha‑Bhrata** ritual, each household must present a **handful of rice seedlings** to the village deity **Mansa** to ensure a plentiful harvest.

Mala privately **withdraws a sack of the finest seed‑rice** from the granary and places it in the **box** earmarked for Ratan’s share. When the village elders, led by **Baba Gopal**, announce the distribution, Ratan receives the **golden‑hued** seed‑rice, while Sarbajit's portion is the ordinary brown grain.

The village watches, whispers: *“The mother favours her own.”* But Mala rubs her hands together and mutters, “If Ratan looks upon the gods with a *prasāda* of gold, he will become **bhālo‑bhābo** (a respectable man) yet forever indebted to my guidance.”

---

### The Third Test – The Wrestling Match (*Dhingule*)

When the summer **Bhaiyā** (festival of brotherhood) arrives, the village arranges a **dhingule** (traditional wrestling on a mud pit). It is customary for the **jutho** to **watch** his elder brother **learn humility** by competing first.  

Mala arranges for Ratan to **coach** Sarbajit secretly, even though Sarbajit is still a toddler. She tells Ratan, “If you teach him the *khela* (game), he will see you as a mentor, and you will earn *shraddha* (respect) from the whole panchayat.”
