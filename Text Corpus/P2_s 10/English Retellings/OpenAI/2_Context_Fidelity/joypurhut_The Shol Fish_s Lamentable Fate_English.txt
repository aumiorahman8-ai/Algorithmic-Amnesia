**The Shol Fish’s Lamentable Fate – A Joypurhut Folktale (Retold)**  

*In the river‑bank village of Joypurhut, where the water of the Padma sighs through the reeds and the evenings are scented with the incense of *paan* and *jhalmuri*, an old tale is still whispered by grandmothers to their grandchildren. It is the story of the shol fish (the great carp) and the human hearts that bound its destiny.*

---

### 1. The Setting and the Characters  

- **Shol Mach** – the mighty carp, revered as a *pitr‑devi* of the river, whose silver scales glitter like moon‑light on the water.  
- **Baba Ratan** – the village elder, a *bujh‑baba* (wise father) who leads the *panchayat* (village council).  
- **Mayer Didi** – the matriarch, a *ma‑didi* who keeps the household hearth alive with *panta bhat* and *pitha*.  
- **Kumari Lila** – the young maiden, called *boudi* by the boys, whose voice is said to be as sweet as the night‑cicada’s song.  
- **Pishi Rani** – the aunt who knows every *tala* (ritual) and *mantra* of the river’s spirits.  

The story unfolds during the **Makar Sankranti** festival, when the villagers perform the *bhog* (offering) to the river, scattering *til* (sesame) and *jaggery* sweets on the banks, and the *prasad* (blessed food) is shared among all.

---

### 2. The Lure of the River  

Every year, as the sun rose over the *dal* (paddy fields), the villagers would gather at the river’s edge. The *panchayat* would chant the ancient *mantra*:

> “*Jal‑raja‑shakti, amader shanti dako, shol‑mach, tumi amader shurjo‑bhor.*”

The shol fish, hearing the reverent words, would surface, its massive body rippling the water like a living moon. Children would squeal, “*Dekho, shol mach!*” and the elders would smile, remembering the old belief that the shol carries the *soul‑of‑the‑river* and can grant a single wish to a pure heart.

---

### 3. The Wish and the Deception  

One year, a drought threatened the fields. The rice paddies cracked, and the *ghor* (granary) was empty. The village gathered for a *prayer* (*prarthana*) at the riverbank. Lila, the maiden, stepped forward and whispered her wish to the shol:

> “*Shol mach, amader krishi‑bari bhalo hok, amader bhat‑pata phire asuk.*”

The shol, moved by her sincerity, promised to help. But a cunning *boka* (trickster) from a neighboring hamlet, *Bikram*, overheard the prayer. He had learned a *tantra* from a wandering *sadhak* (ascetic) that the shol’s power could be harnessed if a human offered a *blood‑sacrifice* (*rakt‑bali*) at the exact moment the fish leapt.

Bikram approached Pishi Rani, who warned him:

> “*Rakt‑bali shudhu shanti‑purnā, na hole shol‑mach bhoy‑pāte bhanga hobe.*”

Ignoring the warning, Bikram prepared a small knife and a goat’s heart, intending to spill it into the river as the shol rose.

---

### 4. The Ritual Gone Awry  

On the night of **Makar Sankranti**, the villagers lit *diyas* (oil lamps) along the riverbank, sang *bhajans* (devotional songs), and performed the *panchayat*’s *shradha* (ritual of remembrance) for ancestors. The shol, sensing the collective devotion, leapt high, its body flashing silver under the moon.

Bikram, hidden behind a *bamboo* thicket, thrust the goat’s heart into the water just as the shol broke the surface. The river roared, the *til* offerings scattered, and a sudden chill swept over the crowd. The shol’s eyes, once calm, turned a deep, mournful black.

A sudden gust blew out the *diyas*. The *panchayat* fell silent. Baba Ratan shouted:

> “*Shol mach! Ki holo?*”

The shol, now wounded by the impurity of blood, let out a mournful cry that echoed through the reeds. Its lament was not just for itself, but for the broken covenant between humans and the river.

---

### 5. The Lamentable Fate  

The shol sank slowly, its massive body dragging the river’s spirit with it. As it disappeared, the water turned a murky brown, and the fields beyond the village began to wither even more. The villagers realized that the *rakt‑bali* had polluted the river’s sanctity.

Mayer Didi, with tears staining her *saree*, gathered the children and said:

> “*Shol mach amader shanti chilo, kintu amra tar shanti bhul kore bhanga diyechi. Ekhon amader dorkar shanti‑purnā bhog, na ki rakt‑bali.*”

She instructed the *panchayat* to perform a *purification* (*shuddhi*) ceremony. The villagers collected *sacred water* (*tirtha*) from the *Ganga* and sprinkled it over the river, chanting:

> “*Jal‑raja‑shakti, amader bhul‑bichar shuddho koro, shol‑mach, tumi phire asho.*”

The *prasad* of *pitha* and *payesh* (sweet rice pudding) was offered again, this time with pure intention. The river’s surface began to calm, and a faint glimmer of silver reappeared—signs that the shol’s spirit was forgiving.

---

### 6. The Moral and the Legacy  

From that day forward, Joypurhut observed a new ritual each year on **Makar Sankranti**:

1. **No blood‑sacrifice** – only *til* and *jaggery* are offered, symbolizing sweetness and purity.  
2. **The Lament Song** – a *bhajan* called *“Shol Mach-er Kotha”* is sung, recounting the fish’s lament and the villagers’ repentance.  
3. **The River Walk** – every family walks along the riverbank, holding hands, and whispers a *shanti‑mantra* for the water’s health.  

The story of the shol fish’s lamentable fate is told by *dada* (grandfather) and *dadi* (grandmother) to the *nana* (maternal uncle) and *nani* (maternal aunt), reminding each generation that **respect for nature and sincerity in worship are the true sources of prosperity**.  

Thus, the shol fish lives on—not as a physical creature, but as a living lesson etched into the heart of Joypurhut, its silver scales reflected in every ripple of the Padma, and its lament heard in the hush of the night when the river sings.
