**The Pitha Tree and Rakkhoshi**  
*An oral folktale from the coastal villages of Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh*  

---

### 1. Setting the Scene  

In the low‑lying hamlet of **Bashkhali**, where the salty breeze of the Bay of Bengal mingles with the scent of **pitha** (steamed rice cakes) being prepared for the **Makar Sankranti** festival, lived a modest family of **four generations**:  

* **Baba** (grandfather) – a retired fisherman who still mended his old net each evening.  
* **Ma** (grandmother) – the keeper of the hearth, the one who taught the children the art of shaping **bhapa pitha**, **patishapta**, and **chitai pitha**.  
* **Dadi** (the mother) – a diligent weaver of **nakshi kantha**, who sang lullabies in the **Bengali dialect of the Chittagong Hill Tracts**.  
* **Baba‑Mohan** (the son) – a lanky boy of twelve, known for his curiosity and his habit of wandering to the **paddy fields** after school.  

The family’s modest house stood under a **large banyan tree** whose roots seemed to hold the earth itself. Near the edge of the village, on a small rise, grew a **single, twisted pitha‑tree**—a mythic tree that, according to the elders, bore edible pithas instead of fruit. The tree was said to sprout **golden‑hued rice‑flour buds** every full moon, and the villagers would gather to harvest them for the upcoming **Poush Sankranti** celebrations.

---

### 2. The Legend of the Pitha Tree  

According to the village **baba‑bari** (story‑telling circle), the pitha‑tree was a gift from **Rakkhoshi**, a shape‑shifting forest spirit who roamed the mangroves and the hills. Rakkhoshi was neither wholly benevolent nor wholly malevolent; she was a **guardian of the harvest**, demanding respect and a proper **offering of pitha** each year. If the people forgot the ritual, she would turn the tree’s bounty sour, and the village would know famine.

The ritual went as follows:

1. **Sondhya Pitha Prasad** – at dusk on the night of the full moon, the women of the house would knead fresh rice flour with jaggery, coconut, and a pinch of **poppy seeds** (posto).  
2. **Shukra‑Brahma Puja** – a small earthen lamp (diya) was lit, and a **prayer to Shukra (the planet Venus)**, who in local belief guides the harvest, was recited in the old **Bengali chant**:  
   > “Shukra‑Brahma, amader pitha‑bari,  
   > Shanti‑shanti, amader bari.”  
3. **Offering to Rakkhoshi** – a single, perfectly round pitha was placed on a banana leaf at the foot of the pitha‑tree, and the eldest daughter (or, if no daughter, the eldest son) would whisper **“Rakkhoshi, amader shanti rakhun”** (“Rakkhoshi, keep our peace”).  

When the offering was accepted, the tree would glow faintly, and the buds would open, revealing **soft, fragrant pithas** ready for harvest.

---

### 3. The Day the Ritual Was Forgotten  

One year, a **monsoon flood** devastated the rice paddies. The villagers were forced to **relocate temporarily** to a higher ground near the **Karnafuli River**. In the haste, **Ma** and **Dadi** missed the full‑moon night. Baba‑Mohan, now fourteen, was sent to fetch water from the river and never returned before sunrise.

When the flood receded, the family returned to their home. The banyan tree stood as before, but the **pitha‑tree** was **bare**—its twisted branches were leafless, and no golden buds swayed in the night breeze. The villagers whispered, “Rakkhoshi has been angered.”

That night, a **low, mournful howl** rose from the mangrove thicket. The sound was unlike any **bagula** (heron) or **bansh** (bamboo flute) the children knew. It was the voice of **Rakkhoshi**, who had taken the form of a **large, black‑spotted tiger‑like creature** with eyes that glimmered like wet coal.

---

### 4. The Encounter  

Baba‑Mohan, restless and brave, decided to **seek the spirit** and plead for forgiveness. He slipped out of the house, taking only a **small bamboo basket** and a **piece of leftover pitha** his mother had left on the kitchen shelf. He walked along the **silt‑laden path** toward the pitha‑tree, chanting the old **Rakkhoshi mantra** his grandmother had taught him:

> “Rakkhoshi, amader bhul,  
> Shanti dao, pitha phul.”  

When he reached the tree, the air grew cold, and the **Rakkhoshi** emerged from the shadows, its **fur glistening with sea‑foam**. Its voice was a mixture of **hissing wind** and **soft lullaby**:

> “Why do you come, little **bhai** (brother), when the offering is missed?”

Baba‑Mohan fell to his knees, holding the pitha reverently. “**Dada** (elder brother), we forgot the ritual because the flood took our rice. Please, forgive us. Let the tree bear fruit again.”

Rakkhoshi’s eyes softened. She remembered the **generations of devotion** that had once been offered at her feet. She spoke of the **balance** between **human need** and **nature’s demand**:

> “When the people forget, the earth sighs. When they remember, the earth sings. Bring the **pitha** to the **riverbank**, and let the **water** carry it downstream. Offer it to the **river goddess** **Bonobashi**, and then return to the tree with a **new promise**.”

---

### 5. The Ritual of Reconciliation  

Following Rakkhoshi’s instruction, Baba‑Mohan carried the pitha to the **Karnafuli’s edge**. He placed it on a **white cloth** (shital) and whispered a **prayer to Bonobashi**, the river goddess who protects travelers and fishermen:

> “Bonobashi, amader pitha,  
> Shanti dao, amader shara.”  

He then **submerged the pitha** gently, letting the river’s current take it downstream. As the pitha floated away, a **soft golden light** rose from the water, spreading upstream like a **glimmering veil**.

When Baba‑Mohan returned to the pitha‑tree, he found **tiny buds** beginning to unfurl, each one cradling a **miniature pitha**. The tree’s bark glowed faintly, as if **blushing** at the renewed respect.

He ran back to the house, shouting, “**Maa! Dadi! Baba!** The tree is alive again!” The family gathered, and together they performed the **full ritual**:

1. **Sondhya Pitha Prasad** – all the women prepared a fresh batch of **bhapa pitha** with **jaggery** and **coconut**.  
2. **Shukra‑Brahma Puja** – the eldest daughter, **Rupa**, lit the diya and recited the chant.  
3. **Offering to Rakkhoshi** – a **golden‑hued pitha** was placed at the foot of the tree, and Baba‑Mohan, now **Baba‑Mohan‑Babu**, whispered, “**Rakkhoshi, amader shanti rakhun**.”  

The tree shimmered, and a **sweet fragrance** of **poppy seed** and **coconut milk** filled the air. The villagers, hearing the news, came from the fields, each bringing a **small offering**—a handful of **jaggery**, a **piece of banana leaf**, or a **song** sung in the **Chittagonian dialect**.

---

### 6. The Moral and the Continuing Tradition  

From that year onward, the people of **Bashkhali** never missed the **full‑moon pitha offering** again. The story of the **Pitha Tree and Rakkhoshi** was told at every **Bengali New Year (Pohela Boishakh)**, at **weddings**, and especially to the **children** before they went to school. The tale reminded them:

* **Respect the cycles** of nature—rain, flood, harvest.  
* **Honor the spirits** (Rakkhoshi, Bonobashi) with **simple, sincere offerings**.  
* **Remember kinship**—the terms **baba, ma, dada, bhai, didi, shona** (dear one) are not just words but bonds that keep the community together.  

Even today, when the **full moon** rises over the **Bay of Bengal**, the **pitha‑tree** stands, its branches heavy with **golden pithas**, and the villagers of Cox’s Bazar still gather, chant, and share **pitha** under its shade—knowing that the **Rakkhoshi** watches, content, as long as the **ritual** is kept alive in heart and hearth.  

---  

*Note: This retelling is based on oral tradition collected from elders in the coastal villages of Cox’s Bazar. Variations of names, rituals, and dialogue exist across families, but the core elements— the pitha‑tree, the spirit Rakkhoshi, the missed offering, and the reconciliation through water and renewed promise—remain consistent.*
