**The Golden Knife – “Shonar Kāṭi, Rupor Kāṭi”**  
*A tale from *Thākurmār Jhuli* (Grandma’s Bag of Stories), collected by Dakshinaranjan Mitra Majumdar*  

---

### Setting the Scene  

In a bright, mango‑laden village of Bengal, beside a languid river that sang soft “bhor‑bhor” at dawn, lived a poor but honest brahmana family:  

- **Bābā** – the father, a diligent scholar of the Vedas.  
- **Mātā** – his devoted wife, known for the fragrant scent of jasmine that clung to her saree.  
- **Munni** – their tender‑hearted daughter, the darling of the village, whose laughter was as sweet as the **misri** that the old sweet‑seller sold at the bazaar.  

The family was blessed with a tiny shrine in the courtyard, where they performed **Puja** each **Suklapaksha** (the waxing fortnight) to **Lakshmi‑Mata**, seeking her grace for a modest grain‑yield.  

---

### The Envious Neighbour  

Next door lived a **bāiḍhya** (rich and ostentatious) landlord, **Bikashchand**. He owned a big **pāri**, kept a **horimukhi** (peacock) in his garden, and boasted of a **shonar kathi** (golden wooden stick) that shimmered whenever the **sun‑rays** brushed it. He would flaunt it to the villagers, his **dalal** (messenger) chanting, “*Ei kathi dekhle, godhuli te dhara na!*” (If you see this stick, you’ll never be poor again).  

Bikashchand, though rich, was **lopna** (jealous). He envied the humble devotion of Bābā’s household and plotted to possess whatever wealth seemed beyond his reach.

---

### The Dream‑Message  

One night, under the silvery **chand** (moon), **Munni** fell into a deep sleep. In her dream, a **siddhānta‑Brahma** (celestial sage) appeared, his beard white as **safed‑mach** (white rice). He whispered:

> “*Munni, hear my words—*  
> *In the forest of **Kalimati**, there lies a **shonar kāṭi** (a golden knife) and a **rupor kāṭi** (silver knife).  
> *If you bring them home, your family shall never know hunger again.  
> *But heed this: the knives belong to **Baba‑Bali**, the forest spirit. Offer him **til‑pani** (sesame water), **tulsi** leaves, and a **bhog** of **payesh** (sweet rice pudding) before you take them.*”

Munni awoke, heart pounding like the **dhak** (drum) at a wedding. She told her **Mātā** and **Bābā**, who, after an earnest discussion, decided to respect the dream’s warning and seek the forest spirit’s blessing.

---

### The Journey to Kalimati  

Before setting out, the family performed a **Puja** to **Ganesha**—the remover of obstacles. They smeared the entrance of their home with **kumkuma**, rang **conches**, and offered **modak**. The **Mātā** tied a **raksha‑bandhan** (protective thread) around Munni’s wrist, chanting:

> “*Shubho shishir, shanti‑shree, bhulbe na jāno kokhono*”  
> (O gentle breeze of the season, bring peace; may you never forget us.)

Munni, accompanied by **Bābā’s younger brother**—her affectionate **cācā**, the **khalā** (maternal uncle)—set out at first light, carrying a **khaddā** (coconut) filled with **til‑pani**, a handful of **tulsi** leaves, and a small earthen pot of **payesh**.
