**Kironmala – A Tale from *Thakurmar Jhuli***  

*The story that follows is drawn from the beloved 19‑th‑century Bengali folk‑anthology *Thakurmar Jhuli* (Grandmother’s Bag of Tales).  It is retold here in a prose style that aims to keep the original flavors— the kinship titles, the village rituals, the sounds of the dhol, the scent of incense, and the cadence of the old Bengali tongue.*

---

### 1. The Village and the Family

In a modest hamlet by the banks of the **Brahmaputra**, lived a widowed **Nani** (maternal grandmother) with her young grandson **Kironmala** (the boy’s name is a contraction of *Kiron* “griffin‑like” and *Mala* “garland”, denoting a child of extraordinary destiny).  

Kironmala’s mother had died when he was a babe; his **Pishi** (father’s sister) and **Masi** (mother’s sister) lived in the same *bari* (house). The household was simple: a mud‑thatched roof, an earthen **chula** (cooking hearth), a small **pond** where children splashed, and a little shrine in the corner where the family kept a **shiva‑lingam** and a **Shakti** idol.  

Each evening, before the **aarti** (ritual of light) of the deity, the family would chant **“Om Namah Shivaya”**, offer **tulsi** leaves and a handful of **poppy seeds** (khush), and the Nani would recount bygone tales of **raja** (kings), **rakshasa** (demons), and brave heroes.

---

### 2. The Prophetic Dream

One moon‑lit night, while the village slept, a fierce wind rattled the **janala** (window). The Nani, who was known for her *puranic* wisdom, fell into a deep **swapna** (dream). In her vision, the goddess **Durga** appeared on a tiger’s back, brandishing a shining **trishul** and chanting:

> “*Jaga dhara, dadu‑dada, shona khela,
>  Kironmala ke rakhbo shaktir dhela.*”

(“The world shall tremble, the older brothers shall be humbled; Kironmala shall be cloaked in power.”)

She awoke with tears wetting her *khadi* (cotton cloth). She gathered the family around the **diya** (lamp) and announced that Kironmala was destined for a **mahavidya** (great knowledge) that would either lift them from poverty or bring ruin if misused.

---

### 3. The Wicked Aunt and the Test of Kindness

Kironmala’s **Pishi**, however, was a **kshatra** (harsh) woman. She was jealous of the affection the boy received and often blamed him for any mishap in the house. One day, after a storm, the **bari’s** *bamboo* roof **churi** (splintered) and the roof‑thatched **chhatri** (coconut‑leaf fan) fell, spilling water into the **cooking pit**.

The Pishi seized the moment, shouting, “*Kironmala!*” and thrust a **goru‑baaki** (cow‑ dung cake) into his hand, demanding he clean the mess. The child, his small palms trembling, obeyed obediently, but his pure heart felt the sting of injustice.

When the **Muktadhara** (local priest) came for the **Bodhu** (wedding) preparations of the village chief’s daughter, he noticed Kironmala’s tears and asked the Nani why the boy was sorrowful. The Nani whispered that kindness is the seed of true strength, and that *karma* (action) will always return to its source.

---

### 4. The Festival of **Mahalaxmi Pujā**

Soon the village prepared for **Mahalaxmi Puja**, a grand celebration of the goddess of wealth, held on the auspicious **Bhadra** full moon. The whole **gram** (village) threw open its doors: the women painted **rangoli** (kolam) in front of their homes, the men rang **dhak** and **dholak**, and the children sang **“Tumi Je Amar“** while scattering **pata‑pata** (rice powders) on the ground.

Kironmala, under the watchful eyes of his **Mama** (father’s brother) and **Baba** (father), was asked to **prasad** (tribute) the goddess. He was given a **shakha** (sacred twig) to offer, a bowl of **payesh** (sweet rice pudding), and a small **kalash** (pot)
