**The Prince and the Shepherd**

When they were boys, a prince and a humble shepherd were inseparable friends. The prince swore that, once he wore the crown, he would make his shepherd companion his minister. The shepherd accepted the promise with a simple “Alright,” and the two spent their days together: after the shepherd tended his cattle, they would sit arm‑in‑arm beneath a tree, the shepherd playing his flute while the prince listened.

**The King’s Forgetting**

Years later the prince became king, married Queen Kanchanmala, and filled the palace with jewels. Caught up in his new wealth and power, he forgot the promise to his shepherd. One day the shepherd arrived at the palace gate and asked, “My friend, you never even showed me your queen.” The doorman drove him away. Heart‑broken, the shepherd vanished without a trace.

**The Needle‑King**

The next morning the king could not open his eyes. His whole body—face, skin, even his hair—was covered with sharp needles. He could neither eat, sleep, nor speak, and he understood that the punishment came from breaking his promise to the shepherd, though he could not explain this to anyone. The kingdom fell into gloom; Queen Kanchanmala, despite her grief, kept the realm running.

**The False Handmaiden**

While bathing at the river, the queen met a striking girl who offered to become her handmaiden. The queen promised the girl that, if she could free the king of his needles, she would be taken as a servant. The girl accepted, and the queen gave her the bangles on her wrists as payment.

The girl bathed the queen, removed her ornaments, and urged the queen to immerse herself in the river. As the queen submerged, the girl slipped into the queen’s clothes, took the royal jewelry, and climbed back onto the riverbank, proclaiming herself the queen. When the real queen emerged, she found herself dressed as a servant. In despair she followed the impostor back to the palace.

There the false queen, believing herself restored, demanded the king’s preparations for her return from the bath. She ordered elephants, horses, and a palanquin; the minister and a courtier who failed to obey were beheaded. No one dared to speak against her, and the impostor kept the throne while the true queen was reduced to a handmaiden, crying by the rubbish heap about the loss of her bangles and the kingdom’s ruin.

**The Thread‑Seeker**

One day the true queen, still in her lowly state, went to the river to wash coarse clothes. She met a strange person sitting under a tree with a bundle of thread, reciting riddles about needles and wealth. The queen asked the person what he wanted, offering needles in return for help. The person accepted and accompanied her to the palace, where the queen narrated her suffering.

The thread‑person devised a plan for the upcoming festival of Pitha‑Kuduli. He told the false queen to decorate the courtyard and the handmaiden to make rice cakes. The false queen produced crude, lumpy cakes, while the handmaiden created exquisite varieties (Chandrapuli, Mohanbanshi, Kheer‑murli, Chandanpata). Seeing the difference, the thread‑person recognized the true queen.

When the handmaiden painted the alpana, she made a delicate lotus vine with golden pitchers, paddy stalks, peacocks and the goddess Lakshmi’s footprints. The false queen’s rough painting was a coarse smear of rice paste. The thread‑person then challenged the impostor, calling her a “serpent‑maid” who had bought the throne with the queen’s bangles.

Enraged, the false queen ordered the executioner to behead the handmaiden and the thread‑person, declaring she would bathe in their blood. As the executioner seized them, the thread‑person sang a spell. Threads flew from his bundle, binding the executioner’s hands and feet, then entered the queen’s nostrils, rendering her speech nasally and frantic.

He invoked the threads again, commanding them to pass through the needles studding the king’s body. Tens of thousands of threads swirled into the king, weaving among the needles. The needles, now animated, asked where to pierce first. The thread‑person directed them to the fake queen’s eyes and mouth. The needles rose from the king, flew to Kanchanmala, and stitched shut her eyes and mouth, causing her excruciating agony.

**The Restoration**

The stitching freed the king from the needles. He opened his eyes and, to his astonishment, saw his long‑lost shepherd friend standing before him. Overcome with joy, the king and the shepherd embraced, each weeping for the other. The king proclaimed, “Friend, do not blame me. I could never find another friend like you. From this day on you shall be my minister.” The shepherd asked for his flute; the king commissioned a golden flute for him.

Meanwhile the false queen, Kanchanmala, died from the needle‑spear wounds, ending her sorrowful reign.

**A New Harmony**

The shepherd took his appointed role as minister, serving the king faithfully. Each night, under the same tree by the riverbank where they once sat, the shepherd played his new golden flute while the king listened, the two arm‑in‑arm once more. Their days, and the memory of Kanchanmala’s tragic end, passed in peace and happiness.
