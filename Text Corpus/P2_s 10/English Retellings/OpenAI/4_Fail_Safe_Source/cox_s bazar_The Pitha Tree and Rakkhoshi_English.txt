**The Tale of the Pitha Tree and the Ogress**

A boy who had lost his father lived with his mother in poverty. From his earliest years he adored pithas, and his mother would make them for him every day, though it grew harder for her to keep up the task.

One day, after making a final pitha, the mother planted it in a corner of the courtyard, hoping it might provide food later. Within days a sapling sprouted, grew into a strange, beautiful tree, and soon the branches were laden with pithas. The boy climbed the tree, plucked a pitha, and tasted a joy he had never known. From then on he lived happily, feeding on the miraculous fruit.

Soon an Ogress, disguised as a woman, began to linger beneath the tree. She tried to lure the boy with sweet words and repeatedly asked him for a pitha. When he tossed one toward her, she complained it was dirty or broken; when he tried to hand one directly, she reached to seize him. Each time she almost caught him, the boy screamed and escaped.

One day the Ogress finally succeeded: she bound the boy’s hands, feet, and mouth, stuffed him into a sack, and set off with him. While she stopped in the jungle to answer nature’s call, the boy tore the knot of the sack and slipped out. He then filled the empty sack with clods of earth, leaves, thorns, and a bamboo tube of water, sealed it, and fled home. The Ogress returned, saw the sack looking unchanged, hoisted it onto her shoulder, and continued walking.

As she walked, water leaked from the bamboo tube onto her body; she thought the boy was urinating on her and cursed, “You are wetting me now; I will eat you.” When a thorn pricked her, she shouted that the boy was pricking her and vowed revenge. When a clod of earth struck her, she claimed the boy was kicking her and again threatened vengeance. Enraged, she reached her home, tore open the sack, and found only earth, leaves, thorns and the bamboo tube. Furious, she screamed wildly.

The next day the Ogress again tried to coax the boy from the pitha tree, using ever‑sweeter words. She eventually captured him once more and carried him away. On the way she ranted about cutting the boy into pieces and eating him, calling for her wife, “Ghutila!” to do the work. The wife took the sack to the kitchen, opened it, and the boy emerged.

Seeing a huge cleaver, the boy quickly beheaded the Ogress’s daughter‑in‑law, Ghutila, then put on her clothes, disguising himself as the wife. He lowered the veil, placed Ghutila’s body on the fire, and joined the Ogress’s family at the feast. While they ate, the wife’s sister kept pointing out how the “hand” and “eye” looked just like her sister’s, prompting laughter and scolding.

Before the meal could finish, the boy, still disguised as the wife, slipped away, claiming he would fetch betel leaves. He went to the riverbank with a water pitcher, where he met a merchant. He told the merchant his whole story, and the merchant, moved by pity, took him aboard his boat.

Meanwhile, the Ogress’s family, having finished the meal and finding Ghutila missing, went to the riverbank. From a distance they saw the boy in the wife’s disguise and thought they had found the daughter‑in‑law. Before they could approach, the boy jumped onto the merchant’s boat and discarded the wife’s clothes.

The Ogress’s son, her mother, father and sister wailed loudly; the Ogress herself tore out her hair in fury. The merchant’s boat set off, carrying the freed boy, who began to chant:

*“Ogress, Ogress, howl and whine,  
On your son’s wife’s flesh you dine!”*

Thus the boy escaped the Ogress’s clutches, leaving the monster to rage in vain.
