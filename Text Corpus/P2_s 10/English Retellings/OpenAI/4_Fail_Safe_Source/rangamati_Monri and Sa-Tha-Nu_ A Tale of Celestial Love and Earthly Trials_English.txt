**The Tale of Monori and Sa‑tha‑nu**

Long ago Indra, the King of Heaven, had seven beautiful daughters who were identical in looks and full of cheer. Though they loved their heavenly home, the princesses often wondered about the Earth. After pleading with their father, Indra finally allowed them to visit the mortal world.

Every day the seven sisters descended to Earth, playing and bathing together in a secluded meadow’s lake. One day a hunter hid nearby and, entranced by their beauty, resolved to capture one. He sought help from the Naga King, who gave him a net. The hunter set a trap at the lake; when the princesses rose to fly home, the net snagged the youngest, Monori, on her left little finger. The other six sisters tried desperately to free her, but failed and fled, leaving Monori to fall to the ground.

The hunter rushed to her, and when Monori begged “Brother, please save me,” his heart melted. He released her from the net, and Monori accepted him as a sworn brother. That night she could not return to heaven, so the hunter took her to the kingdom’s prince, Sa‑tha‑nu. The prince, drawn by her extraordinary beauty, rode to the meadow, learned her name, and married her with great pomp. They lived happily together.

Soon war broke out; the prince left for battle, leaving his pregnant wife alone. Monori gave birth to a radiant baby boy and devoted herself to Buddhist practice. The palace’s Brahmins, jealous of her influence, began to malign her, urging her to return to heaven. Meanwhile, Monori’s father‑in‑law dreamed of a white elephant circling the palace, and the Brahmins seized the omen, claiming that only Monori’s sacrifice could save the prince.

Hearing this, Monori decided to ascend to heaven, but she could not bear leaving her son. After briefly flying upward, she heard a dove’s mournful call and turned back, nursing her child again. She then remembered a devout sage in the forest who loved her. She gave him the ring on her left hand and told him how to reach heaven: cross the scorching ocean on the back of the great Naga King, step onto the deserted island that appears beneath the Naga, then fly onward.

Monori left for heaven. Her sisters wept for her, and their father, the King of Heaven, ordered endless bathing of Monori to wash away her earthly scent. Meanwhile, Prince Sa‑tha‑nu returned victorious, but could not find his wife. In his quest he reached the sage, received Monori’s ring, and followed her instructions. He crossed the hot sea on the Naga King’s back, braved storms, and reached the island. Exhausted, he slept under a tree, where two talking birds discussed a grand feast in the heavenly kingdom for Monori’s ritual bathing.

Listening, the prince transformed himself into an ant, slid under the queen bird’s wing, and rode the birds to heaven. Arriving at the celestial pond, he saw nymphs watering Monori; he slipped the ring into the last nymph’s pitcher. When the nymph poured the water on Monori, the ring fell into the princess’s hand. Recognizing it as the one she had given the sage, Monori realized her husband had come.

The King of Heaven, hearing the reunion, declared that Sa‑tha‑nu must pass tests before she could be his daughter‑in‑law. The first test: seven holes in a wall, each sister’s left little finger placed in a hole; Sa‑tha‑nu must choose Monori’s. Monori sent a fly that settled on her finger, revealing the correct one. The second test: the sisters would fly in the sky in identical garments; Sa‑tha‑nu must point out Monori. She had arranged for her scarf and dress hem to hang a little longer and for herself to fly slightly lower; the prince identified her correctly.

Impressed, the king said no more tests were needed, but invited Sa‑tha‑nu to devise one of his own. He asked that the sisters fly again while he shot arrows that would strike only Monori’s clothing, not her body. He shot seven arrows; Monori descended safely, floating upon them. The king then remarried Monori to Sa‑tha‑nu with great ceremony and, that night, sent them and a new palace back to Earth while they slept.

Awakening on Earth, the couple found their old palace ruined and were told that a hundred years had passed on Earth while only a day passed in heaven. Their son, now an old man, was restored to a two‑month‑old infant when Monori cradled him, confirming the time‑difference. The miracle convinced all that heaven’s day equals a hundred earthly years.

From then on Monori’s devotion to Buddhism deepened, as did the bond between her and Sa‑tha‑nu across many lives. In a past life a servant had prayed to the Buddha that he might always be the cause of their union; the Buddha granted the wish, so in this life the hunter who first rescued Monori was reborn as the very cause that brought the couple together. 

Thus the story of the celestial princess Monori, her mortal prince Sa‑tha‑nu, their trials, reunions, and everlasting love across heaven and earth comes to an end.
