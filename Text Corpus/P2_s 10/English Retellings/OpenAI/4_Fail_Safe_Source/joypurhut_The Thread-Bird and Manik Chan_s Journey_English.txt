**The Tale of the Blacksmith, the Tailor, and the Thread‑Bird**

In a kingdom a blacksmith named **Mathur Kamar** and a tailor named **Abbas Khalifa** were famed for their extraordinary abilities. The king summoned them and challenged each to perform a feat that would be remembered forever.

* Mathur Kamar promised to forge **101 iron fish fry** that would come alive and dance when released into a pond.  
* Abbas Khalifa claimed he could make a **bird of thread** that, in a single swoop, would devour all of those fish.

A competition was announced, the date and the traditional pond were proclaimed by drumbeat, and the whole realm gathered to watch.

When the king gave the word, Mathur released his 101 iron fish into the water; they swam about as promised. The crowd cheered, and the tailor then released his thread‑bird. The bird rose, circled once, and swallowed every fish.

Impressed, the king asked the tailor what else his bird could do. The tailor said that, if a two‑and‑a‑half‑day‑old infant were provided, the bird could perform tricks with the child while flying. The king’s youngest queen had such a baby. After some reluctance, she handed the infant to the king on the condition that the child be returned immediately after the performance.

The king brought the queen and child to the pond and ordered the bird to begin. The bird lifted the infant, performed aerial acrobatics, and then, at the tailor’s warning, should have been brought down before a set time limit. The king, greedy for a longer show, ignored the warning. When the time expired, the bird kept rising, carrying the child higher and higher. The queen’s cries and the king’s futile promises could not stop it; the bird left the kingdom and flew into another land.

---

### The Bird’s Arrival in a New Country

The bird finally landed in a garden belonging to a **flower‑seller** and her husband. Their garden, long barren, was suddenly full of blossoms. Astonished, the husband shouted for the “virtuous one” who had caused the dead trees to bloom. The bird appeared, still holding the infant.

The couple, childless, took the child in as their own, naming him **Manik Chan**. To explain the sudden child, the flower‑seller pretended to be pregnant, tying a bundle to her belly and spreading the rumor. Ten months later, she hired **Loya Bewa**, a midwife, to announce the birth of a son. The midwife complied, and the story spread; the child grew up as the flower‑seller’s son.

---

### Manik Chan’s Later Life

When Manik became a youth, he wove a **threadless garland** for his mother. While selling garlands at the king’s palace, the king’s daughter **Chandramukhi** bought the garland, admired its beauty, and asked who had made it. Manik’s mother finally admitted that Manik had woven it. Chandramukhi fell in love with Manik, asked her father for his hand, and after initial reluctance the king consented.

Manik and Chandramukhi married and soon had a son; a few years later Chandramukhi became pregnant again. Near the end of her pregnancy she urged Manik to visit his native land. Remembering that only the thread‑bird knew the way, they retrieved it from the flower‑seller’s house, placed their son on it, and set off.

During the flight, Chandramukhi, in labor, begged the bird to land. The bird obliged, touching down on a river sandbar, its wing broken. She gave birth to a second son. While Manik fetched firewood, a huge python approached the newborn. The bird, determined to protect its master’s child, attacked the snake; the snake coiled around the bird and both died.

A passing ferryman found the two infants on the sandbar, took them aboard, and raised them as his own.

---

### Chandramukhi’s Capture

After the birth, Chandramukhi, having washed clothes on the sandbar, was seized by a greedy merchant whose boat passed nearby. He forced her onto his vessel and proposed marriage. To protect herself, Chandramukhi negotiated a delay of four years and demanded that she be kept in a separate house and accompany the merchant on every trading voyage. The merchant agreed.

A year later, while the merchant was on another voyage, Chandramukhi was moored at the sandbar overnight as required by a new royal decree: all merchant boats must stay at the landing and be inspected. The ferryman, now a guard, fell ill and sent the two brothers (the children raised by him) to stand watch.

During the night the younger brother began to doze; the elder sang a mournful song recounting the loss of their parents, the death of the bird, and their own abandonment:

> “Father went to fetch fire…  
> Mother went to the banks of the Yamuna…  
> The bird went to kill the python…  
> We two brothers are left on the river’s sand…”

Hearing the words, Chandramukhi recognized the brothers as her own sons. She accused the brothers of lewd remarks, prompting the merchant to report the matter to the king. The king summoned everyone. When asked what the brothers had said, they sang the same lamentation, proving their identity. Chandramukhi then revealed the whole story, and the king, recalling Manik’s earlier plight, summoned Manik Chan.

Manik recognized his wife among the gathered women and declared that she was his lost Chandramukhi. The king rebuked the merchant, ordered him to return to his own region, and arranged for a boat to carry Manik, Chandramukhi, and their children back to their homeland.

Thus the family was reunited, and the kingdom’s ruler ensured their safe return.
