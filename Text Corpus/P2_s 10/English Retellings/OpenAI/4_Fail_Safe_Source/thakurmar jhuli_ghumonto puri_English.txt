In a certain country there lived a beautiful and virtuous prince whose radiance filled the royal palace. One day he announced that he would travel to foreign lands. The queen fell into sorrow, giving up food and sleep, while the king merely said, “Very well, let him go.”  

The kingdom prepared for his departure: the people gathered in great numbers, the king supplied spies and attendants, and the queen sent platters of gems and jewels. The prince, however, took none of these things. Dressed in fresh clothes with a new sword at his side, he set out alone.

He walked for a long time, crossing many countries, mountains, rivers, and the realms of many kings, until he entered a silent forest where no birds sang and no beasts appeared. He kept walking and at the forest’s heart he discovered a palace of endless size. Its spire reached the sky and its gateway spanned the entire forest, yet no music sounded and no guard stood at the gate.

Inside, the palace shone as though washed in milk—bright, clean, and utterly still. No sound came from anywhere; not a leaf fell, not a speck of dust moved. The prince explored the four sides of the palace and, in a vast courtyard, saw rows upon rows of elephants, horses, sepoys, soldiers, gate‑keepers, and commanders. When he called, no one answered. As he approached, he realized the troops and animals were stone statues, frozen in perfect detail.

He entered the main palace. In one chamber were thousands of shields, swords, bows and arrows, all hanging in rows; the guards and sepoys there were also stone statues. He slipped his sword from its scabbard and moved on. In another chamber lay a great royal court. Golden lamps burned with ghee, gems glittered, and on the throne sat a stone king; beside him a stone minister, and all the courtiers, bards, attendants, soldiers and commanders were likewise turned to stone, eyes unmoving, mouths silent. The royal canopy was tilted, a maid’s fly‑whisk drooped—nothing showed any sign of life.

A third chamber was filled with countless lamps, diamonds, rubies, pearls and other treasures, too many for the room to contain. The prince examined them without touching anything and left. The next chamber overwhelmed him with the fragrance of innumerable flowers. Though there was no water or soil, the middle of the room was a sea of blooming lotus flowers whose scent filled the air.

In that lotus garden stood a golden bedstead. Upon it rose a diamond post, from which a garland of flowers swayed; on a diamond stem sat a golden lotus that cradled a supremely beautiful princess. She slept, her hands and feet unseen, only her moon‑like face shining within the golden petals. The prince gazed at her, and years passed while she remained in deep slumber and he watched, unmoving.

One day the prince noticed a golden stick beside the princess’s head. He picked it up, and at the same time discovered a silver stick on the other side. While examining the two sticks, the golden one lightly tapped the princess’s head. Instantly the lotus garden trembled, the golden bedstead stirred, the golden petals fell away, and the princess’s hands and feet appeared. She awoke, stretching her limbs and rubbing her eyes.

At that moment the whole palace came to life: birds began to sing, the gate guard shouted his call, elephants and horses in the courtyard trumpeted, sepoys’ swords clanged, and in the royal court the stone king, minister, courtiers and all others awoke from a thousand‑year sleep. Soldiers, guards, troops and commanders readied their bows and swords. Everyone was astonished at the newcomers.

The king asked the prince, “From which fortunate king and country do you come, who has saved us from this deathly sleep?” The people added, “A demon had touched us with a silver stick and put our golden kingdom to sleep; you have broken the spell.” The prince bowed silently.

The king declared that he would give the princess to the prince and hand the kingdom over to him. A rain of flowers and sandalwood fell; rice was scattered, drums sounded, maids ground spices, servants chopped vegetables, auspicious pots and bouquets were placed at every door, decorative alpana drawings and married women’s chants filled the courtyards, and conch shells blew. The palace burst into joyous celebration, its treasury opened, and the four courtyards shimmered.

In the bright moonlight, before a fire‑priest, with betel nut and leaf as dowry, the king placed a five‑jeweled crown on the prince’s head and gave the princess to him in marriage. Cries of celebration rose everywhere.

Years passed—one, two, many years—yet the prince who had gone to see the world did not return. The queen fell into despair, weeping and beating her head, and took to her bed. The king, worried and tearful, grew blind, and the kingdom was filled with darkness and sorrow.
