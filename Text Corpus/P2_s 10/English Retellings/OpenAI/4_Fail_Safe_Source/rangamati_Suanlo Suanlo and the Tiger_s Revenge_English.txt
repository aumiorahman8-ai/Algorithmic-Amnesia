**The Tale of the Five Sons, Their Father, and Their Nephew**

A man had five sons and one daughter. One of the sons suggested that they all perform a single, spectacular deed that would bring them fame. They agreed to cut a *ghila* creeper with a single swing of a machete. Their father made his sons swear that anyone who failed to do it in one stroke would have to live in a hut beside a tiger‑run path.

The next morning the brothers sharpened their machetes. While the father was away answering nature’s call, the five brothers secretly dulled his machete by rubbing the blade on a stone. When the cutting contest began, each son, with his sharp machete, sliced the creeper in one blow; the father, with his now‑blunted blade, could not. He tried again and still failed. The youngest son asked him, “Father, even if you gave your word, you should not punish anyone so cruelly.” The father replied, “A man has one word, not two. Build the hut by the tiger’s path.” 

The five sons built the hut on the edge of the tiger trail, left their father there, and returned to their own homes. That night the tigers arrived, roaring. The father shouted a strange chant:

> “I am the father of five brave sons. My body is shaped like a bottle. My teeth are like a chicken‑coop door. My eyes are as big as a ball of thread. I am that man. Where are you?”

The chant scared the tigers away. The next night the same thing happened; the father again kept the beasts at bay. On the third night, exhausted, he fell asleep. The tigers slipped in and killed him.

The next morning the daughter came with food and called, “Father, are you there?” He answered, “Yes, come in,” and she continued to bring him food each morning and evening. After two more nights of the father’s successful fright, the third night he fell asleep again and the tigers killed him. When the daughter called out that morning, there was no answer. She returned home and told her five brothers that their father was dead.

The brothers could not accept it. “We must avenge our father’s murder,” they vowed, sharpening their bows and arrows. The next day they set out for the tigers’ lair, passing through many villages.

* **The Rooster Village** – A rooster asked why they travelled. He promised them meat if they could hit him with an arrow as soon as he crowed from a high ladder. All five shot, but none struck.

* **The Mala‑Pig Village** – The pig asked the same question; again none could hit him.

* **The Cowherd and Cow Villages** – Here a “Debi” (goddess‑like woman) answered a call for entry. She pretended to be sick and finally opened the door, cooked rice, and, horrifically, turned the brothers’ father’s flesh into a curry which they ate. After the meal she asked for their arrows to keep safe from mice. Trusting her, they handed the arrows over. While they slept, Debi chewed the arrowheads, dulling them.

The following morning Debi asked the brothers what they wanted: “the slippery slope of a tree or the slippery slope of high ground?” They chose the high ground and hid behind a mound. She then called her pet dogs – actually tigers – to eat the brothers. The brothers fired their dulled arrows one by one, killing none. The tigers devoured them, and the five brothers perished.

The sister, now alone, mourned her father and brothers. She prayed to **Pakhiyan** (the Creator). While drying paddy on a bamboo platform, a mysterious “amloki” fell from the sky. She ate it, became pregnant, and gave birth to a son. An old widowed midwife suggested naming him **Suanlo Suanlo** (after the amloki). The name was accepted.

Suanlo grew, but his peers excluded him. When he asked his mother for a *ghila* to play with, she told him to ask the sky, “Give me a ghila.” A stone *ghila* fell from the heavens. His friends mocked him, saying the stone *ghila* broke the ones they used and taunted him for being fatherless. His mother consoled him, saying his true father was the Creator and that he must avenge his five uncles.

She taught him the skills needed for vengeance, especially training him on **acila mim**, a very slippery wheat‑like grass on the mountain that is hard to stand upon. She also instructed him to ask the Creator for a powerful bow, arrows, and anything else he might need.

Armed with this knowledge, Suanlo set out. He, too, passed the rooster, pig, cowherd, and cow villages, receiving the same challenges and refusing to give up. He finally reached Debi’s house. When she asked him again to hand over his weapons for safekeeping, he refused and kept his arrows (now made of stone, unlike his uncles’ wooden ones). At night she tried again to blunt them, but the stone arrows could not be dulled.

The next day Debi asked once more, “the slippery slope of high ground or the slippery slope of a barkless tree?” Suanlo chose the barkless tree, climbed it, and waited. Debi summoned her pet dogs—tigers. This time Suanlo was ready: with his stone bow and arrows he shot and killed every tiger that came, including the pregnant chief tigress.
