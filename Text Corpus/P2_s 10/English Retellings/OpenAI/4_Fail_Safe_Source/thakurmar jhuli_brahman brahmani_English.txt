**The Tale of the Brahmani and Her Husband**

A Brahmani lived with a Brahmin who was utterly foolish. While he spent his days eating and doing nothing, the Brahmani shouldered all the work. She was fierce‑tempered, “a battle‑goddess,” whose temper could snap a nose‑ring with a flick. She barely had any food or oil for her hair; her life was a constant struggle of “daily begging to keep body and soul together,” made worse by her husband’s perpetual demands.  

One day the Brahmin asked her to make pitha. The Brahmani, after dropping her winnowing fan, seized a broom and roared that there was no rice, no money, and that the “son of a pitha‑glutton” could not eat any. Her shout made the courtyard tremble. The Brahmin, shocked by her insults, fled in anger into the forest.

In the woods he met a sage. The sage took him to his ashram, where the Brahmin learned to read and write. He spent his days reciting strange sounds—“Ka‑cha‑pa‑a‑a‑a,” “Kha‑cha‑ka‑a‑a‑a,” “Hagarang dagarang …,” “Chang, chhang, krrram…”—and, after much study, believed he had become learned and powerful. Proudly he packed his satchel, manuscript, staff and mat, shouted “Victory to Jagadamba!” and set out for home, confident that his fame would now spread.

He returned to his village at dusk. Hearing the sizzle of the Brahmani frying “taler bora,” he counted the crackles in his mind, then called out, announcing his return “covered in tilak marks” and his new knowledge. The Brahmani, startled, ran out and asked where he had been. He boasted that his learning let him predict the number of fritters she would make, which amazed her. She ran to tell the neighbours that the Brahmin had returned with unprecedented knowledge. The villagers gathered, saw the Brahmin mumble incomprehensible chants (“hang lang bang chang”), and were bewildered but impressed by his reputation.

The Brahmin’s new fame grew. He read palms, told fortunes, and was celebrated far and wide. One day Moti, a washerman, begged him for help because his lame donkey had vanished. The Brahmin promised to perform a Chandi puja first, then to search for the animal. After a fruitless, scorching search, he told Moti that the goddess Chandi was angry and that he must return the next day. Moti left frightened.

That night, fearing a thief, the Brahmin asked the Brahmani for help. She mocked his scholarship and told him to catch the thief. Together they went out; the “thief” turned out to be the missing donkey, tangled in a rope. In the struggle the rope became a noose around the donkey’s neck, the animal died, and the Brahmin fainted dead. The Brahmani, quick‑thinking, claimed the Brahmin was merely meditating and that the donkey’s reappearance was a miracle of his calculations. The villagers tended the Brahmin, gave him oil, water and fans; he revived, shouted “Thief! Thief!” while the Brahmani tried to hush the crowd. Moti later arrived, prostrated himself before the donkey, and, grateful, began washing the Brahmin’s clothes for free, spreading the story further.

The tale reached the king, whose priceless necklace had gone missing. After many scholars failed, the Brahmin was summoned. He asked for two days; the king granted it. Confined for a night, the Brahmin wailed to Jagadamba. The royal gardener’s wife, Jaga, overheard and, pleading not to be named, confessed she had taken the necklace out of greed and begged the Brahmin to hide her identity. He instructed her to place the necklace in a pot and hide it in the rear pond.

When the Brahmin appeared before the king, he was lavishly attired with tilak marks, multiple shawls, rudrakshas, a top‑knot heavy with flowers, and his satchel, manuscript, umbrella and staff. He performed elaborate gestures, recited mantras, drew countless lines and figures, and finally declared that the necklace lay in the king’s pond. The king ordered the pond drained; a pot was found, broken, revealing the glittering necklace. The king untied the Brahmin, proclaimed him chief scholar, and showered him with wealth, jewels and daily reverence. The queen worshipped him, Jaga supplied flowers, and “Thakur” (the Brahmin) worshipped the ten‑armed goddess.

In the end, the Brahmin lived in a three‑storey palace on a golden bed, his pantry overflowing with oil and jewels. The Brahmani, now joyful, did nothing but smile, and she served her husband happily, while the Brahmin ate pitha every day, proclaiming “Ah, what a goddess of fortune!” Thus the once‑foolish husband became a celebrated scholar, and the formerly oppressed Brahmani found happiness in serving him.
