**The Tale of the Four Friends and the Ogress**

A prince, the son of a minister, the son of a merchant, and the son of the kotwal were inseparable companions. They did no work, spending all their time riding their horses. Their idleness annoyed their fathers—the king, the minister, the merchant, and the kotwal—who ordered that when the boys came to eat they should be given ash instead of rice.

When the boys arrived, the minister’s wife, the merchant’s wife, and the kotwal’s wife, tears in their eyes, served only ash. The boys were shocked and rose to leave, for they could not understand how a mother could give her own son ash. The queen, unable to do the same, prepared a sweet rice pudding and placed a pinch of ash in a corner of the prince’s plate. When the prince asked why ash lay there, his mother said it had simply fallen. Not satisfied, the prince demanded a full explanation. The queen told him everything, and the prince, bowing to his mother’s honesty, left.

He gathered his three friends and asked what they had eaten that day; they all looked at one another in silence. Deciding that staying in that land was no longer possible, the four set out together on their horses.

They rode to the edge of a great, barren plain where four roads diverged. They agreed that the kotwal’s son would go south, the merchant’s son north, the minister’s son west, and the prince east. Each tore a piece of his turban, raised a banner at the crossroads, and promised that whoever returned first would wait for the others.

All rode all day without seeing any village, city, or house, and as night fell they all arrived at the same place—a huge forest. The prince, fearing an ogress’s illusion, warned his friends to stay awake. Hungry, they searched for food among the trees, finding only a deer's head.

The kotwal’s son fetched wood, the merchant’s son fetched water, the minister’s son sought fire, while the prince rested and fell asleep. The kotwal’s son, returning with wood, cut the deer's head; a hideous ogress burst from it, devouring the kotwal’s son and his horse and then assuming the deer's head again.

The merchant’s son, seeing the wood, tried to cut the head; the ogress swallowed him and his horse. The minister’s son arrived, saw the water and wood but no friends, and went to prepare the meat. As he cried out, the ogress, having just eaten him, seized the prince’s horse. The prince drew his sword, but the ogress’ approach was warned by the trees and the prince’s own horse, urging him to flee. He refused to run and, weakened, fled blindly through the forest.

The chase continued until the prince reached a mango tree. He begged the tree for protection; the tree split, letting him hide within its trunk. The ogress, now a beautiful woman, cried at the tree’s foot. A hunting king discovered her, brought her to his palace, and made her his queen, unaware that she was the ogress.

To keep the prince from harming her, the ogress pretended illness. She claimed a “bone‑cracking disease” and demanded the smoke of a specific mango tree to cure it. The king ordered thousands of carpenters to fell that tree. Inside the felled tree, the prince, still alive, begged to be turned into a mango. A mango fell into a pond, was swallowed by a large boal fish, and later the fish was caught. The prince, turned into a snail by the fish, escaped from the fish’s belly, and was found by a householder’s wife while she bathed. When she tried to crush the snail, the prince emerged, explaining his plight and naming her Hasan’s wife. Grateful, he stayed with her.

The ogress‑queen learned of the prince’s survival and told the king that only three impossible items— a dancing stick of the Hasan Champa flower, an ivory fine‑toothed comb, and a thirteen‑cubit seed from a twelve‑cubit cucumber—could cure her. She claimed they lay in the house of a certain prince. The king sent thousands of guards to seize the prince, but the prince escaped, pleading with the king that his queen was an ogress.

The prince wandered for days until he came to a massive, empty palace. Inside he found a sleeping princess guarded by a silver wand at her head and a gold wand at her feet. By swapping the wands he awakened her. She told him that ogres had killed her parents and now held her captive, using the wands to keep her alive only to eat her later.
