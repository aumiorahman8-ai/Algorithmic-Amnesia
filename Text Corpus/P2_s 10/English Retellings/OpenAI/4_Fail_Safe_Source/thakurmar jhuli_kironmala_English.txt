**The Tale of Arun, Barun and Kiranmala**

In a forest lived three children of a Brahmin—two brothers, Arun and Barun, and their sister Kiranmala.  As they grew, their laughter made the birds sing and their cries summoned the deer.  They lived simply: Kiranmala kept the house spotless and cared for their cow Kajallata, while the brothers studied, harvested fruit and chased deer.  Together they gathered flowers to cover their home, offered lamps and sandalwood to the Brahmin’s rites, and learned all his knowledge.

One day the Brahmin called them aside, placed his hands on their heads and declared that everything he owned now belonged to them.  He then departed for the heavens, leaving the three in grief.

Soon after, a king whose kingdom was shrouded in sin went hunting.  A storm drove him off course; he lost his companions and spent a night in a hollow tree.  The next day, exhausted and thirsty, he saw a house and entered.  Inside he met the three siblings, whose bodies glittered like gods.  They gave him water; the king, believing it to be milk, asked who they were.  Arun answered that they were the Brahmin’s children.  The king, overwhelmed, promised to help them whenever they needed.

Kiranmala asked what a king owned; the brothers knew only of books that said a king had elephants, horses and a palace.  Determined, they set out to build a palace.  For twelve months and thirty‑six days they laboured—Arun and Barun constructing, Kiranmala fetching water—until a wondrous marble palace rose, its doors of silver, its spires of gold, surrounded by flowering trees and fruit.  The palace shone so brightly that even the gods and demons marveled at it.

A wandering sanyasi praised the palace but said it still lacked three wonders: golden fruits on silver trees, a waterfall of pearl‑water, and a golden bird singing in a diamond tree.  He described a distant “Mountain of Illusion” where such things grew and warned that only a great hero could obtain them.

Arun volunteered to go, giving his sword to Barun and Kiranmala as a token of life.  He left, promising to return.  After many days Barun took the sword to see if it had rusted; it had, and he realized Arun was dead.  He gave Kiranmala his bow and arrow and set out himself.  The sword’s rust and the broken bowstring told Kiranmala that both brothers were gone.

Barun reached the Mountain of Illusion, but the moment he turned to the chanting nymphs he turned to stone, and the same fate fell on Arun.  The brothers remained stone statues forever.

When Kiranmala saw the broken bow and rusted sword she did not weep.  She fed the cow, watered the plants, dressed in princely robes, took a crown and sword, and set out alone for the mountain.  After thirteen nights and thirty‑three days she arrived, only to be assaulted by countless demons, beasts and spirits.  She fought through drums, cymbals, arrows of illusion and violent storms, but never faltered.

At last she reached the base of the diamond tree.  The golden bird there welcomed her and, one by one, gave her the golden fruit, the spring water, the pearl‑fall pitcher, a drum and other treasures.  Kiranmala struck the drum; the mountain fell silent.  She then sprinkled the spring water over the stone princes.  Wherever the drops fell the petrified princes awakened—hundreds of thousands of princes from ages past rose, bowing to her as “blessed hero of seven ages.”  Arun and Barun opened their eyes, wept, and called her their blessed sister.

Returning home, the three siblings tended Kajallata, cleaned the courtyard and planted the silver tree and the branch of the diamond tree.  They opened the pearl‑water pitcher and scattered the pearl‑fruits.  The diamond tree grew tall, the silver tree spread glossy leaves, golden fruits swayed, the golden bird sang, and pearl‑water cascaded down.  The forest birds and deer flocked to the palace, calling it “the city of Indra on earth.”

The king, hearing the marvel, came with his retinue.  He was astonished by the wealth of gold, pearls, emeralds and diamonds that filled every room, and by plates made of gold coins.  He asked how he could eat such food; the golden bird answered rhetorically, exposing a lie the king’s sisters‑in‑law had told him about puppies, kittens and wooden dolls.  Realising the truth, the king embraced the siblings, wept and asked them to bring their mother, who lived in a hut across the river.  The siblings fetched her; seeing her children “in heaven,” she rejoiced.

The golden bird sang their praise, and the kingdom celebrated.  The king moved his court to the siblings’ palace; for seven days and seven nights the people played amid jewels, pearls and emeralds.  Later, executioners burned the houses of a grass‑cutter and a cook and buried the queen’s two wicked sisters under thorny stakes.  After that, the king, his queen, Arun, Barun, Kiranmala and their descendants ruled over countless fortunes for ages, their palace standing as a beacon of wonder in the three worlds.
