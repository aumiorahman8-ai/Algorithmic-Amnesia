**The Tale of Dh’bi**

Long ago an elderly couple, who had no son, were blessed with a single daughter. Their child’s face shone like a fairy’s, and they named her **Dh’bi**. The couple cultivated a lush jhum field that yielded abundant rice, sesame, pumpkins, fruits and vegetables, and they lived happily.

One scorching day in the month of Jyeshtha, while weeding the fresh green rice seedlings, the old man and woman grew thirsty. All the streams and canals had dried up. The old woman found a shallow puddle left by deer tracks and drank, but the water only made her thirst worse. The old man discovered clean water in a crab’s hole, but when his wife begged for a sip, he shouted the word **“Dur ho!”**—the Chakma word for tortoise. Because it was the Age of Truth, his words became reality: the old woman turned into a huge tortoise and waddled into the river beside their house.  

The old man, horrified, returned home and, when his daughter asked where her mother was, told a comforting lie that she would return that evening. She waited all night, then the next day the father told her the truth: the mother had become a tortoise living in the river and would never return. When Dh’bi wept at the riverbank, the tortoise rose, spoke to her, and promised that whenever the girl clapped her hands on the bank the tortoise would surface to comfort her. From then on Dh’bi visited the river whenever she missed her mother, and the tortoise appeared and gave her counsel.

With his wife gone, the old man could no longer tend the jhum alone. Skilled in cane‑work, he began cutting bamboo and selling winnowing fans, tongs and other cane products, wandering from village to village with his daughter.

In a neighboring village lived another old woman, widow of many years, and her daughter **K’bi**—the same age as Dh’bi. When the old man came to sell his wares, the neighbour’s wife took a liking to him and kept inviting him home, offering cool water, food and a place to stay. The old man always refused, saying his own daughter would cry if he left her.

One night the neighbour’s wife forced the old man to stay, hid his unsold wares, and tied a knot between his clothing and hers while he slept. When the rooster crowed, the old woman pretended anger, but the knot forced the old man to marry her. Thus Dh’bi gained a cruel step‑mother and a jealous stepsister.

The step‑mother made Dh’bi a servant, assigning endless chores and beating her for the slightest mistake. Dh’bi could not confide in her father, so she fled to the riverbank, where her tortoise‑mother comforted her: “Endure it; it is your fate.”  

Learning that her step‑mother’s co‑wife lived as a tortoise, the step‑mother plotted to catch her. She set fish‑traps upstream, nets downstream and hooks on the banks. Dh’bi warned her mother in time, so the tortoise escaped, but eventually, driven by hunger, the tortoise ventured near the bank and was caught on a hook. The step‑mother dragged it out, ordered Dh’bi to roast it, and the girl, torn between love and terror, boiled the tortoise while it screamed pleas to its daughter. The step‑mother laughed and cooked the meat, but a voice rose from the pot, “Bobog bobog… I will eat the co‑wife’s head.” Frightened, the step‑mother dumped the stew; a gourd seed sprouted from the waste, grew into a massive vine that struck her on the forehead each day, then a banyan sapling grew from the ash of a later stew, its branches later giving Dh’bi cool shade while she husked paddy.

The step‑mother later fell ill with a bone‑breaking disease and demanded tiger’s milk; K’bi pretended to slip, forcing Dh’bi to go into the forest alone. There a tigress, pleased to be called “Auntie,” gave Dh’bi milk and a bundle of silk cloth. Dh’bi hid the gifts in a hollow of the banyan tree before returning. The step‑mother again feigned illness, this time needing snake’s milk; the same trick sent Dh’bi to a giant snake, which, after being called “Auntie,” offered milk and a trove of jewellery. Again Dh’bi stored the treasure in the banyan’s hollow.
