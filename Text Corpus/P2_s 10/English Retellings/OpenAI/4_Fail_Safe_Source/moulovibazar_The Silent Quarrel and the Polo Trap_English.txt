The story opens with a husband and wife who have been arguing. Their quarrel is so sharp that for two whole days they have not exchanged a single word. While they sit in silence, a stream of villagers passes by their house, each carrying a “polo trap” and heading toward the wetlands to go fishing.

Seeing the villagers leave, the wife thinks that perhaps she can use the occasion to get her husband to go fishing as well. Yet, because of their ongoing dispute, she cannot simply ask him outright. Instead, the two begin a terse, indirect dialogue.

The wife starts the exchange by remarking on the departing crowd:

- **Wife:** “All the folks are off with their polo traps to the fen; but who is there to go for me? Which of the men?”

The husband replies, pointing out his own hunger and lack of food:

- **Husband:** “I haven’t had a bite nor a touch of food, you see; so how am I supposed to go? You tell me.”

Trying to draw his attention to the food already prepared at home, the wife says:

- **Wife:** “The rice and curry are in the shika pot, right there; why don’t you serve yourself and have your share?”

The husband, however, is confused by the state of the food:

- **Husband:** “Some grains are long, some are short—it's a mess complete; how, tell me, am I supposed to eat?”

Finally, the wife delivers a cutting retort, urging him to swallow his own insults along with the food:

- **Wife:** “Just mould a mouthful atop your insults, and swallow it whole!”

Through this brief, sharp exchange, the story shows how the couple’s anger has reduced their communication to riddles and barbs, even as the everyday world outside—villagers with their fishing traps—continues unabated. The wife’s attempt to involve her husband in the fishing task is met with stubbornness and sarcasm, leaving the quarrel unresolved.
