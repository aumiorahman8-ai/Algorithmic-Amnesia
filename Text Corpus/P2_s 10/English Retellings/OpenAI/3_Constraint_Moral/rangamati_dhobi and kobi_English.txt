**The Dhobi and the Kobi**

In a Bengali village a celebrated *kobi* (poet) arrived at the dhobi’s (washerman’s) stall, bragging that his verses made the gods weep. He asked the dhobi to wash his fine silk, then, with a smirk, demanded the dhobi compose a poem that could equal his own.

The dhobi, a humble man with calloused hands, set the poet’s clothes in the river and began his work. While the water swirled, he muttered:

*“Rivers wash away dust,  
yet they keep the moon’s reflection;  
so a proud heart may shine,  
but must first be humbled by the tide.”*

The poet scoffed, “That’s mere chatter, not art!” The dhobi replied, “A verse is not the flourish of language alone, but the truth it bears. Even a simple washer knows the rhythm of life—rising with the sun, laboring till night, sharing laughter with his wife. That rhythm is poetry.”

The poet, humbled, listened as the dhobi described the work, the longing of his wife, the scent of fresh laundry—a world far from lofty courts yet rich in feeling. The dhobi’s verses, plain and sincere, moved the poet to tears.

**Moral**

True poetry springs from honesty and experience, not from lofty pretension. Pride blinds the wise; humility opens the heart to learning. Every occupation holds its own wisdom, and the meek can teach the proud more than wealth or fame ever could.
