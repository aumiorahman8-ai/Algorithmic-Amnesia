Long ago, in a place called Naran, lived five hundred families. The village was difficult for birds to fly over and was home to a priest named Darbasing, who was constantly busy performing rituals for each family. He had two daughters, Khumpuiti, the elder, and Khumbarti, the younger and more beautiful.

Each year, the villagers cleared land for Jhum cultivation, but Darbasing never helped, focusing solely on his religious duties. Khumpuiti and Khumbarti worked tirelessly in the Jhum fields, facing harsh conditions without shelter. As the harvest neared, they worried about storage and protection from animals. Khumpuiti, in her despair, wished for help, even from a creature of the jungle, and vowed to marry whoever aided them.

A large, multi-colored snake appeared and offered its help, transforming into a human form. It agreed to assist in exchange for daily rice and a promise of secrecy. The snake built a shelter, cleared the fields, and protected the crops. Khumpuiti provided the snake with rice from her own portion, fearing discovery.

Darbasing grew concerned about Khumpuiti’s declining health and questioned Khumbarti. After much persuasion, Khumbarti revealed the snake’s help and the conditions attached. Darbasing, fearing misfortune, decided to kill the snake.

He tricked Khumbarti into luring the snake with a feast, then ambushed and killed it. Khumbarti gathered the snake’s remains and scattered them throughout the fields. When Khumpuiti returned home, she discovered the pieces and, heartbroken, fashioned them into a garland and went to the river. She called for her snake-husband, and the river rose, carrying her away.

Khumbarti followed her sister’s instructions and sat on a banyan tree, where she was discovered by the king’s hunters. They brought her to the king, who was captivated by her beauty and married her. She bore him seven children – six sons and a daughter – but the other six queens falsely claimed she had given birth to puppies and kittens, and she was banished to herd ducks.

The children, however, were secretly protected by the river and grew up there. Eventually, they revealed to the king that their mother could feed all seven of them with milk from her breast. The king tested all the women in the kingdom, and only Khumbarti could fulfill this condition. 

The truth was revealed, the deceitful queens were exiled, and Khumbarti and her children were restored to the palace, living happily ever after.
