## The Silent Quarrel and the Polo Trap: A Bengali Folktale

This tale comes from the villages of Bengal, a story whispered around cooking fires and told to children to teach them the dangers of pride and the importance of communication. It's about two brothers, Rohan and Kamal, both renowned for their skill at *Polo* (a traditional stick-and-ball game played on horseback, very popular in Bengal historically).

Rohan was the elder, known for his powerful strikes and unwavering focus. Kamal, though younger, was celebrated for his cunning, agility, and the way he could anticipate his opponent’s moves. They were the pride of their village, but a simmering rivalry always lay beneath their brotherly affection.

One year, the local Raja (king) announced a grand Polo tournament. The prize? A magnificent stallion, the fastest in the kingdom, and the honor of becoming the Raja’s personal Polo player. Both Rohan and Kamal were determined to win.

The tournament began, and both brothers played brilliantly. They were evenly matched, each countering the other’s moves with skill and grace. But instead of celebrating each other’s good plays, a silent competition took root. They refused to acknowledge each other, offering only cold nods or pointedly ignoring each other’s successes. 

This wasn’t a quarrel of words, but a quarrel of *silence*. Each brother believed himself superior and refused to offer even a gesture of respect to the other. They were so consumed by their pride that they stopped playing *as a team*, even though they were on the same side in some matches.  They’d pass the ball grudgingly, or deliberately try to outshine each other, even if it meant jeopardizing their team’s chances.

As the tournament progressed, a cunning and jealous rival, a man named Jagat, noticed their silent feud. Jagat wasn’t as skilled as Rohan or Kamal individually, but he was a master of trickery. He devised a plan to exploit their pride.

He approached the Raja and suggested a special challenge: a one-on-one Polo match, played not on horseback, but *inside a large, cleverly constructed trap*. This trap was a circular pit, lined with smooth, slippery mud. The goal was to hit a ball into a small net in the center, using only their Polo sticks.  It was a test of balance, precision, and quick thinking.

The Raja, intrigued by the novelty, agreed. He announced that the final match would be between Rohan and Kamal, played within Jagat’s “Polo Trap.”

Rohan and Kamal, blinded by their rivalry, readily accepted. They saw it as a chance to definitively prove their superiority.  They didn’t realize Jagat’s true intention.

When they entered the trap, they quickly discovered its treacherous nature. The mud was incredibly slippery, making it almost impossible to maintain their footing.  Each brother struggled to stay upright, let alone hit the ball accurately. 

But instead of helping each other, or even offering a warning, they continued their silent battle. Rohan, attempting a powerful shot, slipped and nearly fell. Kamal, instead of offering a hand, smirked, seeing an opportunity to gain an advantage.  Kamal then tried a clever maneuver, but also lost his balance.

They tumbled and slid, each blaming the other with their eyes, but uttering no words.  They were too proud to ask for help, too focused on winning to acknowledge their shared predicament.

Jagat, watching from above, laughed silently. He had designed the trap not just to be difficult, but to be *impossible* to conquer alone.  He knew their pride would prevent them from cooperating.

Finally, exhausted and covered in mud, both brothers lay defeated, unable to even lift their Polo sticks.  Jagat stepped forward and claimed the stallion, declaring that neither brother had proven worthy.

The Raja, initially amused, grew angry when he realized Jagat’s deception. He questioned Jagat, who confessed his plan to exploit the brothers’ rivalry. 

The Raja then turned to Rohan and Kamal.  “You are both skilled players,” he said sternly. “But your pride and your silence have cost you the prize, and brought shame upon your village.  A true champion knows the value of humility and cooperation.”

Rohan and Kamal, humbled and ashamed, finally spoke to each other. They apologized for their foolish pride and acknowledged their mistake. They realized that their silent quarrel had not only cost them the stallion but had also nearly led to their downfall.

The Raja, seeing their remorse, allowed them to share the honor of becoming his Polo players, but only on the condition that they always play with respect and cooperation. 

And so, Rohan and Kamal learned a valuable lesson: that even the greatest skill is useless without humility, and that a silent quarrel can be more dangerous than any spoken word.  The Polo Trap became a reminder throughout the village – a symbol of the folly of pride and the power of unity.



This tale is often told with variations, but the core message remains the same:  **Pride blinds, silence divides, and cooperation conquers.** It’s a story that continues to resonate in Bengal, reminding people to value relationships and communication above all else.
