## The Pitha Tree and Rakkhoshi: A Bengali Folktale

Long ago, in a small village nestled beside a dense forest, lived a young man named Haru. He was known for his kindness and skill, but also for his terrible luck with marriage. Three times he’d been betrothed, and three times his brides had died shortly after the wedding, leaving him heartbroken and feared by many. People whispered he was cursed, a bringer of misfortune to women.

Haru, weary of loneliness, decided to leave the village and make a life for himself in the forest. He built a small hut and lived a simple life, farming and hunting. One day, while clearing land, he stumbled upon a strange tree. It wasn't like any tree he'd ever seen. Instead of leaves, it bore delicious *pithas* – sweet, rice-flour cakes – of all kinds! There were *pakan pitha*, *chitoi pitha*, *patishapta*… a never-ending supply of delectable treats.

Haru was overjoyed. He ate his fill and realized this was a gift from the gods. He continued to live happily, sharing the *pithas* with passing travelers and animals.  He was no longer lonely, content with the bounty of the Pitha Tree.

However, the sweet scent of the *pithas* drifted on the wind, reaching the nose of Rakkhoshi, a fearsome female demon who lived deep within the forest. Rakkhoshi was known for her insatiable appetite and cruel nature.  She followed the scent to Haru’s hut and was astonished by the Pitha Tree. 

“What is this wondrous tree?” she boomed, her voice shaking the leaves.

Haru, terrified, explained how he’d found it and how it provided him with food. Rakkhoshi, seeing a limitless supply of sweets, decided she *must* have the tree for herself.  She proposed marriage to Haru.

“Marry me,” she said, her voice dripping with false sweetness, “and I will protect you and this tree. We will live in luxury, feasting on *pithas* forever!”

Haru, remembering his past misfortunes, was hesitant. He knew Rakkhoshi was a demon, but the thought of another lonely life was daunting.  He reluctantly agreed, hoping against hope that this time things would be different.

They were married, and Rakkhoshi moved into Haru’s hut.  At first, she seemed content, gorging herself on *pithas* day and night. But soon, her greed grew. She wanted *all* the *pithas* for herself. She forbade Haru from eating them, and she began to guard the tree fiercely, chasing away anyone who came near.

Haru was miserable. He was forced to work tirelessly, tending to the tree while Rakkhoshi ate and ate and ate. He realized he’d made a terrible mistake. He missed sharing the *pithas* and longed for a peaceful life.

One day, a wise old woman from Haru’s village, hearing of his marriage, came to visit. She saw his unhappiness and, recognizing the danger he was in, asked him about the Pitha Tree. Haru told her everything.

The old woman listened intently and then said, “Rakkhoshi’s power comes from her insatiable hunger.  You must trick her into feeling full, truly full, so she loses her strength.”

She instructed Haru to prepare a special *pitha* – a large, hollow one filled with chili peppers and a powerful sleeping potion.  

Haru, following her instructions, presented the *pitha* to Rakkhoshi.  She, greedy as ever, devoured it in one gulp.  At first, she enjoyed the sweetness, but then the chili peppers hit. She screamed and sputtered, her mouth burning.  Then, the sleeping potion took effect.  Rakkhoshi collapsed into a deep, magical sleep.

As she slept, her monstrous form began to shrink and fade.  She transformed into a small, harmless lizard and scurried away into the forest, her power gone.

Haru was free! He continued to live by the Pitha Tree, sharing its bounty with everyone.  He learned that kindness and cleverness were stronger than any curse, and that true happiness came not from avoiding loneliness, but from sharing joy with others.  And the Pitha Tree continued to bear its sweet gifts, a reminder of Haru’s courage and the defeat of the greedy Rakkhoshi.



This tale is often told to emphasize the importance of wit over brute force, and the dangers of greed. It also highlights the Bengali love for *pithas* and their cultural significance.  Different versions of the story exist, with variations in the details, but the core message remains the same.
