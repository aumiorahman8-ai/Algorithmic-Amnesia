## The Cunning Mother: A Bengali Folktale of Wit and Wisdom

This is the story of Shanti, a woman renowned for her intelligence, and her two sons – her own, Gopal, and her stepson, Haru, born of her husband’s first wife. Shanti, though initially resentful of Haru, decided a different path than outright malice. She resolved to raise *both* boys to be successful, but in a way that ensured her own son, Gopal, would always be considered the brighter, more capable one.  Her strategy, however, was far more subtle and clever than simple favoritism.

Haru’s father, a merchant, was often away on trade. While he was gone, Shanti managed the household and the boys’ upbringing. She noticed Haru was naturally strong and quick to learn practical skills – he could mend tools, understand the workings of the farm, and was a gifted fisherman. Gopal, however, was more bookish and needed constant encouragement. 

Shanti began her plan. Whenever Haru excelled at something, she would praise him, but then immediately add a caveat. “Haru, that’s a wonderfully strong net you’ve woven! It’s a shame you lack the refinement to understand the intricacies of trade, though. Gopal, now *he* understands value and profit.”  Or, “Haru, you caught so many fish! But Gopal can calculate the best price to sell them for, ensuring we get the most benefit.”

She consistently framed Haru’s strengths as lacking in crucial areas, and Gopal’s weaknesses as potential for greatness.  She’d ask Haru to perform physically demanding tasks, then praise his strength, but immediately follow it with a question for Gopal about the *why* of the task, the logistics, the planning.  

For example, she’d send Haru to the market to sell vegetables. He’d return with all the produce sold, but Shanti would scold him, “You sold them all, yes, but did you haggle? Did you charm the customers? Gopal, tell me, how *would* you approach selling these vegetables to maximize our earnings?” Gopal, prompted, would offer a detailed, if theoretical, plan. Shanti would beam, “See? Gopal understands the art of commerce!”

This pattern continued for years. Haru became incredibly skilled in all practical matters, a master craftsman and provider. But in the eyes of the villagers, and especially in the eyes of his father upon his return, he was simply “strong and capable,” lacking the intelligence and foresight of Gopal. Gopal, though less naturally gifted, was lauded as a brilliant strategist and a future leader.

One day, the King announced a competition. He needed a solution to a complex problem: a neighboring kingdom was diverting the river that watered their lands, causing a severe drought. The King promised a great reward – land, wealth, and the hand of his daughter – to whoever could solve the crisis.

Gopal, encouraged by Shanti, confidently presented a detailed, theoretical plan based on his studies. It was elegant and well-reasoned, but ultimately impractical. It required resources the kingdom didn’t have and relied on assumptions that proved false.

Haru, hesitant at first, was urged by the villagers to offer his solution. He had observed the river’s flow for years, understood the land, and had a practical, though seemingly simple, plan: to build a series of strategically placed dams and canals to redirect a smaller tributary to irrigate the parched fields. 

The King, skeptical of Haru’s lack of formal education, initially dismissed his idea. But desperate, he allowed Haru to try.  And it worked.  The tributary was redirected, the fields were watered, and the kingdom was saved.

Haru was hailed as a hero.  The King, humbled, offered him his daughter’s hand and a vast estate.  Haru, however, politely declined the princess, explaining he wasn’t suited for court life. He accepted the land, using his wealth to improve the lives of the villagers.

When Gopal’s father returned and learned of Haru’s success, he was astonished. He questioned Shanti, who finally confessed her elaborate scheme.  She hadn’t wanted Haru to fail, she explained, but she wanted Gopal to be seen as the more brilliant son.

The father, though initially angry at Shanti’s manipulation, ultimately understood her motivations. He realized Haru’s true worth, a worth that couldn’t be measured by books or clever words.  He praised Haru’s ingenuity and practicality, and acknowledged Shanti’s cunning, though he gently chided her for her deception.

The tale of the Cunning Mother serves as a reminder that true intelligence comes in many forms, and that judging worth based on narrow definitions can blind us to genuine talent. It also highlights the power of strategic upbringing, though it cautions against using it for selfish gain.  Ultimately, the story celebrates Haru’s quiet strength and resourcefulness, proving that practical wisdom can often triumph over theoretical brilliance.
