## The Tale of Ghumonto Puri (The Wandering City)

The story of Ghumonto Puri is a classic Bengali folktale, a cautionary tale about greed, pride, and the importance of contentment. It's a long story, often told over several sittings, but here's a retelling:

Long ago, in a lush, fertile land, lived a poor but honest farmer named Haru. He toiled tirelessly in his fields, but barely managed to feed his family. One day, while ploughing, his plough struck something hard. He dug and unearthed a beautiful, golden bell. 

The bell wasn't just beautiful; it possessed a magical quality. When rung, it would summon Ghumonto Puri, a magnificent, shimmering city filled with unimaginable wealth. Haru, overwhelmed, rang the bell. Instantly, before his eyes, a city of gold and jewels materialized, overflowing with riches beyond his wildest dreams.

Initially, Haru and his family lived in bliss. They ate the finest foods, wore the most exquisite clothes, and enjoyed a life of luxury. But Haru wasn't content. He began to crave more. He wanted to be the most powerful and respected man in the land. 

He started inviting the local zamindar (landlord), a proud and arrogant man named Rajendra Sen, to visit Ghumonto Puri. Rajendra Sen was initially impressed, but quickly grew envious of Haru’s wealth. He tried to subtly belittle Haru, pointing out his humble origins and suggesting he wasn't worthy of such riches.

Haru, wanting to impress the zamindar further, began to ring the bell more and more frequently, summoning Ghumonto Puri for lavish feasts and displays of wealth. He built grand palaces within the city, filled them with treasures, and hosted extravagant parties. He even started demanding respect from the villagers, forgetting his own humble beginnings.

Rajendra Sen, consumed by jealousy, plotted to steal the bell. He befriended Haru, showering him with false praise, and eventually learned the secret of summoning the city. One night, while Haru slept, Rajendra Sen crept into his house and stole the golden bell.

Overjoyed, Rajendra Sen rang the bell, expecting Ghumonto Puri to appear for *him*. But instead of a glorious city, a dilapidated, crumbling version of Ghumonto Puri materialized. The buildings were broken, the streets were dusty, and the treasures were tarnished and worthless. 

Confused and furious, Rajendra Sen rang the bell again and again, but each time, the city appeared more and more ruined. He realized with horror that Ghumonto Puri wasn't a fixed entity. It reflected the character of the person who summoned it. 

Haru, discovering the theft, understood what had happened. He confronted Rajendra Sen, who, humbled and ashamed, confessed his greed and envy.  He returned the bell to Haru, begging for forgiveness.

Haru, though wronged, was a kind man. He forgave Rajendra Sen, but explained the lesson he had learned. He rang the bell one last time.  Ghumonto Puri appeared, not as grand as before, but still beautiful and prosperous. 

However, Haru didn't indulge in luxury. He used the wealth to help his village, providing food, shelter, and education for those in need. He lived a comfortable life, but remained humble and grateful. 

Finally, Haru realized that true happiness didn't lie in material wealth, but in contentment and kindness. He decided to relinquish the bell, throwing it into the deepest part of the river. Ghumonto Puri vanished, never to be seen again. 

**The moral of the story is clear:** Greed and pride lead to ruin. True wealth lies not in possessions, but in a good heart and a contented spirit.  Ghumonto Puri serves as a reminder that what we create, and what we receive, is a reflection of who we are.



This tale is often told with variations depending on the region and storyteller, but the core message remains consistent. It's a beloved story in Bengal, passed down through generations, reminding people to value humility and generosity above all else.
