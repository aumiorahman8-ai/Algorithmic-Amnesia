## The Princess, The Python, and The Four Brothers: A Bengali Folktale

Once upon a time, in a kingdom nestled beside a vast, dark forest, lived a beautiful princess named Chandrabati. She was known throughout the land for her kindness and grace, but also for a peculiar curse. Every night, as the moon rose, she would transform into a magnificent, but terrifying, python!

The King, heartbroken and desperate, declared a reward beyond measure for anyone who could break the curse. Many princes and brave warriors tried, offering gifts, performing rituals, and even battling the python-princess, but all failed. The python, though not malicious, was powerful and none could subdue her.

In a small village bordering the forest lived four brothers: Rohan, the strongest; Kamal, the cleverest; Dipen, the most courageous; and Arun, the most devoted. They were poor but bound by a deep love for each other. Hearing of the King’s plight and the immense reward, they decided to try their luck, each believing their unique strength could succeed where others had failed.

Rohan, confident in his physical power, went first. He found the python-princess coiled around a giant banyan tree. He wrestled with her, straining every muscle, but the python’s scales were too strong, her grip too tight. He was eventually forced to retreat, bruised and defeated.

Next came Kamal, the cleverest. He brought with him a cage woven from enchanted vines, hoping to trap the python. He used riddles and illusions to lure her closer, but the princess, even in her serpentine form, possessed a sharp mind. She saw through his tricks and easily broke free of the cage, amused by his efforts.

Dipen, the most courageous, approached the python-princess with a sword drawn. He vowed to slay the beast, believing that only by destroying the creature could he save the princess. But as he raised his sword, he saw the sadness in the python’s eyes, the hint of a human soul trapped within. He couldn’t bring himself to strike, realizing this wasn’t a monster, but a cursed woman. He lowered his sword and retreated, humbled.

Finally, it was Arun’s turn. He carried no weapons, no traps, no grand plans. He simply sat before the python-princess and began to sing. He sang songs of kindness, of compassion, of understanding. He sang of the beauty of the moon, the peace of the forest, and the longing for freedom. He sang not *to* the python, but *with* her, pouring his heart into every note.

As he sang, the python-princess slowly uncoiled. She listened intently, her head resting near Arun’s feet.  The music seemed to soothe her tormented spirit.  Arun sang for hours, until the first rays of dawn touched the forest floor. 

As the sun rose, a miraculous transformation began. The scales shimmered, the body shrunk, and before Arun’s eyes, the magnificent python turned back into the beautiful Princess Chandrabati. 

The curse was broken, not by strength, cleverness, or courage, but by pure, selfless devotion and the power of heartfelt music.  

Chandrabati, overjoyed, explained that the curse had been placed upon her by a jealous sorceress. It could only be broken by someone who saw beyond the monstrous form and recognized the suffering within, someone who offered not force, but compassion.

The King, overjoyed at his daughter’s return, offered the reward to all four brothers. But Chandrabati insisted that Arun deserved it all.  She chose him as her husband, not for his strength or skill, but for his kind heart and the music that had freed her. 

And so, the Princess, the Python, and the Four Brothers lived happily ever after, a testament to the power of love, compassion, and the enduring magic of a simple song.
