Okay, here's a retelling of the Bengali folktale, "The Consequence of a Broken Promise" (often known as "Chand Sadagar's Curse" or variations thereof). It's a long story, so I'll aim for a comprehensive but readable version.  I'll also include notes at the end about variations you might encounter.

**The Tale of Chand Sadagar and the Broken Vow**

Chand Sadagar was a wealthy merchant, renowned throughout Bengal for his ships, his trade, and his immense fortune. He was a powerful man, respected and feared in equal measure. However, Chand Sadagar was also known for his arrogance and a certain…lack of piety. He believed his wealth was solely due to his own cleverness and hard work, and he paid little heed to the gods or the needs of others.

One day, Chand Sadagar was preparing to send a fleet of ships laden with goods to distant lands. A wandering *sadhu* (holy man), old and frail, approached him, begging for alms. Chand Sadagar, busy with his preparations, dismissed the sadhu with a curt wave and a handful of copper coins – a paltry sum for a man of his wealth.

The sadhu, though outwardly calm, was deeply offended. He warned Chand Sadagar, "You amass wealth, but you have no compassion. Your heart is as hard as stone. Beware, merchant! The sea is a powerful goddess. If you fail to honor a vow made to her, she will take everything from you."

Chand Sadagar scoffed. "The sea? A goddess? I control the sea with my ships! My wealth is my protection." He paid no further attention to the sadhu, who quietly moved on.

As his ships were about to set sail, a terrible storm brewed.  Chand Sadagar, fearing for his investment, impulsively cried out, "Goddess of the Sea! If you calm this storm and allow my ships to sail safely, I will send you the most beautiful *sindoor* (vermilion powder, worn by married women) in all of Bengal, worth a king’s ransom!"

The storm miraculously subsided. The sea became calm, and Chand Sadagar’s ships sailed smoothly, returning with a massive profit. He quickly forgot his vow, attributing the calm seas to luck and his own skill. He indulged in lavish celebrations, spending his newfound wealth on himself and ignoring the sadhu’s warning.

Years passed. Chand Sadagar continued to prosper, sending ship after ship across the oceans. He married again and again, each wife bringing him more wealth and status. He had twelve sons, all strong and capable.  But the memory of his promise to the sea goddess faded with each passing year.

Then, tragedy struck. One by one, Chand Sadagar’s sons began to die in mysterious accidents at sea.  First, the eldest son, then another, and another. Each loss was a devastating blow, but Chand Sadagar, blinded by his pride, refused to see the connection to his broken vow. He blamed fate, bad luck, or the incompetence of his captains.

Finally, only his youngest son, Dhananjay, remained. Dhananjay was a kind and pious young man, unlike his father. He pleaded with Chand Sadagar to remember the promise made to the sea goddess and to fulfill it.

"Father," Dhananjay said, "The goddess is angered by your broken vow. She is taking our family as payment. We must appease her!"

Chand Sadagar, consumed by grief and fear for his last son, finally relented. He ordered the finest *sindoor* to be prepared – a magnificent offering, far exceeding anything he had initially promised. He instructed Dhananjay to take the *sindoor* and sail to the deepest part of the sea to offer it to the goddess.

Dhananjay set sail with the precious *sindoor*. As he reached the designated spot, a terrifying whirlpool formed.  He was about to cast the *sindoor* into the vortex when a voice boomed from the depths of the sea.

“This *sindoor* is beautiful, but it is not enough. You promised the *most* beautiful *sindoor* in all of Bengal. Your father’s heart is still filled with pride. He believes wealth can buy forgiveness.  He must come himself and offer the *sindoor* with a truly humble heart.”

Dhananjay returned to shore and relayed the goddess’s message to his father. Chand Sadagar, now broken and desperate, agreed to go himself. He boarded a small boat with Dhananjay and the *sindoor*.

As they approached the same spot, the sea began to rage once more.  A monstrous wave rose, threatening to engulf them. Chand Sadagar, trembling with fear, finally understood the gravity of his sin. He threw the *sindoor* into the churning waters, and with tears streaming down his face, he begged the goddess for forgiveness.

But the goddess was not appeased.  She revealed herself in a terrifying form, and declared, “Your pride has cost you everything. You valued wealth above all else, and you broke your word. As punishment, you and your entire city will be submerged beneath the waves!”

As Chand Sadagar watched in horror, the sea rose and swallowed his city whole.  He and Dhananjay were the only ones spared, clinging to a piece of driftwood. They drifted for days, lost and alone.

Finally, they were washed ashore on a distant, desolate island. Chand Sadagar, stripped of his wealth, his family, and his city, lived out the rest of his days in poverty and regret, a constant reminder of the consequence of a broken promise. Dhananjay, though saddened by their fate, remained devoted to his father and helped him survive.



**Variations and Notes:**

*   **The City's Name:** The city is often named Champaknagar or Swarnapur (Golden City).
*   **The Number of Sons:** The number of sons varies, sometimes being seven, sometimes more.
*   **The Sadhu's Form:** The sadhu is sometimes described as a disguised form of the sea goddess herself, testing Chand Sadagar's piety.
*   **The Offering:** Sometimes the offering isn't just *sindoor* but also a golden temple or other valuable items.
*   **Dhananjay's Role:** In some versions, Dhananjay is instrumental in discovering the reason for the misfortunes and actively seeks a way to appease the goddess.
* **Moral of the Story:** The core moral remains consistent: the importance of keeping promises, humility, and respecting the divine.  Pride and greed lead to ruin.



This tale is a powerful reminder of the importance of integrity and the consequences of disrespecting the natural world and the divine. It's a story that has been passed down through generations in Bengal, serving as a cautionary tale and a testament to the enduring power of folklore.
