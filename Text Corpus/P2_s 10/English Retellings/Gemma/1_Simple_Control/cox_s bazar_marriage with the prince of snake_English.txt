## The Tale of Chandrabati and the Snake Prince

This is the story of Chandrabati, a tale whispered across Bengal for generations, a story of courage, cleverness, and a love that defied expectations.

Chandrabati was the youngest of seven sisters, daughters of a wealthy merchant. They lived a comfortable life, but their happiness was shadowed by a terrible curse. Every night, a fearsome snake would visit their home and demand one sister as his bride. The sisters lived in constant terror, knowing their turn would come.

One by one, the older sisters were taken. Each night, a beautiful palanquin would descend from the sky, carried by invisible hands. The chosen sister would be adorned in jewels and taken away, never to be seen again.  The merchant, heartbroken and helpless, could do nothing to protect his daughters.

Finally, it was Chandrabati’s turn. She was the most beautiful and intelligent of the sisters, but also the most afraid.  Unlike her sisters who wept and begged, Chandrabati decided to be brave. She wouldn't simply accept her fate. 

Before being taken, she made a series of conditions to the snake.  "If I am to be your wife," she declared, "I have three requests. First, I must be able to return to my father's house once a year to visit. Second, I must be able to summon you whenever I desire, simply by calling your name. And third, you must promise never to reveal your true form to anyone but me."

The snake prince, captivated by her boldness and beauty, agreed to all her conditions. He was, after all, a powerful Naga prince named Behula, cursed to live as a snake until he found a wife who could love him for who he was, not for his power or wealth.

Chandrabati was taken to Behula’s magnificent underwater palace, a realm of shimmering pearls and coral. She lived in luxury, attended by countless servants, but she missed her family terribly.  

True to his word, Behula allowed Chandrabati to visit her father once a year.  Each year, she would return, bringing gifts from the underwater world, and her father would rejoice to see her. However, Chandrabati always felt a pang of sadness, knowing she couldn't truly share her life with her family.

One year, Chandrabati’s sisters, consumed by jealousy and curiosity, followed her. They wanted to see this wondrous palace and understand how their youngest sister lived.  They secretly observed as Chandrabati returned to the underwater realm.

When Behula came to greet his wife, Chandrabati, overcome with longing, called his name – “Behula! Behula!” – and he appeared before her.  Her sisters, hidden nearby, gasped.  They saw Behula transform from a magnificent snake into a handsome, regal prince. 

Behula, remembering his promise, was furious. He had been betrayed.  He cursed Chandrabati’s sisters, turning them into trees, forever rooted to the spot where they had broken his trust. 

Chandrabati was devastated. She pleaded with Behula to forgive her sisters, but he was unyielding.  Finally, Chandrabati, heartbroken, made a desperate plea.  She offered to share her fate with him, to live with him permanently in the underwater world, if he would only lift the curse on her sisters.

Behula, deeply in love with Chandrabati, couldn't bear to see her suffer. He relented and lifted the curse, turning her sisters back into humans. However, they were left with a permanent reminder of their transgression – they were rendered mute, unable to speak.

Chandrabati and Behula then lived happily ever after in the underwater palace.  Chandrabati, through her courage and love, had broken the curse on Behula and saved her sisters, though at the cost of a life forever separated from the world she once knew.



**The moral of the story is often interpreted as:**

* **The power of courage and intelligence:** Chandrabati didn't succumb to fear, but used her wit to negotiate and ultimately save her family.
* **The importance of trust and loyalty:** The sisters’ betrayal led to their punishment.
* **The transformative power of love:** Behula’s love for Chandrabati ultimately led him to forgive and show compassion.



This tale is often performed as a *Jatra* (traditional Bengali folk theatre) and continues to be a beloved story in Bengal, reminding people of the strength and resilience of the human spirit.
