## Dalim Kumar: A Tale from Thakurmar Jhuli

Thakurma (grandmother) settled deeper into her rocking chair, the oil lamp casting dancing shadows on the walls. “Shono, shono (listen, listen),” she began, her voice a warm rumble, “Today I’ll tell you the story of Dalim Kumar, a tale as sweet as *rosogolla* and as thorny as a *babla* bush.”

Once upon a time, in a kingdom far, far away, lived a Raja (king) and a Rani (queen). They had no children for many years, and their hearts were heavy with sorrow. Finally, through the grace of *Iswar* (God) and the blessings of a *sadhu* (holy man), the Rani gave birth to a son. They named him Dalim Kumar, for his complexion was as ruby-red as the seeds of a pomegranate (*dalim*).

The Raja and Rani adored their *putra* (son). But fate, Thakurma sighed, is a fickle thing. When Dalim Kumar was just seven years old, the Rani fell gravely ill.  Despite the best efforts of the *baidyas* (doctors) and the chanting of *mantras* by the *purohit* (priest), she passed away. The Raja was heartbroken, and Dalim Kumar, a small, lost soul, clung to his *pita* (father).

Years passed. The Raja, consumed by grief, remarried.  The new Rani, however, was a woman with a heart as cold as winter. She disliked Dalim Kumar intensely, seeing him as a reminder of her husband’s first love. She had two daughters of her own, *meye shontan* (daughters), named Duti and Muti, whom she favored above all else. 

The step-Rani treated Dalim Kumar with cruelty. She made him do all the menial tasks – cleaning the stables, fetching water, and tending to the garden. She dressed him in rags and fed him leftovers, while her own daughters enjoyed silks and sweets.  Dalim Kumar, though kind and gentle, bore it all with patience, remembering his mother’s teachings. He found solace in the company of the palace animals, especially a beautiful white horse.

One day, the Raja announced a *shobha yatra* (procession) to celebrate the birthday of his daughters. He ordered that all the young princes and *rajkumar* (princes) from neighboring kingdoms be invited.  The step-Rani, eager to see her daughters married to powerful princes, prepared lavishly.  She forbade Dalim Kumar from even *dekha* (seeing) the procession.

“You are a servant boy, not a prince!” she sneered. “Stay hidden in the stables!”

But Dalim Kumar, drawn by the music and the excitement, peeked out.  As he watched, the white horse he cared for nudged him gently. Suddenly, a divine light enveloped the horse, and it transformed into a magnificent *ashwa* (horse) with wings!  

“Mount me, Dalim Kumar,” the horse said, its voice ringing with power. “I am a *devdoot* (divine messenger) sent by your mother.  I will take you to a faraway kingdom where you will find your fortune.”

Dalim Kumar, trusting in the divine, climbed onto the winged horse. They soared through the sky, leaving the palace and the cruel step-Rani far behind.  They landed in the kingdom of Raja Indrajit, a powerful and just ruler. 

Raja Indrajit was holding a *swayamvar* (a ceremony where a princess chooses her husband).  He had set a difficult task: whoever could string a heavy *dhanuk* (bow) would win the hand of his beautiful daughter, Rajkumari Chandrabala. Many princes tried, but none could even lift the bow.

Dalim Kumar, still dressed in rags, arrived at the *swayamvar*.  He was mocked and ridiculed by the other princes. But when he stepped forward and effortlessly strung the bow, a hush fell over the assembly.  Rajkumari Chandrabala, captivated by his strength and humility, chose him as her husband.

Raja Indrajit, impressed by Dalim Kumar’s courage and grace, welcomed him as his son-in-law.  Dalim Kumar ruled wisely and justly, earning the love of his people.  

News of Dalim Kumar’s success reached his step-Rani.  Consumed with envy and shame, she and her daughters traveled to Raja Indrajit’s kingdom, hoping to beg for forgiveness.  But Dalim Kumar, though forgiving, did not invite them to live with him. He understood that their hearts were unchanged.

Thakurma paused, adjusting her shawl. “And so, Dalim Kumar lived happily ever after with Rajkumari Chandrabala, always remembering his mother’s love and the kindness he showed to all creatures.  He proved that true worth lies not in riches or lineage, but in a good heart and a strong spirit.”

She smiled at the wide-eyed children gathered around her. “Remember this, *shona* (darling).  *Dharma* (righteousness) always triumphs over *adharmo* (unrighteousness). And even in the darkest of times, *Iswar* will always send help, sometimes in the most unexpected forms.”



This retelling attempts to capture the essence of the tale as found in Thakurmar Jhuli, including:

* **Kinship terms:** *Raja, Rani, putra, pita, meye shontan* are used throughout.
* **Cultural nuances:** References to *rosogolla, babla*, the importance of *Iswar*, *sadhu, baidya, purohit* and the significance of rituals like *shobha yatra, swayamvar* and chanting *mantras*.
* **Specific rituals:** The *swayamvar* is described with the bow-stringing challenge, a common trope in Bengali folktales.
* **Language:**  Incorporation of Bengali words and phrases like *shono, dekha, shona, dharma, adharmo* to maintain authenticity.
* **Moral of the story:**  The emphasis on righteousness, humility, and the eventual triumph of good over evil, central to Thakurmar Jhuli’s tales.
