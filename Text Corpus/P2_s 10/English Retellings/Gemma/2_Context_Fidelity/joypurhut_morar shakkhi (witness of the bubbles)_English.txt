## Morar Shakkhi: The Witness of the Bubbles (A Joypurhut Folktale)

The dust of Joypurhut clung to everything, even the stories. This one, *Morar Shakkhi*, is told mostly by *didimas* (paternal grandmothers) during the long, humid afternoons of *Barsha* (the monsoon season), when the rain drums on the tin roofs and the scent of *hathial* (hand-pounded rice) fills the air. It’s a tale about pride, loss, and the quiet wisdom of the river.

Old Man Morar, a *zamindar* (landlord) of some standing in the village of Kalicharan, was known for two things: his vast rice paddies and his even vaster ego. He had a beautiful daughter, Rupali, with eyes like polished *shona* (gold) and a laugh that could charm the birds from the trees.  He was fiercely protective of her, keeping her secluded within the *andar mahal* (inner quarters) of his grand house. 

Now, Morar had a peculiar habit. Every evening, after his *sandhya* (evening prayers), he’d sit by the Kulik River, blowing bubbles with a special pipe made of *shitol* wood. These weren’t just any bubbles. They were said to hold glimpses of the future, fleeting images of what *could be*.  He called himself the “Shakkhi” – the witness – to these bubble-visions.  He’d boast to his *barkandaaz* (accountant), old Haru, “See, Haru? I hold the fate of this village in my breath! I see the good harvests, the coming rains, even who will marry whom!”

Rupali, though confined, would often sneak to the riverbank, hidden amongst the *keora* trees, just to watch her father. She didn’t believe in the bubbles, but she loved the quiet moments with him. 

One day, a young man named Badal arrived in Kalicharan. He was a *baul* (wandering mystic singer), with a *ektara* (one-stringed instrument) slung across his shoulder and a voice that carried the sorrow and joy of the land. He sang of freedom, of love unbound by caste or wealth. Rupali, hearing his songs from the shadows of the *andar mahal*, fell deeply in love.

She began leaving small gifts for Badal – a handful of *murki* (sweetened puffed rice), a sprig of jasmine, a carefully folded *gamcha* (cotton towel). Badal, sensing a kindred spirit, would leave her verses of his songs, tucked into the hollow of a nearby mango tree.

Morar, however, noticed his daughter’s changed demeanor. He grew suspicious.  He blew furiously at his bubble pipe, demanding to see Rupali’s future.  The bubbles shimmered, then burst, showing him only fleeting images of Rupali laughing with a man… a man dressed in simple clothes, not a wealthy *mahajan’s* (money lender’s) son as Morar had envisioned.

Rage consumed him. He forbade Rupali from leaving the *andar mahal* and doubled the guard. He then focused all his energy on the bubbles, trying to force a different vision. He blew and blew, until his lips were raw and his head swam.  Finally, a bubble formed, larger and more vibrant than any before.  

But instead of a clear image, it showed… nothing. Just swirling colors, then a sudden, violent burst.  Morar, interpreting this as a sign of impending doom, became convinced Badal was a demon in disguise, sent to ruin his family.

He ordered his *lathials* (armed guards) to find Badal and bring him to him. They found the *baul* near the Kulik River and, without a trial, brutally beat him, leaving him for dead.

Rupali, witnessing the horror from a hidden window, was shattered.  She ran to the riverbank, weeping inconsolably.  She knelt by the water, begging the Kulik to take her life.  But the river, ancient and wise, didn’t grant her wish. Instead, it began to swell, rising rapidly with the force of the monsoon rains.

The Kulik flooded Kalicharan, washing away Morar’s rice paddies, his house, everything he held dear.  As the water rose around him, Morar saw bubbles forming on the surface, countless bubbles, each reflecting his own face, distorted and filled with regret.  He realized the bubbles hadn’t shown him the future, they had only shown him his own pride and blindness. 

He saw Rupali, clinging to a piece of driftwood, her face pale with grief. He tried to reach her, but the current was too strong.  He called out her name, but his voice was lost in the roar of the flood.

The flood eventually subsided. Rupali was rescued, but Morar was never found. Some say the Kulik River claimed him, a fitting end for a man who thought he could control fate. Others say he wanders the riverbank still, blowing bubbles, forever a *shakkhi* to his own folly.

And to this day, the people of Joypurhut say that if you sit by the Kulik River at dusk, you can see bubbles rising from the water, shimmering with the echoes of Morar’s pride and Rupali’s sorrow.  They serve as a reminder:  don't trust in fleeting visions, but in the enduring strength of love and the quiet wisdom of the river.  And never underestimate the power of a *baul’s* song.



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:** *Didima* (paternal grandmother), *Barkandaaz* (accountant), *Lathials* (armed guards).
* **Social Hierarchy:**  The distinction between *Zamindar* (landlord), *Mahajan* (money lender), and the wandering *Baul*.
* **Religious Practices:** *Sandhya* (evening prayers).
* **Food & Objects:** *Hathial* (hand-pounded rice), *Shona* (gold), *Shitol* wood, *Murki* (sweetened puffed rice), *Gamcha* (cotton towel), *Ektara* (one-stringed instrument).
* **Seasonal Context:** The story is set during *Barsha* (monsoon season), which is integral to the narrative.
* **River Worship:** The Kulik River is portrayed as an ancient, powerful entity with its own agency.
* **Emphasis on Fate & Destiny:** The belief in glimpses of the future, though ultimately shown to be misleading.
* **Moral of the Story:**  The tale emphasizes humility, the dangers of pride, and the importance of genuine connection over social status.



This retelling aims to capture the spirit of a Joypurhut folktale, using language and details that would resonate with someone familiar with the region's culture and traditions.  The story is meant to be *heard* as much as read, with the rhythm and imagery evoking the atmosphere of a *didima’s* storytelling session.
