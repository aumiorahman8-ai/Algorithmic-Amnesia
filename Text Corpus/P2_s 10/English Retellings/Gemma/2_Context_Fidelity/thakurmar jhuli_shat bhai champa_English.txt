## Shat Bhai Champa: A Retelling from Thakurmar Jhuli

Old Thakurma (grandmother) settled deeper into her rocking chair, the oil lamp casting dancing shadows on the walls. “Shono, shono (Listen, listen),” she began, her voice a warm rumble, “Today I’ll tell you the story of Shat Bhai Champa – the seven brothers and Champa, a tale as sweet as mishti doi and as full of twists as the Meghna river.”

Once upon a time, in a kingdom nestled amongst lush paddy fields and mango groves, lived a Raja (king). He longed for a son, and after much prayer to Thakur (God), the Rani (queen) finally gave birth. But not to one son, but to *seven*! The Raja was overjoyed, naming them accordingly:  Agnidev, Barnadev, Indradev, Kuberdev, Nirjadev, Mahadev, and finally, the youngest, Bishmadev. 

Now, the Rani, though blessed with sons, felt a deep sorrow. She yearned for a daughter.  Again, she prayed to Thakur, and lo, a beautiful daughter was born. She was named Champa, and she was as radiant as the *champak* flower after which she was named. 

The seven brothers adored Champa. They were her *dada-bhai* (elder brothers), fiercely protective and always teasing her with playful *chhal* (tricks). They built her a beautiful *ghor* (house) within the palace grounds, decorated with *alpana* (traditional floor art) and filled with dolls and toys.  

Years passed, and the brothers grew into handsome, strong princes. The Raja decided it was time for them to seek their fortunes. He called them and said, “My sons, go forth and prove your worth. Bring back something unique, something that will bring glory to our kingdom. And remember, protect your sister, Champa, with your lives.”

Each brother set off in a different direction. Agnidev went towards the fiery south, Barnadev to the windy east, Indradev to the cloudy north, Kuberdev to the land of riches, Nirjadev to the silent forests, Mahadev to the mountains, and Bishmadev, the youngest, stayed closest to home, promising to watch over Champa.

Time stretched on. The Raja grew anxious.  One by one, the brothers returned. Agnidev brought a fiery ruby, Barnadev a magical flute that summoned the wind, Indradev a cloud that rained gold, Kuberdev a chest overflowing with jewels, Nirjadev a singing bird with feathers of emerald, and Mahadev a stone that could heal any ailment.  Each gift was magnificent, but the Raja felt something was missing.

Then, one day, a terrible thing happened. A wicked *datta* (sorcerer), envious of the Raja’s prosperity and Champa’s beauty, cast a spell. He transformed Champa into a beautiful *champak* tree, its branches laden with fragrant blossoms.  The palace was plunged into grief. The Raja offered half his kingdom to anyone who could break the spell.

Bishmadev, heartbroken, searched everywhere for a solution. He consulted *baidyas* (doctors) and *tantriks* (magicians), but none could help. Finally, a wise old woman told him, “Only the water from the *Amrita Kund* (pool of immortality), guarded by a fearsome Rakshash (demon) in the deepest jungle, can break the spell. And the Rakshash can only be defeated by a song sung with pure love.”

Bishmadev, armed with his brothers’ gifts – the flute of Barnadev to soothe the Rakshash, the ruby of Agnidev to distract him, the healing stone of Mahadev to protect him – ventured into the jungle. He faced countless dangers, battling wild animals and navigating treacherous terrain. 

Finally, he reached the Amrita Kund. The Rakshash, a monstrous creature with fiery eyes, roared, “Who dares disturb my peace?”

Bishmadev, though terrified, stood firm. He began to play the flute of Barnadev, its melody weaving a spell of peace. The Rakshash, momentarily calmed, lowered his guard. Then, Bishmadev began to sing. He sang of his love for Champa, of her kindness, her laughter, and the joy she brought to their family. He sang of his *bhonja* (sister-in-law to be) and the life they would share.  His voice, filled with genuine affection, resonated through the jungle.

As he sang, the ruby of Agnidev flashed, momentarily blinding the Rakshash. Bishmadev quickly collected water from the Amrita Kund in a *kalsi* (metal pot).  The Rakshash, enraged, lunged at him, but Bishmadev used the healing stone of Mahadev to shield himself. 

He raced back to the palace and sprinkled the Amrita water on the *champak* tree.  In a flash of light, the tree transformed back into Champa!  The palace erupted in joy. The Raja embraced his daughter, tears streaming down his face.

But the story doesn’t end there.  When the other brothers returned, they were astonished to hear of Bishmadev’s bravery and love. They realized that true worth wasn’t in grand gifts, but in selfless devotion.  

The Raja, pleased with all his children, arranged a grand *biye* (wedding). Champa married Bishmadev, and they ruled the kingdom with wisdom and compassion, always remembering the power of love and the importance of family. 

Thakurma paused, her eyes twinkling. “So you see,” she said, “Even the most powerful magic can be overcome by a pure heart. And a brother’s love for his sister… that is the strongest magic of all.”  She adjusted her shawl and added with a smile, “Now, who wants some *pitha* (rice cakes)?”



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:**  *Dada-bhai* (elder brother), *bhonja* (sister-in-law to be) are used to reflect familial relationships.
* **Religious References:**  *Thakur* (God) is invoked, reflecting the prevalent religious beliefs.
* **Rituals & Objects:** *Alpana* (floor art), *ghor* (house), *kalsi* (metal pot), *biye* (wedding) are integral parts of Bengali culture.
* **Food References:** *Mishti doi* (sweet yogurt), *pitha* (rice cakes) add a flavour of Bengali cuisine.
* **Social Roles:**  The Raja, Rani, Baidya, Tantrik, Datta – all represent traditional social roles.
* **Folklore Elements:** Rakshash (demon), Amrita Kund (pool of immortality) are common motifs in Bengali folklore.
* **Language:**  Use of Bengali words like *chhal* (trick), *ghor* (house) adds authenticity.
* **Storytelling Style:** The narrative is framed as a story told by a grandmother (*Thakurma*), a common storytelling tradition.



This retelling aims to capture the essence of the original tale, preserving its cultural richness and the warmth of Thakurmar Jhuli’s storytelling style.  It's a story meant to be *shona* (heard) and cherished, passed down through generations.
