## The Tale of Khumpui: A Rangamati Story

The mist clung to the hills like a shy *boudi* (older sister/wife’s sister) to her husband, and the Langadu river murmured secrets as old as the Chakma people themselves. This is the story of Khumpui, a tale whispered around the *chang* (bamboo house) fires of Rangamati, a story of love, loss, and the enduring power of ancestral spirits.

Old Man Shanti Ram, a respected *chacha* (paternal uncle) in the village of Golakpara, had a daughter named Malati. She was known throughout the seven hills for her beauty – her eyes like polished jet, her hair cascading like the waterfall at Borang. But Malati wasn’t just beautiful; she was skilled in weaving *panis*, the traditional Chakma cloth, and her hands moved with a grace that seemed blessed by the spirits.

One day, a young man named Rajan, a *dada* (older brother) from a neighboring village, came to Golakpara to trade. He saw Malati and his heart was instantly captured. He asked Shanti Ram for her hand in marriage, offering a generous dowry of buffaloes and silver ornaments. Shanti Ram, pleased with Rajan’s character and wealth, agreed.

The wedding was a grand affair. For three days and three nights, the village resonated with the beat of the *dhol* (drum) and the joyful cries of *nani* (grandmother) and *mami* (paternal aunt). The *deowan* (village headman) oversaw the rituals, ensuring everything was done according to tradition.  Malati and Rajan were blessed with *argho* (sacred water) and *pitha* (rice cakes) by the elders, wishing them a life filled with prosperity and children.

But their happiness was short-lived. Rajan, a skilled hunter, ventured deep into the jungle one day, seeking a wild boar for a feast. He didn’t return. Days turned into weeks, and the village searched tirelessly, offering prayers to the *thangma* (spirits) for his safe return.  But Rajan was lost, swallowed by the unforgiving forest.

Malati was heartbroken. She refused to eat, refused to weave, and spent her days weeping by the river, calling out Rajan’s name.  Shanti Ram, seeing his daughter wither away, consulted the *ojha* (traditional healer/shaman). The *ojha*, after performing a *bhat puja* (rice offering to the spirits) and interpreting the signs, delivered a grim message. 

“Rajan hasn’t simply been lost,” he said, his voice heavy with sorrow. “He has been taken by Khumpui, the spirit of the deep jungle. She is a beautiful, lonely spirit who falls in love with mortal men, lures them into her realm, and keeps them as her own.”

Khumpui, the *ojha* explained, was a creature of immense power, born from the ancient trees and the mist. She could appear as a stunning woman, her voice like the rustling of leaves, her eyes holding the secrets of the forest.  To bring Rajan back, Malati would have to venture into Khumpui’s domain and challenge her.

Malati, fueled by her love for Rajan, didn’t hesitate. She prepared herself for the journey, donning a simple *orin* (Chakma traditional dress) and carrying a small pouch of *dhan* (rice) as an offering.  She sought the blessings of her *mama* (maternal uncle), who gifted her a silver amulet said to ward off evil spirits. 

Following the *ojha’s* instructions, Malati journeyed for days, deeper and deeper into the jungle. She navigated treacherous paths, crossed rushing streams, and endured the chilling silence of the ancient forest. Finally, she reached a clearing bathed in an ethereal glow.  In the center stood a magnificent *chang*, unlike any she had ever seen, woven from living vines and adorned with luminous flowers.

And there, sitting on a throne of moss and roots, was Khumpui. She was even more beautiful than the stories described, her long hair adorned with orchids, her eyes radiating a captivating sadness.  Beside her, looking lost and forlorn, sat Rajan.

Khumpui greeted Malati with a voice that echoed through the trees. “You are brave to come to my realm, little mortal.  You seek your husband? He is happy here, with me.  He has forgotten his old life.”

Malati, though trembling with fear, stood firm. “He may have forgotten, Khumpui, but *I* haven’t.  He is my husband, and I will not leave without him.”

Khumpui proposed a challenge. “If you can weave a *pani* more beautiful than any I have ever seen, a *pani* that captures the essence of the jungle and the sorrow of my heart, I will release Rajan.”

Malati accepted. For seven days and seven nights, she wove, her fingers bleeding, her spirit unwavering. She poured all her love, her grief, and her hope into the *pani*. She used dyes extracted from jungle flowers, incorporating patterns that depicted the mountains, the rivers, and the spirits of the forest. 

When she presented the *pani* to Khumpui, the spirit was stunned. It wasn’t just beautiful; it was a mirror reflecting her own loneliness and longing. Tears welled up in Khumpui’s eyes.

“You have won, Malati,” she said, her voice filled with a newfound sadness. “Your love is stronger than my power.  Take your husband and go. But remember this: even spirits crave companionship.  Perhaps, one day, I will find a love that doesn’t require taking others.”

Khumpui released Rajan, who, though dazed and confused, recognized Malati instantly.  Together, they returned to Golakpara, where they were greeted with joyous celebrations. 

Malati and Rajan lived a long and happy life, always remembering their encounter with Khumpui.  And every year, during the *boishakhi puja* (Bengali New Year festival), Malati would weave a special *pani* and leave it at the edge of the jungle, a silent offering to the lonely spirit of Khumpui, a reminder that even in the deepest darkness, there is always hope for love and connection.



This retelling attempts to preserve the cultural nuances by:

* **Using Bengali kinship terms:** *boudi, chacha, dada, nani, mami, deowan, mama*
* **Incorporating specific rituals:** *chang* construction, *argho* and *pitha* blessings, *bhat puja*, *boishakhi puja*
* **Mentioning traditional crafts:** *panis* and *orin* weaving.
* **Referencing spiritual beliefs:** *thangma* (spirits), *ojha* (shaman), amulets for protection.
* **Maintaining the tone and atmosphere:**  The story is told with a reverence for nature and the spiritual world, common in Rangamati folklore.
* **Highlighting the importance of community:** The village’s involvement in the search and the wedding celebrations.



This is just one version of the tale, as folktales are often fluid and vary depending on the storyteller. However, it aims to capture the essence of the Khumpui legend as it is known in the Rangamati region.
