## The Silent Quarrel and the Polo Trap: A Tale from Moulovibazar

Old Man Haru Charan, a respected *Dada* (grandfather) in the village of Rajnagar, Moulovibazar, was known for two things: his prize-winning *Shona* (golden) paddy and his equally prized, but notoriously temperamental, bull named Badal. Badal wasn’t just any bull; he was a *holstein-friesian*, a breed brought in by the tea gardens, and possessed a strength that could pull a cart laden with a mountain of tea leaves. 

Haru Dada’s son, Borhan, a man with a quick tongue and a quicker temper, also fancied himself a skilled *polo* player. Not the horse-polo of the wealthy *Zamindars*, but the local *bhui-polo* – a ground-level game played with a bamboo stick and a woven ball of paddy straw, a favourite pastime during *Boishakh* (the first month of the Bengali calendar).

The trouble began during the preparations for the annual *Rash Mela* (festival celebrating Radha and Krishna). Borhan, brimming with confidence after a few successful practice rounds, boasted to his father, “Dada, this year, I’ll win the *bhui-polo* tournament. My aim is true, my strength unmatched! I’ll bring honour to our family.”

Haru Dada, a man of few words, simply grunted. He was worried. Borhan’s boasting often preceded a fall. He knew Badal, usually docile, had been restless lately, sensing the approaching monsoon and the change in the air. He also knew Borhan had been eyeing Badal to help him practice, claiming the bull’s strength would give him an edge.

“Badal is not for games, Borhan,” Haru Dada finally said, his voice gravelly. “He’s for the fields. The *Shona* paddy needs him.  Don't disturb him during this crucial time.”

This was the spark. Borhan, feeling his authority challenged, retorted, “Dada, you always underestimate me!  I know what I’m doing. A little practice won’t harm Badal. You treat him like a son, more than you treat me, your own *chhele* (son)!”

Here, the quarrel began, but it was a *moun-jatok* (silent quarrel).  Neither man raised his voice.  This was the way of the Sylheti people – to hold grievances close, letting them fester like a hidden wound.  Haru Dada turned back to his paddy, meticulously examining the ripening grains. Borhan stormed off to the *baithakkhana* (community sitting place), muttering under his breath.

The next day, Borhan, fueled by resentment, secretly hitched Badal to a makeshift *polo* cart. He intended to practice in the secluded *jal-mahal* (water palace ruins) near the village. He didn’t realize his *Kakima* (father’s younger sister), old Ayesha Bibi, had been watching from her window.  She was a woman known for her sharp eyes and even sharper tongue.

“Borhan!” she called out, her voice carrying across the fields. “What foolishness is this?  Badal is a blessing, not a plaything!  You disrespect your father and invite misfortune.  Remember the stories of the *Bhoot-er Pol* (ghost trap) near the *jal-mahal*!  It’s said to ensnare those who act with arrogance and disregard for tradition.”

Borhan scoffed, dismissing her warning as old wives’ tales. He whipped Badal, and the bull, confused and agitated, charged forward.  As they neared the *jal-mahal*, the ground beneath the cart suddenly gave way.  It wasn’t a simple sinkhole, but a cleverly concealed pit, overgrown with weeds and camouflaged by the monsoon’s early rains.  It was, indeed, the legendary *Bhoot-er Pol*.

The cart and Badal tumbled into the pit.  Borhan, thrown clear, landed with a thud, thankfully unharmed but shaken.  Badal, however, was trapped, his powerful legs flailing helplessly.  

Panic seized Borhan. He called for help, his voice cracking with fear.  The villagers, alerted by his cries, rushed to the *jal-mahal*.  They tried everything – ropes, levers, even the combined strength of several men – but Badal remained stuck.

Seeing his son’s distress, Haru Dada arrived, his face etched with worry, but his voice still controlled.  He didn’t scold Borhan. He didn’t say “I told you so.”  Instead, he calmly assessed the situation. 

“Bring the *dhol* (drum) and the *shankh* (conch shell),” he instructed. “And summon the *Baul* (wandering mystic singer) from the neighbouring village.”

The villagers, confused, obeyed. The *Baul* arrived, his face serene. He began to play a haunting melody on his *ektara* (one-stringed instrument) and chant verses dedicated to *Dharma Thakur* (the deity of justice and fate).  He performed a *shanti-mantra* (peace invocation) and sprinkled holy water around the pit.

As the music swelled, Haru Dada, guided by the *Baul*, began to carefully dig around the pit, reinforcing the sides with bamboo poles.  He worked with a quiet determination, his movements precise and deliberate.  Finally, after hours of painstaking effort, they managed to create a ramp and coax Badal out of the trap.

Badal, exhausted but safe, nudged Haru Dada gratefully.  Borhan, humbled and ashamed, rushed to his father, tears streaming down his face.

“Dada, forgive me,” he sobbed. “I was foolish and arrogant. I didn’t listen to you, or to Kakima. I almost lost Badal, and I almost lost your respect.”

Haru Dada embraced his son, his silence finally broken. “The *Shona* paddy is important, Borhan, but life is more precious.  And respect for tradition, for our elders, and for the blessings we receive – that is the most important harvest of all.  The *Bhoot-er Pol* doesn’t trap with chains, *chhele*, it traps with pride.”

The *Rash Mela* came and went. Borhan didn’t participate in the *bhui-polo* tournament. Instead, he helped his father harvest the *Shona* paddy, working alongside Badal, a newfound respect blossoming in his heart.  And the tale of the Silent Quarrel and the Polo Trap became a cautionary story whispered amongst the villagers of Rajnagar, a reminder that even the strongest bull, and the most skilled player, are no match for the wisdom of tradition and the power of humility.



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:**  *Dada* (grandfather), *Chhele* (son), *Kakima* (father’s younger sister) – these terms are integral to Bengali family structure and show the relationships between characters.
* **Specific Rituals:** The use of *dhol*, *shankh*, *Baul* and *shanti-mantra* reflects the syncretic religious practices common in rural Bengal, blending Hindu and folk traditions.  The *Baul’s* role is particularly significant, representing a spiritual authority.
* **Agricultural Life:** The importance of *Shona* paddy and Badal the bull highlights the agrarian lifestyle of Moulovibazar.
* **Sylheti Culture:** The emphasis on *moun-jatok* (silent quarrel) is a characteristic of Sylheti social interactions, where direct confrontation is often avoided.
* **Local Folklore:** The *Bhoot-er Pol* (ghost trap) is a local legend, adding a layer of mystery and cultural specificity.
* **Festivals:** The story is framed around the *Rash Mela*, a significant cultural event.
* **Games:** The description of *bhui-polo* provides a glimpse into local recreational activities.
* **Respect for Elders:** The story emphasizes the importance of respecting elders and tradition, a core value in Bengali society.



This retelling aims to capture the essence of a Bengali folktale, preserving its cultural richness and moral lessons.  It’s a story not just about a bull and a game, but about family, respect, and the consequences of pride.
