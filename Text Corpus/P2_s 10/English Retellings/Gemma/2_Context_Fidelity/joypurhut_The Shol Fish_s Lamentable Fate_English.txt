## The Shol Fish's Lamentable Fate: A Joypurhut Tale

Old Man Haru Kaka, his face a roadmap of wrinkles etched by the sun and the Padma’s currents, always said the Shol fish held the sorrow of Joypurhut in its silver scales. He’d tell this story, sitting on the *charpoi* under the banyan tree, the scent of *shitolpati* mats and drying chilies thick in the air, to anyone who’d listen – mostly the *bhatijas* (nephews) and *bhatnis* (nieces) gathered for evening tea. This is how he’d tell it:

Long ago, before even my *dada’s dada* (great-grandfather’s father) was born, the Padma River flowed differently. It was wider, more generous, and teeming with Shol fish – the most prized catch in all of Bengal. These weren’t just any fish, mind you. They were *deota-er mach* – almost divine. Their flesh was delicate, their flavour unmatched, and they were considered auspicious for *bratistha* (religious ceremonies) and weddings. 

Now, in those days, lived a fisherman named Ramjan. He wasn’t a bad man, not exactly. He had a *boudi* (older sister-in-law) who worried over him constantly, a young *chhele* (son) named Karim, and a wife, Fatima Bibi, who was known for her beautiful *kantha* embroidery. But Ramjan was… ambitious. He wanted to be the richest fisherman in Joypurhut, to have a house with a tin roof instead of thatch, to give Karim a proper education, not just the lessons learned on the river.

He started taking more and more Shol fish, ignoring the warnings of the village *mahajan* (elder) and the whispers of the river itself. The elders said, “Ramjan, the Shol fish is a blessing. Take only what you need. Don’t disturb the *jal-prakriti* (water’s nature). Offer a *nivedan* (offering) of rice and flowers to the river goddess before each catch.”

But Ramjan scoffed. He’d throw a handful of rice, barely enough to feed a sparrow, and then cast his net, dragging it across the riverbed with greedy force. He’d boast to his *shagrid* (apprentice), “See, boy? This is how a man makes his fortune!”

One day, he caught the largest Shol fish anyone had ever seen. It shimmered like moonlight, its eyes holding an ancient wisdom. As Ramjan hauled it onto his *nouka* (boat), the fish *spoke*. 

“Fisherman,” it said, its voice like the rustling of reeds, “You have taken too much. You have forgotten the respect due to the river and its creatures. I am the last of my kind, the guardian of the Shol shoal.  Release me, and I will ensure the river remains bountiful.  Keep me, and a curse will fall upon Joypurhut.”

Ramjan, blinded by greed, laughed. “A talking fish! What nonsense! This one will fetch me a king’s ransom!” He ignored the fish’s plea and hurried back to the *haat* (market).

He sold the magnificent Shol fish for a fortune. He built his tin-roofed house, sent Karim to a *maktab* (school), and Fatima Bibi bought silks for her *kantha*.  For a while, Ramjan prospered.

But then, the river began to change. The Shol fish disappeared. Other fish became scarce. The Padma, once a source of life, became unpredictable, prone to violent floods and devastating erosion.  Crops failed.  Sickness spread.  Joypurhut fell into hardship.

Ramjan’s fortune dwindled. Karim fell ill, and the expensive education couldn’t save him. Fatima Bibi’s hands, once nimble with embroidery, grew weak with worry.  His tin roof leaked, and the house felt cold and empty.

One evening, sitting alone, Ramjan heard a mournful sound carried on the wind. It sounded like a weeping, a lament. He went to the riverbank and saw a shimmering form rising from the water. It was the spirit of the Shol fish.

“You did not heed my warning,” the spirit said, its voice filled with sorrow. “You valued wealth over respect, greed over gratitude.  The river remembers. Joypurhut remembers.  The Shol fish is gone, and with it, a part of our soul.”

Ramjan, broken and remorseful, fell to his knees. He begged for forgiveness, offering a lifetime of service to the river. But the spirit simply faded away, leaving behind only the sound of the Padma’s mournful flow.

Haru Kaka would pause here, his eyes distant. “And that, my children,” he’d say, “is why we always offer a *nivedan* before fishing. Why we respect the river. Why we remember the lamentable fate of the Shol fish.  It’s a reminder that taking too much, forgetting our traditions, and disrespecting nature… it always comes at a price.” 

He’d then offer everyone a *mishti* (sweet) and say, “Let us pray to the river goddess for her continued blessings, and let us never forget the lesson of the Shol fish.”  And the scent of *shitolpati* and chilies would hang heavy in the air, a silent testament to a tale as old as the Padma itself.
