## The Exaggerating Matabbar and the Shrinking Snake of Joypurhut

Old Matabbar Harun, a man known throughout Joypurhut for his *atishyo* (exaggeration), was a pillar of the village, though a rather wobbly one. He wasn’t malicious, mind you, just…enthusiastic with the truth. Every fish he caught was a *machh-raja* (fish-king), every harvest a bounty fit for a Nawab, and every ailment he overcame a battle against death itself. His *bhatija* (nephew), young Rahim, often sighed and shook his head, knowing the stories would grow taller with each telling around the *adda* (gathering place) under the banyan tree.

One scorching afternoon, Rahim was tending to the family’s paddy field when he saw it – a *sap* (snake), a thick, glistening black cobra, coiled near the irrigation channel. It wasn’t a particularly large snake, maybe six *hath* (arms lengths) long, but Rahim, a sensible boy, was terrified. He ran, shouting, “*Kaka* (uncle)! *Kaka*! Snake! A monstrous snake in the field!”

Matabbar Harun, enjoying a cool *lassi* with his *shoshur bari* (mother-in-law’s house) relatives, heard the commotion. He puffed out his chest, already composing the tale. He rushed to the field, grabbing his *danda* (walking stick) for show. 

“Where, Rahim? Where is this beast?” he boomed.

Rahim pointed, trembling. Matabbar Harun squinted. “*Eman ki* (is this it)? This…this is no ordinary snake! This is a *nagaraj* (snake king)! A creature from the depths of the *beel* (wetland)! It must be thirty *hath* long, thicker than a mango tree! Its hood is like a *thal* (plate), and its eyes burn like coals!”

The villagers, drawn by the noise, gathered around. Old Ayesha *khala* (aunt), known for her knowledge of *jatra* (folk theatre) and local lore, gasped. “*Astaghfirullah!* (God forbid!) A *nagaraj*! We must perform a *maanot* (votive offering) to appease it!”

Matabbar Harun, basking in the attention, nodded gravely. “Indeed! A grand *maanot*! We shall offer a *pitha* (rice cake) made with the finest rice, a *murghi* (chicken) sacrificed to Ma Manasa, the snake goddess, and a *dhermi* (small earthen lamp) lit with *til tel* (sesame oil)!”

He began to describe the snake’s ferocity, claiming it had already swallowed a goat whole and was eyeing old Karim *chacha’s* (uncle’s) cow.  With each telling, the snake grew in size and menace.  He even claimed it spoke to him, demanding a golden *bali* (offering).

Meanwhile, the snake, thoroughly confused by all the shouting and the growing crowd, was getting…smaller.  Not physically, of course. But as Matabbar Harun’s story swelled, the snake seemed to shrink in comparison.  It was no longer a terrifying *nagaraj*, but a rather ordinary cobra, looking increasingly embarrassed by the attention.

Ayesha *khala*, observing this, noticed something peculiar.  “Matabbar,” she said, her voice sharp, “your snake seems to be…diminishing with each tale.  Is it perhaps…afraid of your exaggerations?”

The villagers chuckled.  Matabbar Harun, flustered, tried to maintain his composure. “Nonsense! It’s merely…respectful! It’s shrinking in awe of my bravery!”

But the truth was becoming clear.  The snake, overwhelmed by the sheer absurdity of Matabbar Harun’s claims, was literally losing its presence.  It was as if the weight of the exaggeration was crushing its mythical aura.

Finally, as Matabbar Harun declared the snake to be a thousand *hath* long and capable of swallowing the entire village, the cobra, with a final, exasperated hiss, simply…vanished.  It slithered away into the paddy field, leaving behind only a faint trail in the mud.

The villagers burst into laughter.  Rahim, relieved, shook his head.  “*Kaka*,” he said, “you scared it away with your stories, not your bravery!”

Matabbar Harun, though slightly deflated, couldn’t resist adding one final flourish. “Of course! It was a magical snake, defeated by the power of my…description! A feat unheard of in Joypurhut!”

And so, the tale of the Exaggerating Matabbar and the Shrinking Snake became a beloved story in Joypurhut, a reminder that even the most fearsome creatures can be overcome, not by strength or offerings, but by the sheer, overwhelming power of *atishyo*.  And Rahim, the sensible nephew, continued to sigh, knowing his *kaka* would never change, and that the stories of Joypurhut would always be a little bit…larger than life.



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:**  Use of *Kaka, Bhatija, Shoshur Bari, Khala, Chacha* to reflect familial relationships and respect.
* **Local Terms:** *Atishyo* (exaggeration), *Machh-raja* (fish-king), *adda* (gathering place), *hath* (arm’s length), *beel* (wetland), *thal* (plate), *maanot* (votive offering), *pitha* (rice cake), *murghi* (chicken), *dhermi* (earthen lamp), *til tel* (sesame oil), *bali* (offering), *sap* (snake), *nagaraj* (snake king).
* **Religious/Cultural Practices:**  Mention of Ma Manasa, the snake goddess, and the ritual of offering a *maanot* with specific items.
* **Social Life:**  The importance of the *adda* and the role of elders like Ayesha *khala* in community knowledge.
* **Folk Entertainment:** Reference to *jatra* (folk theatre) to highlight the storytelling tradition.
* **Everyday Life:**  Details about paddy fields, irrigation channels, and *lassi* drinking to ground the story in the local environment.
* **Humor:** The story relies on the humorous contrast between Matabbar Harun’s boasts and the snake’s shrinking presence, a common element in Bengali folktales.



This retelling aims to capture the spirit and flavor of a Joypurhut folktale, using language and details that would resonate with a Bengali audience familiar with the region's culture and traditions.
