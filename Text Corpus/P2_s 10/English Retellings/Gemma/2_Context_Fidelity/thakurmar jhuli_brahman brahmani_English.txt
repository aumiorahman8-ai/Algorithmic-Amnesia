## Brahman Brahmani: A Tale from Thakurmar Jhuli

Old Thakurma began, settling deeper into her woven mat, her voice a low hum like the evening cicadas. “Shono, shono (Listen, listen), children. Today I’ll tell you the story of Brahman Brahmani. It’s a tale of cleverness, hardship, and the blessings of Ma Manasa.

Once, in a small village nestled amongst rice paddies, lived a poor Brahman, a priest named Haripada. He was a good man, devoted to his dharma, but *dukho-kristo* (burdened with sorrow) because he and his wife, Brahmani, had no children. Brahmani, a woman of quiet strength and devotion, spent her days tending their small *bari* (homestead) and praying to all the *dev-devi* (gods and goddesses) for a son. 

Years passed. Haripada, despite his best efforts, couldn’t earn enough to support them properly. He’d go door-to-door offering *pujo* (worship) and reciting *mantra*, but often returned with only a handful of *paisa* (coins) and a stomach rumbling with hunger. Brahmani, ever resourceful, would make *pitha* (rice cakes) from whatever little rice they had and sell them in the *haat* (market).

One day, Haripada, desperate, decided to travel to a distant city, hoping to find a wealthy patron. He promised Brahmani, “I’ll return with enough to live comfortably, *shono shono* (listen, listen). Don’t worry, *bouma* (daughter-in-law - a term of respect for his wife).”

Brahmani, though her heart ached with worry, nodded bravely. “Go, *priyo* (beloved). May Ma Durga protect you on your journey.”

Haripada left, but months turned into a year, and still he didn’t return. Brahmani, left alone and with dwindling resources, faced immense hardship. She continued to sell *pitha*, but the rains were poor, the rice harvest meager, and the village *mahajan* (moneylender) demanded his dues. 

One scorching afternoon, exhausted and despairing, Brahmani went to the river to bathe. As she was washing her clothes, a beautiful, shimmering snake emerged from the water. It was Ma Manasa herself, the Snake Goddess, revered and feared throughout Bengal.

Brahmani, though startled, remembered her dharma. She quickly offered a small *bali* (offering) of rice and flowers to the Goddess. Ma Manasa, pleased with Brahmani’s devotion, spoke, “I know your sorrow, Brahmani. Your husband is alive, but held captive by a cruel king. I can help you, but it will require a great sacrifice.”

Brahmani, without hesitation, asked, “What sacrifice, Ma? I will give anything to have my husband back.”

Ma Manasa replied, “When your son is born, you must offer him to me.”

Brahmani’s heart clenched. To give up a child… but her love for Haripada was immense. She agreed, knowing the weight of her promise.

Soon after, Brahmani conceived and gave birth to a beautiful son. They named him Dukhiram – ‘the one who removes sorrow’. He was a bright, cheerful child, the light of Brahmani’s life.  As Dukhiram grew, Brahmani’s sorrow deepened. She knew the day of her promise was approaching.

When Dukhiram turned seven, Ma Manasa appeared again. “The time has come, Brahmani. Bring your son to the river.”

Brahmani, tears streaming down her face, led Dukhiram to the riverbank. She performed a *puja* (worship) with all the devotion she could muster, offering flowers, incense, and a beautifully decorated *jhapi* (bamboo basket).  As she prepared to fulfill her promise, Dukhiram, sensing his mother’s anguish, asked, “Ma, why are you so sad?”

Brahmani, unable to hold back any longer, confessed everything – her husband’s captivity, Ma Manasa’s help, and the promise she had made.

Dukhiram, a wise child beyond his years, said, “Ma, don’t worry. I will not be truly offered to Ma Manasa. I will trick her.”

He instructed his mother to create a clay replica of him, dressed in his clothes. When Ma Manasa arrived, Brahmani offered the clay figure, pretending it was Dukhiram.  Ma Manasa, though a goddess, was pleased with the devotion and accepted the offering. 

Immediately, Haripada was released from captivity and returned home. He was overjoyed to see his wife and son.  

But Ma Manasa, realizing she had been tricked, was furious. She sent a deadly black cobra to bite Dukhiram.  

Just as the cobra was about to strike, Dukhiram began to play his flute. The music was so enchanting, so filled with devotion, that the cobra was mesmerized. It stopped, swayed to the music, and finally, transformed into a garland of flowers around Dukhiram’s neck.

Ma Manasa, witnessing this, was deeply moved. She realized Dukhiram’s cleverness and devotion were greater than any sacrifice. She blessed him and his family, showering them with wealth and prosperity. 

“See, children,” Thakurma concluded, her eyes twinkling. “Cleverness and devotion, combined with a strong heart, can overcome even the most difficult trials. And remember, Ma Manasa is a powerful goddess, to be respected and appeased with sincere *bhakti* (devotion).”



This retelling attempts to capture the essence of the tale as found in Thakurmar Jhuli, including:

* **Kinship terms:** *Bouma, Priyo* are used to show the relationship between Haripada and Brahmani.
* **Cultural nuances:** The importance of *dharma*, *puja*, *mantra*, *bali*, *pitha*, *haat*, *mahajan* are all integral to the story's setting and meaning.
* **Rituals:** The detailed description of the *puja* Brahmani performs before offering Dukhiram, and the use of a *jhapi* are specific to Bengali folk traditions.
* **Respect for deities:** Ma Manasa is portrayed as both powerful and capable of being moved by devotion.
* **Emphasis on cleverness:** Dukhiram’s intelligence is highlighted as a key element in overcoming the challenge.
* **The use of Bengali words:** Scattered throughout the narrative to maintain authenticity.



This story, like many in Thakurmar Jhuli, serves as a moral lesson, emphasizing the importance of devotion, resourcefulness, and the power of a mother’s love.
