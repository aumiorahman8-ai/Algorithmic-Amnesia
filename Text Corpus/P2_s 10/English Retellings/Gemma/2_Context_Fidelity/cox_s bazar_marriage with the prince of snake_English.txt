## The Tale of Chandrabati and the Serpent Prince - A Cox's Bazar Story

This story, *Shaaper Moto Biye* (Marriage Like a Curse), is whispered amongst the older women of Cox's Bazar, especially during the monsoon when the rain mimics the hiss of snakes. It’s a tale of beauty, sacrifice, and the enduring power of *shakti* (divine feminine energy).

Chandrabati was the youngest of seven daughters born to a poor *zamindar* (landlord) named Ramchandra Chowdhury. He lived in a modest, though respectable, house near the Inani beach, known for its rocky shores and the constant roar of the Bay of Bengal.  Ramchandra Chowdhury, despite his dwindling fortune, was a proud man. He’d already married off his six elder daughters –  *Didi* (elder sister) Shanti to a merchant in Chittagong, *Didi* Kamala to a schoolteacher, and so on – each marriage bringing less joy and less dowry than the last. 

Chandrabati, however, was different. She was renowned throughout the villages for her beauty – skin like polished sandalwood, eyes like the deep ocean, and hair that flowed like black silk. She was also deeply devoted to her *Ma* (mother), Shovavati Devi, and spent her days tending to the *tulsi* plant in the courtyard, offering prayers to Durga *Maa* (Mother Durga).

One day, while collecting *shona* (golden) seaweed from the beach – a common practice for women to supplement the family income – Chandrabati stumbled upon a magnificent, jeweled crown half-buried in the sand. It pulsed with a strange, cool energy.  As she touched it, a voice, smooth as river stones, echoed in her mind.

“This is mine, beautiful one. Return it to its rightful owner, and you will be richly rewarded.”

Chandrabati, though frightened, was a dutiful girl. She brought the crown home, and her *Baba* (father) was astonished. He’d heard tales of the *Naga Raja* (Serpent King), Sheshnag, who ruled the underwater kingdom beneath the Bay of Bengal.  It was said he possessed immense wealth and power. 

Soon after, a messenger arrived – not a man, but a colossal cobra, adorned with rubies and emeralds. He announced that the crown belonged to Sheshnag, and as a reward for Chandrabati’s honesty, the Naga Raja wished to marry her.

Ramchandra Chowdhury was overjoyed. A marriage to a king, even a serpent king, would solve all their problems! He ignored Chandrabati’s silent terror and readily agreed.  Shovavati Devi, however, felt a cold dread creep into her heart. She knew the stories – the Naga Raja was a powerful being, but his world was alien and dangerous.

The wedding was unlike anything seen in Cox's Bazar.  The ceremony took place on the beach at midnight, under a full moon.  Instead of a *mandap* (wedding platform) made of wood and flowers, a shimmering palace of coral and pearls rose from the sea.  The air thrummed with the chanting of *tantriks* (tantric priests) summoned by Sheshnag.  Chandrabati, dressed in a crimson *Benarasi* sari and heavy gold jewelry, was led to the altar, her *ghat* (wedding pot) filled not with water, but with fragrant sandalwood paste. 

Sheshnag, in his true form, was a breathtaking sight – a massive serpent, his scales glittering like a thousand stars. He took Chandrabati as his wife, and she was taken down into the underwater kingdom.

Life in Naga Loka was opulent, but lonely. Chandrabati was surrounded by riches beyond imagination, but she missed the warmth of her *Ma’s* embrace, the scent of the *tulsi* plant, and the familiar sounds of her village. Sheshnag, though not unkind, was a creature of a different world. He couldn’t understand her longing for the sun, the taste of rice, or the comfort of human companionship.

Years passed. Chandrabati bore Sheshnag seven sons, each with scales shimmering on their skin. But her heart remained heavy with sorrow.  She secretly prayed to Durga *Maa*, begging for release. 

One day, she overheard Sheshnag speaking to his advisors. He revealed that Chandrabati’s life was bound to his by a curse – a curse placed upon him by a powerful *sadhu* (holy man) for a past transgression. The only way to break the curse, and free Chandrabati, was for her to perform a specific ritual during the *Durga Puja* festival. 

She had to return to her village, offer a *bali* (sacrifice) of seven white pumpkins to Durga *Maa*, and recite a specific *mantra* (incantation) while facing the sea.  But there was a catch – Sheshnag would be weakened during this time, and his enemies, the *Rakshash* (demons) of the deep, would try to seize his kingdom.

Chandrabati, knowing the risk, bravely agreed. Sheshnag allowed her to return to her village, providing her with a magical conch shell that would summon him if she was in danger.

Back in Cox's Bazar, Chandrabati was a ghost of her former self. Her *Ma* barely recognized her.  The villagers whispered, calling her *bhootni* (ghost woman).  She prepared for the *Durga Puja*, gathering the seven white pumpkins and learning the complex *mantra*. 

On the night of *Maha Ashtami*, she performed the ritual on the beach, facing the turbulent sea. As she chanted, the ocean roared, and monstrous forms rose from the depths – the *Rakshash* were attacking!  Chandrabati, fueled by her devotion to Durga *Maa* and her longing for freedom, continued chanting, her voice unwavering.

Just as the *Rakshash* were about to overwhelm her, she blew the conch shell.  Sheshnag, despite his weakened state, arrived in a whirlwind of water and scales, battling the demons with ferocious power.  

The battle raged throughout the night. Finally, with a mighty roar, Sheshnag vanquished the *Rakshash*.  The curse was broken. 

As the first rays of dawn touched the horizon, Sheshnag appeared before Chandrabati, no longer a terrifying serpent, but a handsome prince, freed from his serpentine form. He thanked her for her courage and devotion. 

He couldn’t stay in the human world permanently, but he promised to visit her every year during *Durga Puja*.  He gifted her a chest filled with pearls and precious stones, enough to secure her family’s future.

Chandrabati returned to her village, a changed woman. She lived a long and peaceful life, revered by the villagers as a symbol of courage and devotion.  And every year, during *Durga Puja*, the people of Cox's Bazar would gather on the beach, watching for a glimpse of the shimmering waves, hoping to catch a sight of the Serpent Prince returning to his beloved Chandrabati.



This story is more than just a fantastical tale. It reflects the deep connection the people of Cox's Bazar have with the sea, their reverence for nature, and their strong belief in the power of *shakti* and the enduring strength of a woman’s spirit.  The specific rituals – the *tulsi* worship, the *Durga Puja* offerings, the use of sandalwood paste – are all integral parts of Bengali culture and add authenticity to the narrative.  The kinship terms – *Baba, Ma, Didi* – ground the story in a familiar familial context, making it a cherished part of the local folklore.
