## The Cunning Mother: A Mymensingh Tale of Two Sons

The story unfolds in the lush, riverine lands around Mymensingh, a place where the scent of *paddy* fields hangs heavy in the air and the rhythm of life is dictated by the seasons. It concerns Rokeya Bibi, a woman renowned not for beauty, but for a sharp wit that could cut through bamboo. She was the second wife of Karim Sheikh, a prosperous *zamindar* (landlord) with a son, Rahim, from his first wife, who had passed away. 

Rokeya Bibi, though initially treated with a respectful distance, quickly proved her worth in managing the household. But her true ambition lay in securing a bright future for her own son, Jabbar. She knew Karim Sheikh, despite his wealth, was a simple man, easily swayed by appearances. And she understood the subtle power dynamics within a joint family, the importance of *rishtanati* (relationships and social standing).

Rahim, the elder son, was a good-natured boy, but lacked ambition. He preferred the company of the *barkandazes* (boatmen) and the thrill of *baul* songs to the responsibilities of a *zamindar*. He was content with a simple life, a trait Rokeya Bibi viewed with thinly veiled disdain. Jabbar, however, was malleable, a blank slate upon which she could write her desires.

From the very beginning, Rokeya Bibi employed a cunning strategy. She didn’t openly favor Jabbar, that would be too obvious. Instead, she subtly undermined Rahim while simultaneously elevating Jabbar, all under the guise of “proper upbringing.” 

When Rahim expressed interest in learning to ride a horse, a skill befitting a *zamindar*, Rokeya Bibi would lament, “Oh, Rahim *bhai* (elder brother), you are so clumsy! You’ll fall and break your bones. It’s not a suitable pastime for a delicate boy like you. Perhaps you should focus on your studies, learning *persian* verses.”  She’d then turn to Jabbar, “Jabbar, my son, you have a strong build. You should learn to ride. It will make you a man!”  She’d hire the best *sarang* (horse trainer) for Jabbar, ensuring he mastered the skill with ease.

The same pattern repeated itself with other skills. Rahim wanted to learn *lathi khela* (stick fighting), a popular rural sport. Rokeya Bibi declared it “uncouth” and “beneath a zamindar’s son,” steering him towards *tabla* lessons instead. Jabbar, meanwhile, was secretly trained in *lathi khela* by a retired *pahelwan* (wrestler) she’d hired, becoming surprisingly proficient.

She even manipulated the religious observances. During *Eid*, she’d ensure Rahim received a slightly smaller *eidi* (gift money) than Jabbar, explaining it away as Rahim having “already received so many blessings.”  She’d meticulously oversee Jabbar’s participation in *namaaz* (prayer), praising his piety to Karim Sheikh, while subtly pointing out Rahim’s occasional lapses in devotion.

The most telling instance involved the annual *Bhatiali* festival, a vibrant celebration of river life. Rahim, captivated by the music and the atmosphere, wanted to participate in the boat races. Rokeya Bibi vehemently opposed it, claiming it was “too dangerous” and “unbecoming.”  She then arranged for Jabbar to be the sponsor of a particularly impressive boat, ensuring his name was prominently displayed and he received the accolades of the crowd.

Karim Sheikh, oblivious to the subtle machinations, saw only a dutiful son in Jabbar and a carefree, somewhat irresponsible son in Rahim. He began to rely more and more on Jabbar for managing the estate, impressed by his apparent competence and dedication. 

Years passed. Rahim, resigned to his fate, lived a quiet life, content with his simple pleasures. Jabbar, groomed by his mother’s relentless strategy, became a shrewd and capable *zamindar*.  When Karim Sheikh finally passed away, Jabbar inherited the estate, leaving Rahim with a small allowance.

There was murmuring amongst the villagers. Some whispered about Rokeya Bibi’s cunning, calling her a *dakshina* (clever) woman, but also a *shaitan* (devil) for her manipulation.  Rahim, however, bore no resentment. He understood his mother-in-law’s ambition and accepted his fate with a gentle sadness. 

One day, Jabbar, now a powerful *zamindar*, visited Rahim. He offered him a more generous allowance, acknowledging his brother’s kindness and good nature.  “You always had a good heart, Rahim *bhai*,” he said, “but you lacked the… the drive.  Mother always said I needed to be prepared for responsibility.”

Rahim smiled sadly. “Your mother is a very clever woman, Jabbar. She saw what you could become and shaped you accordingly.”  He paused, then added, “But remember, Jabbar, true wealth lies not just in land and power, but in contentment and kindness.  Don’t let ambition consume you.”

The tale of Rokeya Bibi and her two sons became a cautionary legend in Mymensingh, a reminder that while ambition is a powerful force, it must be tempered with compassion and that a mother’s love, even when cunning, can leave a lasting, and sometimes bittersweet, legacy.  It’s a story still told during *addas* (gatherings) under the shade of the *bat* trees, a testament to the enduring power of Bengali folklore and the complexities of family dynamics in the heart of rural Bangladesh.



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:**  *Bhai* (brother), used respectfully even between those not directly related.
* **Social Hierarchy:**  The importance of *zamindari* and the associated responsibilities.
* **Religious Observances:**  Mention of *Eid* and *namaaz* as integral parts of life.
* **Rural Life:**  References to *paddy* fields, *barkandazes*, *baul* songs, *lathi khela*, *pahelwan*, *Bhatiali* festival, and *bat* trees.
* **Language:** Use of Bengali words like *dakshina*, *shaitan*, *addas* to maintain authenticity.
* **Subtlety of Manipulation:** Rokeya Bibi’s strategy isn’t overt cruelty, but subtle undermining and elevation, reflecting the indirect communication often found in Bengali culture.
* **Acceptance of Fate:** Rahim’s lack of resentment and acceptance of his position, a common theme in Bengali folklore.
* **Emphasis on *Rishtanati*:** The importance of social standing and relationships in shaping one's destiny.



This retelling aims to capture the spirit of a Mymensingh folktale, preserving its cultural richness and the nuanced portrayal of its characters.  It's a story that speaks to the enduring themes of ambition, family, and the complex role of a mother in shaping her children's lives.
