## Suanlo Suanlo and the Tiger's Revenge: A Rangamati Tale

The mist clung to the hills around Rangamati like a shy *boudi* (older sister/wife’s sister) to her brother-in-law. Old Man Shanti, his face etched with the stories of a hundred monsoons, began to speak, his voice a low rumble like distant thunder. “This story, *chhele* (child/son), is of Suanlo Suanlo, a young Chakma man, and the wrath of a *bagh* (tiger) wronged. It happened long ago, when the hills were even wilder, and the spirits walked closer to us.”

Suanlo Suanlo wasn’t known for his strength, but for his cleverness. He was the youngest son of the *Karbari* (village headman) of Golakpara, a small village nestled beside the Karnafuli River. His *didi* (older sister) had married into a neighboring village, and he often visited, bringing gifts of woven *panis* (traditional lower garment) and carefully crafted bamboo flutes. 

One day, while returning from his *didi’s* village, Suanlo Suanlo was walking through the dense jungle. He carried a small offering of *poi* (rice porridge) and *achhar* (pickle) for his ailing *thamma* (grandmother). Suddenly, he heard a terrifying roar. A magnificent Royal Bengal tiger, its stripes blazing like fire in the dappled sunlight, stood before him. 

This wasn’t just any tiger. This was *Baghraj*, a creature revered and feared by the villagers. It was said Baghraj protected the forest, and to harm him brought terrible misfortune. But Suanlo Suanlo hadn’t harmed Baghraj. He’d simply stumbled upon the tiger enjoying a freshly killed deer.

Baghraj, however, was furious. “You humans are greedy!” he growled, his voice shaking the leaves. “You take and take from the forest, leaving nothing for us! My kill, you disturb! I demand a *bali* (sacrifice) for this disrespect!”

Suanlo Suanlo, though trembling, remembered his *Karbaribapa’s* (father/Karbari) teachings. He knew appeasing a spirit, even a powerful one like Baghraj, required respect and understanding. He couldn’t offer an animal – that would be against the Buddhist principles his family followed.  Instead, he bowed low, touching his forehead to the earth in a *pranam* (respectful greeting).

“Great Baghraj,” he said, his voice surprisingly steady. “I meant no disrespect. I carry only *poi* for my sick *thamma*. I have nothing of value to offer, except my skill. I am a weaver. I will weave you a *panis* unlike any other, a cloth that reflects the beauty of the forest itself. Will that appease your anger?”

Baghraj, intrigued, agreed. “Weave me a *panis* that captures the spirit of the jungle. If it pleases me, I will let you pass. If not…” He left the threat hanging in the air.

Suanlo Suanlo returned to Golakpara and spent days weaving. He didn’t use ordinary cotton. He gathered fibers from the *tula* plant, dyed them with the juice of berries and roots, and incorporated feathers shed by the jungle birds. He wove into the cloth images of the Karnafuli River, the towering trees, and the very stripes of Baghraj himself. He chanted *mantras* (Buddhist prayers) with each pass of the shuttle, imbuing the cloth with his respect for the forest.

Finally, the *panis* was finished. It shimmered with life, a miniature jungle captured in threads. He carried it back to the spot where he’d met Baghraj, performing a small *puja* (prayer ritual) with incense and flowers before laying the cloth at the tiger’s feet.

Baghraj examined the *panis* with a critical eye. He circled it, sniffing, then lay down upon it, testing its softness. A low rumble, not of anger this time, but of satisfaction, emanated from his chest. 

“This… this is truly a gift worthy of the forest,” Baghraj admitted. “You have shown respect, and skill. You may pass, Suanlo Suanlo. But remember this: the forest provides, but it demands respect in return.”

Suanlo Suanlo continued on his journey, delivering the *poi* to his *thamma*. But Baghraj wasn’t finished with him.  

Years later, Suanlo Suanlo had become a renowned weaver, his *panis* sought after by villages far and wide. He had married and had a son, little Rajan. One day, a greedy merchant from Chittagong arrived in Golakpara, offering a fortune for Suanlo Suanlo’s weaving skills. He wanted him to weave only for him, to exploit the demand for his work. 

Suanlo Suanlo refused. He wouldn’t abandon his village, his people, and the traditions he held dear. The merchant, enraged, spread rumors, accusing Suanlo Suanlo of hoarding wealth and disrespecting the authorities.  He even bribed some villagers to turn against him.

One night, Suanlo Suanlo’s house was burned to the ground.  He and his family barely escaped with their lives.  Desperate and heartbroken, Suanlo Suanlo wandered back into the jungle, seeking solace. 

He found Baghraj, older now, but still majestic.  “Great Baghraj,” he cried, “I have been wronged! My home is gone, my reputation ruined, all because I refused to betray my people!”

Baghraj listened patiently.  “You showed me respect once, Suanlo Suanlo. Now, I will return the favor.”

That night, Baghraj unleashed his fury upon the merchant and those who had conspired against Suanlo Suanlo. He didn’t kill them, but he terrified them, destroying their goods and driving them from the village.  The villagers, witnessing Baghraj’s wrath, realized the truth and apologized to Suanlo Suanlo.

With the help of his community, Suanlo Suanlo rebuilt his home, even more beautiful than before. He continued to weave, his *panis* now imbued with a deeper understanding of the delicate balance between humanity and nature. 

Old Man Shanti finished his story, the mist swirling around him. “So you see, *chhele*, the tale of Suanlo Suanlo teaches us that respect for the forest, for our traditions, and for each other, is the greatest protection of all. And that even a tiger remembers a kindness.” He paused, then added with a knowing smile, “And sometimes, a little cleverness can go a long way.”



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:**  Use of *boudi, chhele, didi, thamma, Karbaribapa* to reflect the importance of family and social hierarchy in Chakma society.
* **Traditional Clothing:**  Mention of *panis* as a significant cultural garment.
* **Food & Offerings:** *Poi* and *achhar* as traditional food items and offerings.
* **Religious Practices:**  Incorporation of *pranam, puja, mantras* reflecting Buddhist beliefs.
* **Social Structure:**  The role of the *Karbari* as the village headman.
* **Respect for Nature:**  The reverence for the tiger (*bagh*) and the forest as a sacred space.
* **Rituals:** The small *puja* performed before offering the *panis*.
* **Storytelling Style:** The narrative is framed as an oral tale told by an elder, with a conversational tone and moral lesson.
* **Local Geography:** Specific mention of Rangamati and the Karnafuli River grounds the story in its geographical context.



This retelling aims to be more than just a translation; it strives to capture the *feeling* of a Rangamati folktale, imbued with the cultural values and beliefs of the Chakma people.
