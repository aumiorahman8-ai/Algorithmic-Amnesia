## Fifteen Sizzles, Three to Eat, The She-goat's Sign Is By The Binna Tree: A Moulvibazar Tale

Old Man Haru Charan, a *Jomidar* (landlord) of modest means in the villages around Kulaura, Moulvibazar, was known for two things: his stubbornness and his love for *ilish* (Hilsa fish). He wasn’t a cruel man, mind you, just… fixed in his ways. His wife, *Boudi* (older brother’s wife - a respectful term for his wife) Shanti Debi, a woman weathered like the tea gardens, often sighed at his inflexibility. They had one son, Gopal, a good-natured lad but easily swayed.

Now, Haru Charan had a peculiar habit. Every year, during the *Nabanno* (harvest festival), he’d insist on preparing fifteen *ilish bhaja* (fried Hilsa) – a truly extravagant amount for a family of three.  “Fifteen sizzles, *Shanti*!” he’d boom, his voice echoing through the bamboo walls of their house. “Fifteen sizzles to thank Dhanadevta (the rice goddess) for a good harvest! We’ll eat three, offer three to the *Brahmin* (priest), and the rest… well, the rest are for the spirits of the land!”

Shanti Debi knew better. The “spirits” were mostly devoured by the village dogs under the cover of darkness.  She’d try to reason with him, “*Dada* (respected elder), fifteen *ilish* is too much! We can offer a smaller, more manageable amount. Think of the cost!” But Haru Charan would just wave her off, muttering about tradition and the importance of appeasing the unseen forces.

This year, however, things were different. A particularly lean season had hit the village. *Ilish* were scarce and expensive.  Gopal, worried about his father’s pride and their dwindling savings, managed to procure only eight *ilish*. He hid the truth from Haru Charan, hoping to find a way to make up the difference.

When Haru Charan discovered the shortfall, he flew into a rage. “Eight?! Eight *ilish* for fifteen sizzles?! This is an insult to Dhanadevta! The harvest will be cursed!  We must find seven more, *Gopal*!  Seven more, or face the wrath of the spirits!”

Gopal, desperate, confided in his childhood friend, Ramjan, a Muslim boy who worked as a cowherd. Ramjan, a clever lad, suggested a risky plan. “*Dada* Haru Charan believes in the power of signs.  There’s an old legend about a white she-goat appearing near the *Binna* tree (a specific type of tree considered sacred in the region) when someone is in dire need. If you can convince him a she-goat has appeared, he might be satisfied with a different offering.”

Gopal was hesitant.  Deceiving his father felt wrong. But the thought of a cursed harvest and his family’s hardship spurred him on.  He spent the next day secretly painting a regular goat white with rice paste, then led it near the ancient *Binna* tree on the edge of the village. 

That evening, he dramatically announced to Haru Charan, “*Baba* (father), a miracle! A white she-goat! It appeared by the *Binna* tree, grazing peacefully. It must be a sign from Dhanadevta, accepting our offering even with fewer *ilish*!”

Haru Charan, his eyes wide with superstitious awe, rushed to the *Binna* tree. He examined the goat, the rice paste barely holding, but his mind was already convinced.  “A white she-goat!  Indeed, Dhanadevta is merciful!  The spirits are appeased!  This is a powerful sign!”

He returned home, a relieved man.  He allowed Gopal to proceed with the *Nabanno* preparations, using the eight *ilish* and offering three to the *Brahmin*.  The rest were, as usual, quietly enjoyed by the village dogs.

However, the story doesn’t end there.  The next morning, the village *Brahmin*, a wise and observant man, visited Haru Charan. He’d noticed the suspiciously white goat and the faint smell of rice paste.  He gently questioned Gopal, who, overcome with guilt, confessed everything.

The *Brahmin* didn’t scold them. Instead, he smiled knowingly.  “Haru Charan *Jomidar*, your stubbornness almost led to a greater deception.  Dhanadevta doesn’t need fifteen sizzles or a painted goat. She needs sincerity and gratitude.  The true offering is a thankful heart and a willingness to share what you have.”

Haru Charan, humbled and ashamed, finally understood. He apologized to Gopal and vowed to be more reasonable in the future.  From that day on, the number of *ilish* prepared for *Nabanno* was reduced, and the focus shifted from extravagant displays to genuine gratitude and community sharing. 

And the tale of the fifteen sizzles, the three to eat, and the she-goat’s sign by the *Binna* tree became a beloved story in Moulvibazar, a reminder that true devotion lies not in outward show, but in the sincerity of the heart.  It’s still told during *Nabanno*, often with a chuckle and a knowing glance towards the ancient *Binna* trees that dot the landscape.



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:**  Use of *Boudi, Dada, Baba* to reflect familial respect.
* **Religious Beliefs:**  Emphasis on Dhanadevta (rice goddess) and the importance of appeasing spirits.  The role of the *Brahmin* as a spiritual guide.
* **Rituals:**  Detailed description of *Nabanno* (harvest festival) and the offering of *ilish* to the goddess and the priest.
* **Social Hierarchy:**  Mention of *Jomidar* (landlord) and the social dynamics of the village.
* **Interfaith Harmony:** Inclusion of Ramjan, a Muslim character, demonstrating a degree of communal coexistence.
* **Specific Flora:**  The significance of the *Binna* tree as a sacred space.
* **Culinary Detail:**  Emphasis on *ilish bhaja* (fried Hilsa) as a prized delicacy.
* **Regional Specificity:**  Setting the story in Kulaura, Moulvibazar, grounding it in a specific geographical location.



This retelling aims to capture the essence of a Bengali folktale, preserving its cultural richness and the subtle nuances of life in a rural Moulvibazar village.  It’s a story about tradition, deception, and ultimately, the importance of sincerity and gratitude.
