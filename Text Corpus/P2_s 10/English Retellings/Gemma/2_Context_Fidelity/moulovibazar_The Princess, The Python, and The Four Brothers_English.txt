## The Princess, the Python & The Four Dadas: A Tale from Moulovibazar

The scent of *sholpa-jol* (light rain) clung to the tea gardens around Kulaura in Moulovibazar, a land known for its lush green and stories whispered on the wind. This tale, *thakuma* (grandmother) used to say, happened long ago, when the world felt closer to the spirits. It concerns Rajkumari (Princess) Chandrabala, the only daughter of Raja Jagat Singh, the ruler of a small *zamindari* (estate). 

Chandrabala wasn’t known for her beauty, though she was pleasant enough. Her true gift was her *bhakti* (devotion) – she spent hours in the *thakur-ghor* (family temple) praying to Thakurani (the Goddess), and was renowned for her kindness to all, from the *chawkidar* (gatekeeper) to the poorest *bhatiali* (boatmen) on the Monu River.

Now, Raja Jagat Singh, worried about his daughter’s future, announced a *shobha-yatra* (procession) and a challenge. He declared he would give Chandrabala’s hand in marriage to the man who could bring him the most precious treasure in the land. Many *zamindars*, merchants, and brave hunters came, offering jewels, silks, and even a magnificent elephant. But the Raja remained unimpressed.

Then came four *dadas* (elder brothers) – not of noble birth, but known for their courage and skill. There was Haru Dada, strong as a bull; Boru Dada, clever and quick-witted; Madhu Dada, a master archer; and finally, the youngest, Lachu Dada, quiet and observant, but with a heart full of *dhairyo* (patience). They weren’t wealthy, but they were respected in the villages for their honesty.

“We have no riches to offer, Maharaj,” Haru Dada said, bowing respectfully. “But we will seek the greatest treasure and bring it to you.”

The Raja, intrigued by their sincerity, agreed. The four *dadas* set off, travelling deep into the *jungle* (forest) bordering the estate. After days of searching, they stumbled upon a clearing. In the center, coiled around a massive banyan tree, lay a python – not just any python, but *Boropith*, a creature of immense size and ancient power, said to be a guardian of the forest. 

Boropith wasn’t aggressive, but spoke in a voice like rustling leaves. “You seek treasure? I possess the greatest treasure in this land, but it is not for taking. It is the *shanti* (peace) of the forest, the balance of nature. But… I am cursed. A wicked sorcerer, angered by my protection of these lands, has bound my spirit. Only the tears of a truly compassionate heart can break the spell.”

The *dadas* were perplexed. How could tears be a treasure? Haru Dada, ever the pragmatist, suggested they try to fight the python, hoping to force it to reveal its treasure. Boru Dada proposed trickery. Madhu Dada readied his bow, thinking to wound the creature and then negotiate. But Lachu Dada, remembering Chandrabala’s gentle nature, stepped forward.

He sat before Boropith and listened. He listened to the python’s sorrowful tale, to the lament for the dying trees, the dwindling animals, and the encroaching greed of men. He spoke of Chandrabala, of her kindness, her devotion, and her unwavering belief in the goodness of all beings.  As he spoke, a single tear rolled down his cheek, landing on Boropith’s scales.

Then another, and another.  Lachu Dada wasn’t crying from sadness, but from a deep empathy, a shared sorrow for the suffering of the world.  With each tear, Boropith’s scales seemed to shimmer, the air around them growing lighter. 

Finally, with the last tear, a blinding light erupted. When the *dadas* could see again, Boropith was gone. In its place lay a single, perfect lotus flower, radiating a soft, golden glow.  

“This,” a voice echoed, “is the true treasure. The purity of a compassionate heart, manifested in the beauty of nature.”

The *dadas* returned to the Raja, presenting the lotus flower.  Raja Jagat Singh, though surprised by the humble offering, felt a profound peace wash over him. He understood. He had been searching for material wealth, but the true treasure was something far more valuable.

He asked the *dadas* about their journey, and Lachu Dada recounted their encounter with Boropith. The Raja, deeply moved, declared Lachu Dada the winner.  

Before the *biye* (wedding) could take place, however, a traditional *haldi* (turmeric paste application) ceremony was held. Chandrabala, dressed in a *benarasi* (silk saree) the color of the setting sun, applied the turmeric to Lachu Dada’s forehead, chanting prayers for a long and happy life.  The *barat* (wedding procession) was a joyous affair, filled with the sounds of *dhak* drums and the blessings of the villagers.

And so, Rajkumari Chandrabala married Lachu Dada, not for his wealth or strength, but for his compassionate heart. They ruled the *zamindari* with wisdom and kindness, always remembering the lesson of Boropith – that the greatest treasure lies not in what we possess, but in the empathy we share with all living things.  And to this day, in the villages around Moulovibazar, *thakumas* tell this tale, reminding everyone that a single tear of compassion can be more powerful than any sword or spell.



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:** Use of *thakuma* (grandmother), *dada* (elder brother), *Maharaj* (King).
* **Specific Rituals:** Mention of *shobha-yatra* (procession), *thakur-ghor* (family temple), *bhakti* (devotion), *haldi* ceremony, *barat* (wedding procession).
* **Local Terms:** *Sholpa-jol* (light rain), *zamindari* (estate), *bhatiali* (boatmen), *jungle* (forest), *dhairyo* (patience), *shanti* (peace), *biye* (wedding).
* **Religious Context:**  Reference to Thakurani (the Goddess) and the importance of prayer.
* **Social Hierarchy:**  Acknowledging the different social classes – Raja, *zamindars*, merchants, and the common villagers.
* **Geographical Specificity:**  Setting the story in Kulaura, Moulovibazar, and mentioning the Monu River and tea gardens.
* **Emphasis on Values:**  The story highlights Bengali values like compassion, empathy, humility, and respect for nature.



This retelling aims to be as authentic as possible, drawing on the rich cultural tapestry of Moulovibazar and the traditions of Bengali folklore.  It's a story meant to be *shona* (heard) and *bola* (told) around a flickering lamp, carrying the wisdom of generations.
