## Monri and Sa-Tha-Nu: A Tale from the Chakma Hills of Rangamati

The mist clung to the Tanchana hills like a lover’s embrace, and the Lumah (Chakma village) of Golakpara hummed with the quiet rhythm of life. Old Maama Chitta, his face etched with the stories of generations, would often tell the tale of Monri and Sa-Tha-Nu, a story whispered on the wind, carried down from the time when the world was younger and the boundaries between heaven and earth were thin.

Monri, a beautiful Chakma girl with eyes like polished jade and hair the colour of monsoon clouds, was known throughout the Lumah for her kindness. She wasn’t a *Chong*, a princess, but the daughter of a respected *Karbari* (village headman), Baba Jatin. She spent her days weaving *panis* (traditional lower garment) on her loom, her fingers flying with practiced grace, and tending to the *jhum* (shifting cultivation) fields with her *Nani* (grandmother). 

One day, while collecting *bansi* (bamboo shoots) near the Khagrachari forest, Monri heard a melody unlike any she’d ever known. It wasn’t the call of the *koyal* (cuckoo) or the rustle of the leaves, but a sound that resonated deep within her soul. Following the music, she came to a clearing bathed in an ethereal glow. There, descending from the clouds on a ladder of moonlight, was Sa-Tha-Nu, a celestial being, a *Devata* from the realm above. He was breathtakingly handsome, his skin shimmering like pearl, and his eyes held the wisdom of ages.

Sa-Tha-Nu, captivated by Monri’s beauty and gentle spirit, fell instantly in love. He revealed he was a guardian of the mountains, tasked with protecting the forests and the people. He courted her with gifts of shimmering dew drops and songs woven from the whispers of the wind.  Monri, though initially awestruck, found herself drawn to his kindness and the way he spoke of the world with reverence.  

Their love blossomed in secret, meeting under the ancient *Bodhi* tree at the edge of the forest.  Sa-Tha-Nu taught Monri the language of the birds and the secrets of the healing herbs. He promised to take her to his celestial realm, a place of eternal beauty and peace. But Baba Jatin, a pragmatic man steeped in tradition, had already chosen a husband for Monri – a strong, respected *Chong* from a neighboring Lumah, a match that would strengthen their community.

When Baba Jatin discovered Monri’s secret meetings with Sa-Tha-Nu, he was furious.  He saw Sa-Tha-Nu as a *bhut* (spirit), a dangerous being who would bring misfortune to their family. He forbade Monri from seeing him, and preparations for the wedding began.  

Monri was heartbroken. She pleaded with her Baba, explaining her love for Sa-Tha-Nu, but he remained firm.  “A *Devata* cannot be a husband for a daughter of the earth, Monri!  It is against the *riwaj* (custom)!” he thundered. 

Desperate, Monri sought the counsel of her Nani, a woman known for her wisdom and knowledge of ancient rituals. Nani, after listening patiently, advised her, “Child, the *Devata* world and our world are different. To bridge the gap, you must prove your devotion. You must perform the *Deo-Chandi Puja* (a complex Hindu ritual dedicated to the goddess Durga) for seven consecutive nights, offering your purest intentions and unwavering faith. If your love is true, the goddess will guide you.”

Monri, with a heavy heart but renewed hope, began the *Deo-Chandi Puja*.  She fasted, chanted mantras, and offered flowers and incense to the goddess.  The Lumah whispered about her devotion, some with skepticism, others with a glimmer of hope.  On the seventh night, as the final *arati* (ceremonial offering of light) was performed, the forest around the Lumah began to tremble.  A blinding light erupted from the Bodhi tree, and Sa-Tha-Nu appeared, his face filled with worry.

But he wasn’t alone.  Beside him stood the goddess Durga, radiant and powerful.  She addressed Baba Jatin, “Your daughter’s devotion is unwavering.  Her love for Sa-Tha-Nu is pure and true.  However, a *Devata* cannot fully reside on earth, nor can a human fully live in the heavens.  To unite them, a sacrifice must be made.”

The goddess decreed that Monri and Sa-Tha-Nu could be together, but only for half the year.  For six months, Monri would live with Sa-Tha-Nu in his celestial realm, experiencing eternal beauty and joy.  For the other six months, she would return to her Lumah, tending to her family and her people, reminding Sa-Tha-Nu of the beauty and struggles of the earthly world.

Baba Jatin, humbled by the goddess’s presence and witnessing his daughter’s unwavering faith, finally relented.  The wedding was performed, a blend of earthly and celestial traditions.  Monri, adorned in a *panis* woven with moonlight threads gifted by Sa-Tha-Nu, became the bridge between two worlds.

And so, to this day, the Chakma people of Rangamati tell the tale of Monri and Sa-Tha-Nu.  They say that during the monsoon season, when the mist hangs heavy on the hills, you can sometimes catch a glimpse of Monri ascending to the heavens with her beloved, a reminder that even the greatest divides can be bridged by love, devotion, and the blessings of the goddess.  The story serves as a reminder of the importance of respecting tradition, the power of faith, and the enduring strength of love that transcends all boundaries.  



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:**  *Maama* (uncle), *Baba* (father), *Nani* (grandmother), *Chong* (princess/high-born woman)
* **Social Structure:**  *Karbari* (village headman), *Lumah* (village)
* **Traditional Practices:** *Jhum* cultivation, weaving *panis*, *Deo-Chandi Puja*, *arati*, *riwaj* (custom)
* **Spiritual Beliefs:**  Belief in *Devatas* (celestial beings), *bhuts* (spirits), and the goddess Durga.
* **Flora & Fauna:** Specific mention of *bansi* (bamboo shoots), *koyal* (cuckoo), and the *Bodhi* tree.
* **Language:**  Incorporation of Bengali and Chakma words and phrases.
* **Storytelling Style:**  The framing of the tale as being told by an elder (*Maama Chitta*) adds authenticity and emphasizes the oral tradition.



This retelling aims to be respectful and accurate to the cultural context of the Rangamati region, while still being engaging and accessible to a wider audience.  It’s important to remember that folktales evolve with each telling, and this is just one interpretation of the beautiful story of Monri and Sa-Tha-Nu.
