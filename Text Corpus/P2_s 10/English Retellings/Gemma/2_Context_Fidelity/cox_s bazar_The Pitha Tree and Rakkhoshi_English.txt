## The Pitha Tree and Rakkhoshi: A Tale from Cox's Bazar

Old Man Haripada, a *jethamoshai* (respected elder uncle) to everyone in the village of Himchari, near Cox’s Bazar, was known for two things: his skill with a *tat* (loom) weaving the finest *jamdani* and his love for *pitha*. Not just any *pitha*, mind you, but the *chandrapul* – delicate, moon-shaped rice pancakes filled with sweet coconut and *gur* (jaggery). He’d spend the whole winter making them, offering them to *Thakur* (the village deity) during *Puja*, and sharing them with everyone, especially the children.

Now, Haripada *dada* (grandfather) had a small plot of land, and in the middle of it grew a peculiar tree. It wasn’t a mango, or a jackfruit, or even a coconut tree common to these parts. This tree bore *pitha*. Yes, you heard right. Every winter, instead of fruit, it blossomed with perfectly formed *chandrapul pitha*, warm and fragrant. The villagers believed it was blessed by *Thakur* himself, a gift for Haripada’s piety.

But the blessing came with a shadow.  Deep in the dense *jhau* forest bordering the village lived Rakkhoshi, a fearsome female demon. She wasn’t like the *petni* (ghosts) who just caused mischief. Rakkhoshi craved the sweetness of the *pitha* tree, but she couldn’t simply take them. The tree was protected by Haripada’s devotion and the blessings of *Thakur*.  She could only reach it during the darkest night of the month, *Amavasya* (new moon), when the protective energy was at its weakest.

For years, Rakkhoshi would try. She’d send chilling winds to shake the tree, hoping to dislodge the *pitha*, but Haripada would always be awake, chanting *mantras* and offering *dhup* (incense) to *Thakur*. She’d try to disguise herself as a lost *boudi* (elder sister-in-law), weeping and asking for shelter, hoping to get close enough to steal the *pitha*. But Haripada, wise and observant, always saw through her disguise, recognizing the cold, unnatural glint in her eyes.

One *Amavasya*, Rakkhoshi devised a cunning plan. She transformed herself into Haripada’s beloved *nani* (grandmother), the woman who had taught him to make *pitha* in the first place.  She appeared at his door, frail and trembling, claiming to have lost her way in the forest. 

“*Dada*, my dear grandson,” she croaked, her voice mimicking Haripada’s *nani* perfectly. “I am so cold and hungry. Just a single *chandrapul* would warm my old bones.”

Haripada’s heart ached. He remembered his *nani*’s gentle hands, her stories, the sweet smell of *gur* and coconut filling her kitchen. He almost fell for the deception. But then, he noticed something. His *nani* always wore a silver *bali* (bracelet) on her wrist, a gift from his grandfather. This woman had none. 

He quickly performed a small *shanti puja* (peace offering), lighting a *diya* (oil lamp) and offering a handful of rice to *Thakur*.  Then, he spoke, his voice firm but filled with sorrow.

“*Nani*, you are a beautiful illusion. Rakkhoshi, reveal yourself!”

The illusion shattered. The frail old woman transformed into the terrifying Rakkhoshi, her eyes burning like coals, her teeth like shards of glass.  She roared with fury, shaking the very ground. 

“You old fool! You have thwarted me for the last time!” she shrieked, lunging for the *pitha* tree.

But Haripada was prepared. He had anticipated this. He quickly took a handful of *akshata* (unbroken rice grains) mixed with turmeric, a sacred offering, and threw it at Rakkhoshi, chanting a powerful *mantra* taught to him by a travelling *sadhu* (holy man). 

The turmeric and rice burned Rakkhoshi like fire. She screamed in agony, shrinking back into the forest, vowing revenge. But the *mantra* had weakened her.  She could no longer approach the *pitha* tree.

From that day on, Rakkhoshi never troubled the village again.  Haripada *dada* continued to make *pitha*, sharing them with everyone.  The *pitha* tree flourished, a symbol of faith, devotion, and the power of recognizing true intentions.  And every *Amavasya*, the villagers would gather around the tree, lighting *diyas* and chanting *mantras* to *Thakur*, remembering the tale of Haripada *dada* and the Rakkhoshi who craved the sweetness of the blessed *pitha* tree.  They knew that even the sweetest blessings needed protecting, and that true devotion could ward off even the darkest of evils.



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:**  Use of *jethamoshai, dada, boudi, nani* to reflect Bengali family structure and respect.
* **Religious Practices:**  *Puja, Thakur, mantras, shanti puja, dhup, diya, akshata* – all integral parts of Bengali Hindu religious life.
* **Food Culture:**  Emphasis on *pitha* (specifically *chandrapul*) as a central part of Bengali cuisine and winter celebrations.  The importance of *gur* and coconut in *pitha* making.
* **Folklore Elements:**  The presence of *Rakkhoshi* (a specific type of demon in Bengali folklore), *petni* (ghosts), and *sadhu* (holy men).
* **Geographical Specificity:**  Setting the story in Himchari, near Cox’s Bazar, and referencing the *jhau* forest, grounding it in a specific location.
* **Social Customs:**  The act of offering *pitha* to *Thakur* and sharing with the community.
* **Language:**  Incorporation of Bengali words and phrases throughout the narrative to maintain authenticity.



This retelling aims to capture the spirit of a traditional Bengali folktale, preserving its cultural richness and nuances while making it accessible to a wider audience.  The story emphasizes the importance of faith, wisdom, and the power of recognizing deception, all within the context of a vibrant Bengali community.
