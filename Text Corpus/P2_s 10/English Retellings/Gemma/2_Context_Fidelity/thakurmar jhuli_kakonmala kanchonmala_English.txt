## Kakonmala Kanchonmala: A Tale from Thakurmar Jhuli

Old Thakurma (grandmother) settled deeper into her woven mat, her eyes twinkling as she began, “Shono, shono (listen, listen) children, today I’ll tell you the story of Kakonmala and Kanchonmala. It’s a tale of sisters, of jealousy, and of the blessings of Ma Manasa, the Snake Goddess.

Once, in a village nestled beside the Ichhamati river, lived a poor Brahmin, Haripada Pandit. He and his wife, Shanti Devi, had two daughters, Kakonmala, the elder, and Kanchonmala, the younger. Kakonmala was dark-skinned, but possessed a gentle heart and a voice like the cooing of a dove. Kanchonmala, however, was fair as the moon, but a little sharp-tongued and prone to vanity. 

Haripada Pandit, despite his poverty, was a learned man. He performed *puja* (worship) diligently, offering *bhog* (sacred food) to the gods. But their lives were hard. One day, he went to a distant town seeking alms. While returning, he stumbled upon a hidden treasure – a chest overflowing with gold coins!  

Overjoyed, Haripada returned home and used the wealth to build a beautiful house, buy fine clothes, and arrange a grand wedding for his daughters.  He decided to marry them both to the sons of a wealthy merchant from a neighboring village.  The elder son, Rajendra, was a kind and thoughtful man, and he was betrothed to Kakonmala. The younger, arrogant son, Madan, was promised to Kanchonmala.

Now, Kanchonmala, seeing her sister about to marry the better man, felt a pang of jealousy. She couldn’t bear the thought of Kakonmala being happier than her.  She confided in her *didi* (elder sister), “Didi, I don’t want to marry Madan. He’s cruel and boastful. I want Rajendra! He’s so good and gentle.”

Kakonmala, ever the loving sister, tried to reason with her. “Kanchonmala, don’t speak like that!  Our father has arranged the marriages. We must accept our fate with grace.  Besides, Madan isn’t so bad, you just need to get to know him.”

But Kanchonmala wouldn’t listen. She hatched a wicked plan. On the day of the *gaye holud* (ceremony where turmeric paste is applied to the bride and groom), she secretly switched places with Kakonmala!  She dressed in Kakonmala’s wedding *sari* (robe) and sat where Kakonmala was supposed to be, while Kakonmala was hidden away, weeping in a locked room.

Madan, blinded by Kanchonmala’s beauty, didn’t notice the switch. He married Kanchonmala, believing he was marrying Kakonmala. Rajendra, however, felt a strange unease. The woman before him lacked Kakonmala’s gentle spirit and melodious voice. He suspected something was amiss, but his father insisted it was just wedding jitters.

Life with Madan proved miserable for Kanchonmala. He was indeed cruel and constantly berated her. She regretted her deceit, but it was too late. Meanwhile, Kakonmala, heartbroken and alone, spent her days praying to Ma Manasa, the Snake Goddess, seeking solace. She offered *pujo* with *dhaan* (rice) and *durba* grass, pleading for help.

Ma Manasa, touched by Kakonmala’s devotion, decided to intervene. She sent a *sap* (snake) to Kanchonmala’s house. The snake bit Madan, and he fell gravely ill.  The *vaidyas* (doctors) tried everything, but nothing worked.  

In desperation, Madan confessed to his father that he suspected he had married the wrong sister.  He revealed his doubts about the bride’s identity.  Haripada Pandit was summoned, and the truth finally came out. 

Filled with remorse, Madan begged for forgiveness. He then went to Kakonmala, confessed his wrongdoing, and pleaded with her to save his life.  Kakonmala, despite everything, had a forgiving heart. She prayed to Ma Manasa, and the Snake Goddess, pleased with her compassion, cured Madan.

Rajendra, finally understanding the deception, married Kakonmala. Kanchonmala, humbled and repentant, remained with Madan, learning to live with the consequences of her actions.  

Thakurma paused, adjusting her shawl. “So you see, children, beauty fades, but a good heart endures.  And remember, Ma Manasa protects those who are devoted and compassionate.  Never let jealousy poison your heart, and always respect your elders and the traditions of our *parampara* (heritage).” 

She smiled, and the children, wide-eyed, leaned closer, already anticipating the next tale from Thakurmar Jhuli.  The scent of *dhupa* (incense) lingered in the air, a reminder of the power of faith and the enduring wisdom of the old stories.
