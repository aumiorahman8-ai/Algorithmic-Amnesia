## Kironmala: A Tale from Thakurmar Jhuli

Old Thakurma (grandmother) settled deeper into her woven mat, the scent of *dhuno* (incense) curling around us. “Listen closely, children,” she began, her voice a low rumble, “for I will tell you the story of Kironmala, a tale of beauty, cunning, and the power of a mother’s love. It happened long ago, in the kingdom of Champavati…”

Champavati was ruled by a Raja, a good man but deeply saddened. His Rani, the queen, had passed away leaving him with no heir. He prayed and prayed to Lord Shiva, and finally, the gods granted him a daughter, a child so radiant she was named Kironmala – ‘rays of light’. 

Kironmala grew into a beauty beyond compare. Her skin was like polished sandalwood, her eyes like the petals of the *rajnigandha* flower, and her hair flowed like a river of black silk. But along with her beauty came a prophecy, delivered by a wandering *sadhu* (holy man): Kironmala would marry only when a prince could successfully string seven *malas* (garlands) made of seven different kinds of precious stones, each guarded by a fearsome creature.  If he failed, Kironmala would remain unmarried forever.

The Raja, though heartbroken at the thought of his daughter remaining alone, accepted the divine decree. He built seven separate *mandaps* (decorated platforms) for each *mala*, each guarded as the *sadhu* had foretold. 

The first *mala* was of rubies, guarded by a *rakshash* (demon) with a voice like thunder. The second, of emeralds, was protected by a giant serpent, its scales shimmering like a thousand sunsets. The third, of diamonds, was watched over by a fearsome *chakrabak* (a mythical bird with a human face).  The fourth, of pearls, was guarded by a swarm of stinging wasps. The fifth, of sapphires, by a monstrous crocodile. The sixth, of topaz, by a pack of wild, man-eating jackals. And finally, the seventh, of coral, was protected by a fearsome, fire-breathing lion.

Many princes, hearing of Kironmala’s beauty and the challenge, came to Champavati. Each, boasting of their strength and courage, attempted to string the *malas*. But one by one, they failed, defeated by the creatures and returning home humiliated. The Raja grew increasingly despondent.

Now, in a small village near Champavati lived a young man named Shantanu. He wasn’t a prince, but the son of a humble *julaha* (weaver). He was known for his intelligence and kindness, and he loved Kironmala from afar, hearing tales of her grace.  His *ma* (mother), a wise woman skilled in herbal remedies and ancient lore, encouraged him. “Shantanu, *beta* (son),” she said, “strength isn’t always in muscle, but in wit. Go, and trust in your intelligence.”

Shantanu, armed with his mother’s blessings and a bag filled with clever tools – a flute, a mirror, a handful of dates, a pot of honey, a strong rope, a bundle of fragrant *dhupa* (incense sticks), and a small, silver bell – set off for Champavati.

He approached the first *mandap* and, instead of fighting the *rakshash*, began to play a sweet melody on his flute. The *rakshash*, captivated by the music, forgot his duty and danced until he was exhausted, allowing Shantanu to string the ruby *mala*. 

For the serpent guarding the emeralds, Shantanu used the mirror. The serpent, seeing its own reflection, believed it was facing a rival and attacked its own image, giving Shantanu the opportunity to string the *mala*.

He offered the *chakrabak* dates, knowing the bird had a sweet tooth.  The wasp swarm he lulled to sleep with the fragrant *dhupa*. He distracted the crocodile with honey, and the jackals with a strong rope tied to a piece of meat, leading them away from the topaz *mala*.  Finally, he rang the silver bell near the lion, startling it just long enough to string the coral *mala*.

One by one, Shantanu overcame the guardians, stringing all seven *malas*. The entire kingdom rejoiced! The Raja, overjoyed, welcomed Shantanu and gave his blessing for him to marry Kironmala.

But the story doesn’t end there. A jealous *didi* (elder sister) of the Raja, who had hoped to marry Kironmala off to a powerful prince for political gain, plotted against Shantanu. She secretly replaced the real Shantanu with a look-alike during the wedding ceremony. 

Kironmala, however, was no fool. She noticed the subtle differences – the way he held his hand, the scent of his clothes.  She cleverly tested him, asking him about a secret promise he had made to his *ma* before leaving for Champavati. The imposter couldn’t answer. 

Kironmala revealed the deception, exposing her *didi’s* treachery. The Raja, furious, banished his sister.  And then, finally, she was united with the real Shantanu.

They ruled Champavati with wisdom and kindness, and Kironmala, the radiant princess, lived happily ever after with her beloved husband, proving that intelligence and a mother’s blessing are more powerful than any strength or royal lineage.”

Thakurma paused, her eyes twinkling. “So you see, children,” she said, “even a humble *julaha’s* son can win the heart of a princess, if he has courage, wit, and the love of his *ma*.”  She then reached for her *pan* (betel leaf) and *supari* (areca nut), signaling the end of the tale.  The scent of *dhuno* lingered, and we, the children, sat in quiet awe, lost in the magic of Kironmala’s story.



This retelling attempts to:

* **Preserve cultural nuances:**  Using Bengali kinship terms (*ma, beta, didi*), mentioning specific rituals like offering *dhuno*, and referencing traditional professions like *julaha*.
* **Maintain the folktale style:**  The narrative is framed as a story told by a grandmother, with a moral lesson at the end.
* **Include specific details:**  The descriptions of the *malas* and the creatures guarding them are detailed, as are the tools Shantanu uses.
* **Reflect Bengali values:**  The importance of a mother’s blessing, intelligence over brute force, and the triumph of good over evil are all central themes.
