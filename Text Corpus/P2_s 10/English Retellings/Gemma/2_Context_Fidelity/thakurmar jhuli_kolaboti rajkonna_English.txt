## Kolaboti Rajkonna: A Tale from Thakurmar Jhuli

Thakurma (grandmother) began, settling deeper into her woven stool, her voice a low hum that drew us children closer. “Shono, shono (listen, listen), I’ll tell you the story of Kolaboti Rajkonna, a tale of cleverness and courage. It happened long, long ago, in the kingdom of Kamrup, a land rich with paddy fields and the scent of *jolpai* flowers.”

Once upon a time, there lived a poor *baidyo* (physician) named Harish Chandra. He had no son, only a beautiful daughter, Kolaboti.  He loved her more than life itself.  When Kolaboti reached marriageable age, Harish Chandra worried. He was too poor to offer a proper *paan-poroman* (betrothal ceremony with betel leaves) to a suitable groom. 

One day, the King of Kamrup, Rajendra Sen, announced a contest. He desired a bride for his son, Prince Badal, but not just any bride. He declared, “Whoever can spin one hundred *tana* (weft threads) of the finest silk, using only moonlight as their light, and complete it before dawn, shall be my daughter-in-law!”

Many *rajkonna* (princesses) and daughters of wealthy *zamindars* (landlords) tried, but all failed. The silk was too delicate, the moonlight too faint. They’d complain to their *mashima* (mother’s sister) and *kakima* (father’s sister) about the impossible task, but to no avail. 

Kolaboti, hearing of the contest, pleaded with her *baba* (father). “Baba, let me try! Even if I fail, at least I will have tried.” Harish Chandra, though hesitant, finally agreed, knowing his daughter was quick-witted.

He built her a small *machan* (raised platform) in the courtyard, and Kolaboti began her work. But she faced a problem. The moonlight was indeed too weak.  She remembered her *dida* (grandmother) telling her stories of the *chandi-mela* (festival dedicated to Goddess Chandi) and the power of mantras. 

Kolaboti, a devout girl, began to chant a mantra to Goddess Chandi, praying for help.  As she chanted, a magical glow surrounded her *machan*.  Not from the moon, but from tiny, shimmering fireflies!  Thousands of them gathered, their lights illuminating the silk with a brilliance brighter than any lamp. 

But the jealous *bor-bou* (elder sister-in-law) of a neighboring *rajbari* (palace), seeing Kolaboti’s success, grew envious. She whispered to the King’s *mantri* (minister), “Maharaj, that girl is using magic! It’s not fair!”

The King, suspicious, sent his guards to investigate. They saw the fireflies and reported back to the King.  Rajendra Sen, furious, ordered the fireflies to be extinguished.  

As the guards tried to swat them away, Kolaboti, quick-thinking as ever, cried out, “Don’t! They are *jhinuk-poka* (tiny shell insects)! They are attracted to the scent of my hair! If you disturb them, they will bite and cause a terrible rash!”

The guards, fearing a painful rash, stopped immediately.  Kolaboti then cleverly sprinkled a little *henna* powder around the *machan*. The fireflies, attracted to the scent, settled on the henna, creating an even brighter glow.

By dawn, Kolaboti had spun all one hundred *tana* of the finest silk.  The King, astonished by her skill and cleverness, realized she was no ordinary girl. He questioned her about the fireflies, but Kolaboti humbly explained her devotion to Goddess Chandi and the natural attraction of the *jhinuk-poka*.

Prince Badal, captivated by Kolaboti’s beauty and intelligence, fell in love with her instantly.  The King happily arranged their marriage, with all the proper rituals – the *haldi* ceremony with turmeric paste, the *sangeet* with songs and dances, and the *biye* (wedding) itself, blessed by the *pandit* (priest).

Kolaboti Rajkonna, the daughter of a humble *baidyo*, became the Queen of Kamrup, proving that cleverness and devotion are more valuable than wealth and status.  And she always remembered to offer *pitha* (rice cakes) and flowers to Goddess Chandi, thanking her for her blessings.



Thakurma paused, her eyes twinkling. “So you see, children,” she said, “even the smallest among us can achieve great things with a little wit and a lot of faith.  Now, who wants some *payesh* (rice pudding)?”



This retelling attempts to capture the essence of the tale as found in Thakurmar Jhuli, including:

* **Kinship terms:** *Baba, Dida, Mashima, Kakima, Bor-bou* are used to reflect the familial relationships.
* **Cultural nuances:**  The importance of *paan-poroman*, the details of the silk spinning contest, the reverence for Goddess Chandi, and the fear of rashes from insects are all culturally specific.
* **Specific rituals:** Mention of *haldi, sangeet, biye* and the role of the *pandit* ground the story in Bengali wedding traditions.
* **Language:**  Use of Bengali words like *tana, jolpai, rajbari, zamindar, machan, pitha, payesh* adds authenticity.
* **Thakurma’s storytelling style:** The framing device of Thakurma telling the story and the concluding moral are characteristic of the Jhuli.



It's important to note that variations of this tale exist, but this version aims to be faithful to the spirit and details commonly found in the published collections of Thakurmar Jhuli.
