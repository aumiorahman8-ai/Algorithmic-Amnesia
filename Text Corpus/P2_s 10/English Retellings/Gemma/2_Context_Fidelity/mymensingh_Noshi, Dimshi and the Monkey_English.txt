## Noshi, Dimshi and the Monkey: A Mymensingh Tale

The air in the village of Borokola, near Mymensingh, hung thick and sweet with the scent of *shona rice* drying in the sun. Noshi and Dimshi were sisters-in-law, *boudi* and *nodi* as they were known, bound by marriage to the same family. Noshi was the elder, married to the gentle, hardworking Rohan, a farmer. Dimshi, younger and known for her quick wit, was wed to Rohan’s brother, the slightly more boisterous Gopal. 

They lived in a joint family, a *kutumb*, with Rohan and Gopal’s parents, *Dada* and *Dadi*. Life was simple, revolving around the rhythm of the seasons, the fields, and the constant bustle of a large household. But a shadow fell upon their peace.

Noshi, despite being a good wife, was known for her stinginess. She’d hoard the best bits of fish for herself, ration the *ghee* until it was practically translucent, and grumble if anyone asked for even a small handful of lentils. Dimshi, on the other hand, was generous to a fault, always offering a share of her cooking to neighbours and even the stray dogs. This difference often caused friction, with Dada and Dadi gently chiding Noshi for her parsimony.

One scorching afternoon, Rohan and Gopal went to the market in Mymensingh town to sell their harvest. Noshi and Dimshi were left to manage the house.  Dimshi was preparing *pitha* – sweet rice cakes – for the upcoming *Janmashtami* festival, a particularly auspicious occasion dedicated to Krishna. She’d carefully prepared the batter, adding fragrant cardamom and a generous amount of *gur* (jaggery). 

Noshi, seeing the abundance of *gur*, felt a familiar pang of possessiveness. “Dimshi,” she said, her voice tight, “You’re using far too much *gur*! It’s a waste. We need to save for the winter.”

Dimshi, used to her *boudi’s* complaints, simply smiled. “It’s Janmashtami, Noshi. We must offer something sweet to Thakur (Lord Krishna).  Generosity is a blessing.”

While they argued, a clever monkey, a *hanuman*, descended from the nearby mango grove. He’d been watching them, his bright eyes gleaming with mischief. He saw the steaming *pitha* and the pot of *gur*, and a plan formed in his cunning little mind.

He crept into the kitchen while Dimshi was momentarily distracted, and snatched the pot of *gur*.  He scampered up the tallest mango tree, stuffing his mouth with the sweet treat and showering the ground with sticky remnants.

“Thief! Thief!” Dimshi cried, rushing outside.  Noshi followed, but instead of being upset about the stolen *gur*, she was furious with Dimshi. “See! This is what happens when you’re so wasteful! If you hadn’t used so much *gur*, this wouldn’t have happened!”

Dimshi, exasperated, ignored her *boudi’s* blame.  She knew they needed the *gur* for the *Janmashtami* offerings.  She pleaded with the monkey, “Oh, clever *hanuman*, please return the *gur*! We need it for Thakur’s puja.”

The monkey, enjoying the chaos, just chattered and threw down a mango pit. 

Finally, Dimshi remembered a story her *Dadi* had told her.  “Monkeys love shiny things,” she recalled.  She quickly ran inside and returned with a small, tarnished brass mirror, a *darpun*, usually used for applying *kajol* (kohl) to the eyes. 

Holding it up, she called to the monkey, “Look, *hanuman*, a beautiful face!  Come and see!”

The monkey, captivated by his own reflection, dropped the pot of *gur* with a thud and cautiously descended the tree, reaching for the shimmering object.  Dimshi quickly snatched the mirror and, holding it firmly, retrieved the pot of *gur*.

Noshi, witnessing this, was speechless.  She hadn’t even thought to try and reason with the monkey, let alone use her wits. 

When Rohan and Gopal returned from the market, they were greeted with the story. Dada and Dadi, hearing of Noshi’s blame and Dimshi’s cleverness, smiled knowingly. 

Dada turned to Noshi. “My dear, a full granary is important, but a generous heart is even more so.  *Dharma* (righteousness) lies in sharing, not hoarding.  Dimshi showed true wisdom today, using her intelligence and remembering the old ways.”

Dadi added, “Remember, *boudi*, a house filled with love and kindness is richer than any treasure.”

Noshi, humbled and ashamed, finally understood.  She apologized to Dimshi and promised to be more generous.  From that day on, Noshi learned to share, and the household of Borokola was filled with even more joy and prosperity, blessed by Thakur and the cleverness of Dimshi, who had outsmarted the monkey and, more importantly, softened her *boudi’s* heart.  And the tale of Noshi, Dimshi, and the monkey became a beloved story, whispered amongst the women of Mymensingh, a reminder that a generous spirit is the greatest wealth of all.



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:** *Boudi* (older sister-in-law), *Nodi* (younger sister-in-law), *Dada* (grandfather), *Dadi* (grandmother).
* **Religious Observances:**  Mention of *Janmashtami* and *Thakur* (Lord Krishna) and the importance of *puja* (worship) and offerings.
* **Food Culture:** Specific mention of *shona rice*, *ghee*, lentils, *pitha* (rice cakes), and *gur* (jaggery) – staples of Bengali cuisine.
* **Household Structure:**  Emphasis on the *kutumb* (joint family) system.
* **Rituals & Beliefs:**  The belief in the cleverness of monkeys (*hanuman*) and the story passed down by *Dadi* (grandmother) highlighting oral tradition.  The use of a *darpun* (mirror) for applying *kajol* (kohl) is a traditional practice.
* **Concept of Dharma:** The importance of *dharma* (righteousness) and the value of generosity.
* **Geographical Specificity:**  Setting the story in Borokola, near Mymensingh, grounding it in a specific locale.



This retelling aims to capture the flavour and spirit of a traditional Bengali folktale, preserving the cultural details that make it unique and meaningful.  It's a story meant to be told and retold, carrying lessons of generosity, wisdom, and the importance of family.
