## Sheyal Pondit (The Jackal Scholar) - A retelling from Thakurmar Jhuli

Thakurma (grandmother) settled deeper into her rocking chair, the oil lamp casting dancing shadows on the walls. “Shono, shono (listen, listen),” she began, her voice a low rumble, “Today I’ll tell you the story of Sheyal Pondit, a tale of cunning and karma. It happened long, long ago, in a village nestled beside the Ichhamati river…”

In that village lived a poor Brahmin, Ramkanto Pundit. He wasn’t a *real* Pundit, mind you, just called that because his father had been one. Ramkanto was lazy, more interested in napping under the tamarind tree than reciting mantras or tending to his small plot of land. His wife, Shanti, a good woman with hands roughened by work, bore the brunt of everything. They had a young son, little Gopal, a bright-eyed boy who loved to hear stories.

Now, near the village lived a jackal, a *sheyal* as we call them. This wasn’t any ordinary jackal. He was clever, observant, and had a wicked glint in his eye. He’d often watch Ramkanto Pundit, noticing how the villagers treated him with respect, bringing offerings of rice and lentils, hoping for blessings. 

“Aha!” thought the Sheyal. “This Pundit life seems easy! No hard work, just chanting and receiving gifts. I want that too!”

One day, the Sheyal, with a mischievous plan brewing, found Ramkanto Pundit asleep under the tamarind tree. He carefully stole the Pundit’s *janju* (sacred thread), the symbol of his Brahminhood, and draped it around his own neck.  He then went to the village, strutting with a newfound air of importance.

The villagers, seeing the *janju*, immediately bowed. “Namaskar, Punditji!” they greeted him. 

The Sheyal, puffing out his chest, began to mimic Ramkanto’s chanting, making up nonsense syllables that sounded vaguely Sanskrit.  “Om bhur bhuvah swaha… khichuri bhaja!” (Om earth sky heaven… fry khichuri!).  The villagers, though a little confused, assumed he was reciting complex mantras. 

He started receiving offerings – rice, lentils, even a small fish! He ate heartily, enjoying the easy life. He even started giving ‘blessings’ – a quick lick of his nose and a grunt.  

Word reached Shanti, Ramkanto’s wife. She was shocked!  “What is happening?” she cried to her *shoshur bari* (parents-in-law), old and wise folks who lived nearby.  “Someone is pretending to be my husband and taking all our offerings!”

Her *shoshur* (father-in-law) said, “This is a grave matter. We must test this imposter.  Prepare a plate of hot *panta bhat* (fermented rice) with a very spicy *bhaja* (fry) – a fiery chilli fry. A true Brahmin would know the proper rituals before eating, especially something so potent. A fake will reveal himself.”

Shanti did as she was told. She presented the plate to the Sheyal Pundit, bowing respectfully.  “Punditji, please accept this humble offering. It is a small token of our devotion.”

The Sheyal, greedy as ever, didn’t bother with any rituals. He snatched the *panta bhat* and began to gobble it down.  The chilli hit him like a thunderbolt! He yelped, his eyes watering, and started frantically lapping at water, howling and jumping. 

“Oho! Oho!  Maa! (Mother!)” he cried, forgetting all pretense of being a Pundit.  “This is fire! This is burning my mouth!”

The villagers, witnessing this spectacle, realized the truth.  “It’s a Sheyal! A jackal pretending to be a Pundit!” they shouted, chasing him away with sticks and brooms.

The Sheyal, stripped of the *janju*, fled back into the jungle, humiliated and burning.  

Meanwhile, Ramkanto Pundit woke up, bewildered to find his *janju* missing.  He learned of the Sheyal’s deception and felt a pang of shame. He realized he hadn’t earned the respect he received.

He resolved to change. He started studying the scriptures diligently, tending to his land, and helping his neighbors. He became a *true* Pundit, earning respect through his knowledge and good deeds.

Thakurma paused, adjusting her shawl. “So you see, my children,” she said, “The Sheyal Pondit learned a hard lesson.  You can’t cheat your way to respect.  Karma always catches up.  And Ramkanto Pundit learned that true worth comes from honest effort, not from pretending to be something you’re not.  Remember that, always.” 

She smiled, a network of wrinkles crinkling around her eyes. “Now, who wants a little *muri* (puffed rice)?”



This retelling attempts to capture the flavour of Thakurmar Jhuli by:

* **Using Bengali terms:** *Thakurma, shono, janju, shoshur bari, shoshur, maa, panta bhat, bhaja, muri, sheyal, Punditji* are all used to maintain authenticity.
* **Highlighting cultural rituals:** The importance of the *janju* as a symbol of Brahminhood, the offering of food, and the specific test with the spicy *panta bhat* are all integral to the story's cultural context.
* **Preserving the narrative style:** The story is told in a conversational, grandmotherly tone, with pauses and direct address to the listeners.
* **Emphasizing moral lessons:** The tale’s core message about honesty, karma, and earning respect is clearly articulated.
* **Descriptive details:**  Details like the tamarind tree, the Ichhamati river, and the description of the food add to the story’s richness and evoke a sense of place.
