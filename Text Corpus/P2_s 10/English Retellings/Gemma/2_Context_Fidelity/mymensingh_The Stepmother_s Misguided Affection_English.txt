## The Stepmother's Misguided Affection (Mymensingh Ghar’s Tale)

This tale, *Meye Bou-er Maya*, is one old women in Mymensingh still whisper about, especially during the rainy season when the *kashful* blooms and the air hangs heavy with memory. It’s a story about a stepmother’s love, twisted by fear and a misunderstanding of what truly protects.

It began with Rohini, a beautiful girl, the only daughter of the Zamindar of Borobaria. Her mother died giving birth to her, and her father, the Zamindar Borhanuddin Chowdhury, remarried a woman named Maloti Devi, from a respectable but less wealthy *kul* (lineage). Maloti Devi was known for her piety, always performing *puja* and offering to the local *thakurani* (village goddess). But she carried a secret sorrow – she couldn’t bear children of her own.

Rohini grew into a girl of surpassing beauty, with eyes like the *kajal* used for *kajal-dano* (eye application ceremony) and a voice that could charm the *dok* birds from the trees. Maloti Devi, unable to have a child to call her own, showered all her affection on Rohini, but it was a possessive, anxious love. She dressed Rohini in the finest *jamdani* sarees, adorned her with gold *churi* (bangles) and *kanbalas* (earrings), and kept her close, almost *ghore bondhi kore rakhte* (keeping her confined to the house).

The problem was, Maloti Devi believed Rohini was too beautiful, too *shobhan* (splendid), for this world. She feared the *buri nazar* (evil eye) would fall upon her, bringing sickness or worse. She consulted astrologers, *jyotish*, who confirmed her fears, warning of a danger coming from a man with a “mark of the tiger” – a birthmark resembling a tiger’s stripe. 

From then on, Maloti Devi’s affection became suffocating. She forbade Rohini from going to the *baishakhi mela* (spring festival), from visiting her *fupu* (paternal aunt) in the neighboring village, even from standing near the window where men might pass. She insisted Rohini wear a heavy *ghomta* (veil) even within the *andar mahal* (inner quarters) and constantly chanted mantras for protection.  Rohini, though grateful for the love, felt like a caged *mayna pakhi* (mynah bird).

One day, a young man named Kamal, a distant relative from a branch of the Chowdhury family, came to visit. He was a handsome man, known for his bravery and kindness. He had a small, barely noticeable birthmark on his shoulder, a faint stripe that resembled a tiger’s claw.  Rohini, glimpsing him during a *namaz* break in the courtyard, felt a stirring in her heart. 

Maloti Devi, however, saw Kamal and instantly recognized the dreaded mark. Panic seized her. She believed Kamal was the very danger the astrologers had warned about.  Instead of confronting him, she devised a cruel plan.  She told Rohini that Kamal was a wicked man, a *dushmon* (enemy) who sought to harm her. She fabricated stories of his ill-intentions, painting him as a villain.

Then, she arranged for Rohini to be married to a much older, sickly man from a distant *zamindari*.  The groom, Haricharan, was known for his wealth but also for his cruel temper and failing health.  Rohini, heartbroken and terrified, pleaded with her stepmother, but Maloti Devi remained firm, convinced she was protecting her.  She reasoned, “Better a weak, old man than a handsome devil with a tiger’s mark!  This is for your *kalyan* (well-being), my child.”

The wedding was a somber affair. Rohini, dressed in a heavy red *benarasi* saree, looked like a wilting flower.  As she was being led away, she caught Kamal’s eye. He looked at her with confusion and sorrow, unable to understand the tragedy unfolding before him.

After the wedding, Rohini’s life became miserable. Haricharan was indeed cruel and demanding.  She suffered in silence, remembering Kamal and the life she could have had.  

Meanwhile, Maloti Devi, despite her efforts, found no peace. She constantly worried about Rohini, convinced the *buri nazar* still lingered.  One day, she fell gravely ill.  On her deathbed, she confessed everything to Borhanuddin Chowdhury – her fear, her deception, and her misguided attempt to protect Rohini.

Borhanuddin Chowdhury, enraged and heartbroken, immediately sent for Kamal. He learned the truth about the birthmark – it was a childhood injury, a scar from a playful wrestling match, not a sign of ill omen.  He arranged for Rohini’s marriage to be annulled, a difficult and scandalous undertaking, but he was determined to see his daughter happy.

Rohini and Kamal were finally united. They lived a long and happy life, raising a family and bringing prosperity to Borobaria.  Maloti Devi’s story became a cautionary tale, a reminder that love, even when well-intentioned, can be destructive when fueled by fear and ignorance. 

The tale is still told, especially by the older women, to remind everyone that true protection lies not in shielding someone from the world, but in preparing them to face it with courage and wisdom.  And that sometimes, the most dangerous tigers aren’t the ones with stripes, but the ones hidden within our own hearts.



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:** *Fupu* (paternal aunt), *Meye Bou* (daughter-in-law - used metaphorically for Rohini)
* **Social Hierarchy:**  The importance of *kul* (lineage) and the Zamindar’s status.
* **Religious Practices:** *Puja*, *Thakurani* (village goddess), *Jyotish* (astrologers), mantras.
* **Rituals & Customs:** *Kajal-dano* (eye application ceremony), *Baishakhi Mela* (spring festival), *Ghomta* (veil), *Namaz* (prayer), wedding attire (*jamdani*, *benarasi*), *churi* (bangles), *kanbalas* (earrings).
* **Beliefs:** *Buri Nazar* (evil eye), the power of astrology.
* **Language:** Use of Bengali words and phrases like *shobhan*, *dushmon*, *kalyan*, *ghore bondhi kore rakhte*, *mayna pakhi*.
* **Setting:**  Specific to the Mymensingh region, referencing *kashful* blooms and the landscape.



This retelling aims to capture the essence of the folktale as it would be told in Mymensingh, with its specific cultural context and emotional weight.  It's a story that speaks to the complexities of family relationships, the dangers of superstition, and the enduring power of true love.
