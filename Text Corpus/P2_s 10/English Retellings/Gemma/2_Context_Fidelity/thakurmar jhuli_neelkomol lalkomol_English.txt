## Neelkomol & Lalkomol: A Tale from Thakurmar Jhuli

Old Thakurma (grandmother) settled deeper into her woven mat, her eyes twinkling as she began, “Shono, shono (listen, listen) children, today I’ll tell you the story of Neelkomol and Lalkomol. It’s a tale as old as the Padma river itself, a story of devotion, trials, and the blessings of Ma Manasa.”

Once upon a time, in a village nestled beside the mighty Padma, lived a poor *brāhman* (priest) named Haricharan. He and his wife, Shanti Debi, had no children for many years. They prayed and prayed to all the *devtas* (gods), but to no avail. Finally, with a heavy heart, Haricharan went to the banks of the Padma to offer prayers to Ma Manasa, the serpent goddess, protector of fertility and fortune. 

He pleaded, “Ma Manasa, we are old and childless. Please, bless us with a son, even if he is born with a *bhāng* (a perceived flaw or disability) – we will accept him as your gift.”

Ma Manasa, pleased with his devotion, granted his wish. Shanti Debi gave birth to a beautiful son, but as Haricharan had feared, the boy, named Neelkomol, was born with legs as blue as the indigo flower – *neelkomol*.  He couldn’t walk like other children. 

Despite his difference, Neelkomol was a bright and gentle boy. He learned the *shastras* (scriptures) from his *pita* (father) and was deeply devoted to Ma Manasa.  He would spend hours crafting tiny boats of leaves and setting them afloat on the Padma, offering flowers and prayers to the goddess.

Years passed.  Haricharan, growing older, worried about Neelkomol’s future.  He knew finding a suitable *bouma* (daughter-in-law) for a son who couldn’t walk would be difficult.  

One day, a royal procession passed through their village.  The king, Rajendra Sen, had a daughter, Lalkomol, named for the vibrant red lotus flower – *lalkomol* – that bloomed in the palace pond.  Lalkomol was renowned for her beauty, her kindness, and her skill in music.  As the procession passed, Lalkomol caught sight of Neelkomol offering prayers by the river.  His devotion and gentle face captivated her.

She secretly sent a message to Haricharan through her *dasi* (maidservant), expressing her desire to marry Neelkomol.  Haricharan was astonished!  A princess wanting to marry his son?  He cautiously accepted, knowing it was Ma Manasa’s grace at work.

The wedding was a grand affair, filled with the sounds of *shahnai* (oboe) and the scent of *dhup* (incense).  But the king’s *mantri* (minister) and other courtiers were outraged.  “How can a princess marry a disabled man?” they whispered.  They plotted to discredit Neelkomol.

They told the king, “Maharaj, test your son-in-law!  Ask him to retrieve a rare pearl from the bottom of the Padma.  If he cannot, it proves he is unfit for your daughter.”

Rajendra Sen, though reluctant, agreed. He announced the challenge.  Neelkomol, hearing this, was heartbroken. He knew he couldn’t swim, let alone dive for a pearl.  He retreated to the riverbank and wept, praying to Ma Manasa.

Suddenly, the river began to churn. A magnificent *nagini* (serpent goddess, a manifestation of Ma Manasa) rose from the water, adorned with jewels.  “Neelkomol, my son,” she said, “your devotion has touched my heart. I will help you.”

The Nagini summoned seven *garudas* (mythical bird-like creatures, enemies of serpents) and commanded them to create a dry path across the Padma.  The garudas obeyed, and a shimmering path of sand appeared, leading straight to the spot where the pearl lay.  Neelkomol, using his hands to pull himself along, crawled across the path and retrieved the pearl.

The king and the court were stunned.  They realized Neelkomol was blessed by Ma Manasa.  The *mantri* and the courtiers, ashamed, apologized. 

But the trials weren’t over.  The *mantri*, still envious, devised another plan. He secretly replaced Lalkomol with a stone idol during the *shobha yatra* (procession celebrating the wedding).  When Neelkomol went to greet his bride, he found only a cold, lifeless stone.

Devastated, Neelkomol once again turned to Ma Manasa.  The Nagini appeared and revealed the deception.  She instructed Neelkomol to perform a *puja* (worship) with seven different types of leaves and seven different colored flowers, chanting a specific *mantra* (sacred verse) dedicated to Ma Manasa.

Neelkomol followed her instructions precisely. As he finished the *puja*, the stone idol shattered, and the real Lalkomol reappeared, unharmed.  The *mantri* was punished, and peace was restored.

From that day on, Neelkomol and Lalkomol lived happily together, their love a testament to devotion and the blessings of Ma Manasa.  Neelkomol, through Ma Manasa’s grace, eventually regained the use of his legs.  They ruled the kingdom with wisdom and compassion, always remembering the power of faith and the importance of accepting everyone, regardless of their differences.

Thakurma paused, her voice softening. “So you see, children, true beauty lies not in physical perfection, but in a pure heart and unwavering devotion. And Ma Manasa always protects those who seek her blessings with sincerity.”  She smiled, and the children, captivated by the tale, leaned closer, eager for another story from Thakurmar Jhuli.



**Cultural Nuances Preserved:**

* **Kinship Terms:**  *Pita* (father), *Bouma* (daughter-in-law), *Dasi* (maidservant), *Mantri* (minister).
* **Religious Elements:**  Emphasis on Ma Manasa, the serpent goddess, and the importance of *puja* and *mantras*.  The role of *nagini* as a manifestation of the goddess.  The reverence for *devtas* (gods).
* **Social Hierarchy:**  The story reflects the social structure of the time, with *brahmans* (priests), kings, and courtiers.
* **Rituals:**  The specific *puja* with leaves and flowers, the *shobha yatra* (wedding procession), offering prayers and boats to the river.
* **Symbolism:**  The blue and red lotus flowers (*neelkomol* and *lalkomol*) representing the couple, the Padma river as a sacred space.
* **Beliefs:** The belief in the power of blessings and curses, the intervention of divine beings in human affairs.
* **Language:** Use of Bengali words and phrases like *shono*, *bhāng*, *shastras*.



This retelling aims to capture the essence of the original folktale as found in Thakurmar Jhuli, preserving its cultural richness and the moral lessons it imparts.  It's a story meant to be *shona* (heard) and remembered for generations.
