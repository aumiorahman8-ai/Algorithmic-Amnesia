### **The Pitha Tree and Rakkhoshi**

In a quiet village on the edge of the mangrove forests, there stood an old, towering *pitha* tree (also called *kajal nim* or *white cedar*—*Tectona grandis* in some tellings, though *pitha* often symbolizes a hollow, ancient tree in folk tradition). Its trunk was wide, its branches knotted and wise-looking; the villagers believed it was sacred—home to spirits and守护者 (*守护者*—guardians) of the forest. Especially, they feared that at twilight, *Rakkhoshi* (রক্ষসী)—a terrifying, demoness-like figure from Bengali folklore—might emerge from its hollow.

Rakkhoshi was no mere ghost. She was said to have long, matted hair, eyes glowing red in the dark, and a mouth filled with broken, jagged teeth. Her voice was a chilling mix of a woman’s sorrow and a beast’s roar. She punished those who trespassed, cut sacred trees, or mocked the forest. Yet, she was also said to appear in dream-like visions—half-woman, half-demon—not to destroy blindly, but to test humility and respect.

One summer, a proud and greedy merchant from the city arrived in the village. He’d heard tales of the *pitha* tree’s valuable, termite-resistant wood, and he coveted it for making royal furniture and fine carvings. Ignoring the elders’ warnings, he declared, “Legends are for the superstitious. No spirit dares oppose a man with enough gold!”

With axes and tools, he and his men approached the pitha tree at dusk—just as the first crickets began their song and the light turned bronze. The village watched from afar, trembling.

The merchant raised his axe and struck the trunk—*thwack!*—but the blade bounced off as if hitting iron. He struck again. Nothing. Sweat beaded on his brow. He grew angrier, hitting harder, ignoring the trembling earth and the sudden stillness of birds.

That’s when the air grew cold. A low, mournful hum rose—not wind, but something *intentional*. The hollow of the pitha tree seemed to breathe. Then, slowly, a figure emerged.

She was tall, draped in ragged, blood-red cloth. Her face was half-covered by long hair, her eyes burning like embers. Her fingers ended in claws, and she smelled of damp earth and decaying leaves. This was Rakkhoshi.

The merchant tried to back away, but his feet were rooted to the spot. Rakkhoshi tilted her head, and her voice echoed—not with anger, but with sorrow and ancient wisdom:

> *“You see only timber. You hear only silence. But this tree breathes. It remembers monsoons and droughts. It holds the songs of cranes, the dreams of foxes. Were you ever humble enough to listen?”*

She raised her hand—and the ground beneath the merchant cracked open, not to swallow him, but to *offer* a single, glistening *pitha* fruit (a soft, white, spongy fruit of the tree, used in traditional sweets). It fell into his trembling palm.

> *“Now taste. Before your greed returns.”*

The merchant.bit into the fruit. It was sweet—like rain on lotus petals—but with a bitter aftertaste that made his eyes water. He wept—not from sorrow, but from sudden clarity: he saw visions in the sweetness—of the tree’s centuries of growth, of birds nesting, of children playing beneath it, of storms endured. He saw * life*, not just wood.

Rakkhoshi vanished as suddenly as she came. The merchant dropped the remaining fruit, ran back to the village, and told his story—not to boast, but to beg forgiveness.

He returned the next morning with offerings: milk, flowers, and a small copy of the tree carved in *shankha* (conch shell) for the village shrine. From then on, the pitha tree was protected. Locals left small tokens at its base, and elders taught children the tale—not as a ghost story, but as a lesson:

> *“The forest does not guard itself with fear. It guards itself with truth—and Rakkhoshi guards it by turning arrogance into understanding.”*

### 🌿 Moral & Cultural Notes:

- The *pitha* tree symbolizes resilience, wisdom, and the deep ecology of the region.
- **Rakkhoshi** is a complex figure: not purely evil, but a guardian spirit (like *Bon Bibi* in Sundarbani folklore) who tests human conduct. She often appears in stories involving forest entry, especially during eclipses or at twilight.
- The tale echoes real tensions in rural Bengal between exploitation and reverence for nature—and remains a powerful allegory for environmental respect.
