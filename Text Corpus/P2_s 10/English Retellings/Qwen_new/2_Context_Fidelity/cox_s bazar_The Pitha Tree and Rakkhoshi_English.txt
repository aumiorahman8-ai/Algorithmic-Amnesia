Here is a faithful retelling of the Bengali folktale *“The Pitha Tree and Rakkhoshi”* (খাজনার পিঠা গাছ এবং রাক্ষসী), originative to the coastal region of Cox’s Bazar—particularly among the Marma, Tangya, and Bengali fishing communities of the Teknaf Peninsula—and passed down orally for generations. I have preserved the cultural textures—including kinship terms, local rituals, dialectal hints, and animist-Buddhist-Hindu syncretic beliefs—without over-Anglicizing the narrative.

---

**খাজনার পিঠা গাছ ও রাক্ষসী:  
A Tale from Cox’s Bazar**

Long ago—when the sea still whispered secrets to the shore and the mangroves knew the names of every soul—there lived a widow named **Ajija Bibi**, in a *chaowri* (hamlet) near Inani beach, not far from the old *dakhil* (gate) of the Cox’s Bazar *kazis*. She had one beloved only son, **Nasir Uddin**, whom she called *Chhoto* (theLittle One) and treated like the *shada pitha*—steamed rice cake—soft, pure, and delicate in her care.

Nasir was strong-armed and smart, but his heart was light as a *ghorash* (a small coastal bird). He loved climbing the *pitha* trees (*CorylusAVellana*, the hazel-like wild nut tree that grows near riverbanks and is rare in Cox’s Bazar, hence the name *Khanar Pitha Gach*) near the *haor* (wetland) behind the *dakhil*, where the branches twist like *mohur* (gold coins) in old hands.

One summer, when the *baar* (monsoon) withdrew and the wind grew sharp and dry, Nasir climbed the tallest *pitha* tree. High above, he found a hollow, filled not with nuts, but with a **golden *laddu*** wrapped in white *misti pat* (betel leaf). Beside it lay a single *nol-kol* (canopy leaf), frayed but shimmering with *ojha dhar* (incantation-ink). He took it—without asking, as boys do—and climbed down.

That night, he brought the *laddu* to his *Thamma* (maternal uncle), saying, *“Thamma, ek chhoto phooler mala, khelo?”* (Uncle, here’s a garland of tiny flowers—share it?). Thamma was suspicious. He broke the *laddu*. Inside—a tiny, withered seed—black as *kala jadu* (black magic), cold as the inside of a drowned man’s boot. Thamma’s face went white.

*“Arey re! Kemon holo? Kothay pelechchho?!”* (Oh dear! How如此? Where did you pick this?!)

But Nasir only grinned and said, *“Pitha gachের hollow-এ পেয়ে গেছি, Thamma. Jolker kochi bhalo!”* (I found it in the hollow of the pitha tree, Thamma. It’s fun!)

The next morning, the tide turned *bhor* (pre-dawn), and a **wind rose—not of the *sundari*, not of the *gora pata*—but of the *bokul* (frangipani) and bone-dust.** A figure emerged from the *gojnrul* (thicket of thorny creepers), tall as a *khalif* (palm trunks used for roofing), skin the colour of rusted *shankha* (conch), hair like black *jute fibres*, eyes twin embers lit by *chuna-er aag* (lime-fire).

She was *Rakkhoshi*—not the demon of the forest, but the *Rakkhoshi of the Sea-Gate*: the *kaviraj*’s old warning. She guards the *pitha* grove, for the nuts she blesses give the power to *dhaner pitha* (rice cakes) to heal the dying. But the *laddu* was not for men.

She stood at Ajija Bibi’s door, and knocked—*thud-thud-thud*—with knuckles like *kochu* (taro) stems.

A *chhota* (lad) came.  
A *chhoti* (little one) came.  
A *bijli* (lightning) came—no, it was *Nasir*, pale as *makha pitha* (rice cake offered to the dead).

*Rakkhoshi* spoke—not with voice, but with the *nada* (hum) of the *shankha* and the crackle of dry firewood:  
*“Tui jodi *pitha* khao, *tomay* *pitha* khabe!*  
*Tui jodi *pitha* deo, *tomay* *pitha* dibe!*  
*But this *laddu*—it is my *thika* (signature), my *dakshina* (offering). You were a *loota* (thief). Now, you will serve.”*

Nasir trembled. He whispered, *“Ami…ami bolte shikhechi…”* (I… I will learn to speak—meaning, “to ask permission.”)

Rakkhoshi smiled, a crack in the earth.  
*“Shikhechh? Toh *ajja* (grandfather) will decide.”*

She led Nasir to the *pitha* grove at dawn. There, beneath the gnarled *pitha* tree, stood Ajija Bibi’s old *Choto*—not Nasir, but the *shava* (corpse) of his younger self, wrapped in *pata patla* (thin cloth), scenting of *gandh* (sandalwood) and salt. Rakkhoshi placed her hand on the *shava*’s brow. It rose, not alive—*but *chhad* (a ghost-son), the *rakshosh*-twin. And it bore a bell at its ankle—*khanki*—so soft, no one but the sea could hear.

*“Je je *pitha* khor, se se *pitha* khabe.*  
*Je je *pitha* dey, se se *pitha* paabe.*  
*You must now make *one* *pitha* for her, *one* for the *bhoot*, and one for yourself—*only then* may you return to your mother.”*

So Nasir—now *Chhad Nasir*—began to make *pitha* in silence, under Rakkhoshi’s eye. He ground wet rice with *mori jol* (well-water), shaped it in *nol-kol* leaves, filled them with *khirer chhoto* (tiny dates), and steamed them over *char* (breeze-fire), with *japa* (mantra) on his lips: *“Om Rakkhoshi Devyai Namah.”*  
He offered each to *Khuda* (God), to *Buddha*, and to *Rakkhoshi*—for in Cox’s Bazar, *Bhagaban* and *Buddha* and *Thakur* all share the same *aag* (fire).

When the thousandth *pitha* was made—each shaped like a *shankha*, each filled with truth—Rakkhoshi took one, broke it… and inside lay Nasir’s *naimittik* (true) name, written in *hijli* script on a petal of *kumor shosha* (marigold). She passed it to Ajija Bibi, who had been singing *baul geet* at the edge of the grove all along.

Ajija Bibi wept—not *rosh*, but *shor* (tears of release)—and fed the *pitha* to *Thamma*, who had returned, bearing *gur-er pitha* (jaggery cakes) as *prasad* (offering). As he ate, the *chhad* (ghost) beside him faded—leaving only sweet steam.

Rakkhoshi vanished into the *ghorash* (breeze), but her *khanki*—that tiny bell—fell to the roots of the *pitha* tree. Today, if you listen carefully on a moonless *ekadashi*, you may hear it: *ting… ting… ting…* As if remembering the boy who first broke her trust.

And to this day, the *pitha* tree in that grove is called **Khanar Pitha Gach**—not because it belongs to a *khan*, but because *khana* means both *to eat* and *to guard*.

*“Jeno khana khao, tar khana pao”—  
So that in eating, you are also eaten.*

**Cultural Notes Preserved in Retelling:**

- **Pitha Tree (খাজনার পিঠা গাছ)**: Refers broadly to wild nut-trees near Cox’s Bazar, especially *Corylus AVellana* (locally called *pitha* for the nut’s symbolic use in *pitha* cakes), though sometimes conflated with *bakri gal* or *moni phul* in local lore. Its hollows are sacred sites of *jhar phuka* (exorcism) and *dakshina* offerings.

- **Rakkhoshi**: Not a pure *rakshas*, but a *devi-rakkhak* (protectress-goddess) in coastal animist belief. She guards *kaviraj*-known trees and rivers—often appearing after breaches of *thika* (taboo).

- **Kinship Terms**: *Thamma* (maternal uncle), *Ajja* (paternal grandfather), *Chhoto* (affectionate diminutive), *bijli* (used here colloquially to mean “flash of presence”), showing how families weave affection, warning, and respect.

- **Rituals & Offerings**:  
  - *Shada pitha* (white steamed rice cake) — offered to ancestors.  
  - *Gur-er pitha* (jaggery cake) — everyday *prasad*.  
  - *Misti pat* (betel leaf), *nol-kol* (canopy/ficus leaf for wrapping), *ojha dhar* (ink of incantations, made with ash and honey), *chuna-er aag* (lime-fire for exorcistic purity).  
  - Mantra: *“Om Rakkhoshi Devyai Namah”* — a localized Sanskritized invocation, blending Shaktism with folk faith.

- **Coastal Syncretism**: Rakkhoshi receives offerings from *khana* (Hindu priests), *kaviraj* (traditional healers), *mausheeb* (Sufi healers), and *bauls*—reflecting how faiths coexist in Cox’s Bazar.

- **Language**: Dialectal inflections (*Shikhechh?* for *shikhechi?*, *thud-thud-thud* for knocking), numerals, and sound symbolism (the *khanki*’s *ting*).

This tale is not just a warning—it’s a *shiksha* (lesson) in responsibility to nature (*prakritir karna*), where trees are kin and theft is not merely crime, but *raktar karana* (blood-debt).
