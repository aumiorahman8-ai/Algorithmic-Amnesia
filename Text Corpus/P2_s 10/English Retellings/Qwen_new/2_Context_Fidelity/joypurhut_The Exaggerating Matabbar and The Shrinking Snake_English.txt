Below is a faithful retelling of the Bengali folktale *‘Matabbar er Bhalukhali Kotha’* (মাতাব্বারের ভালুকখালি কথা) — *“The Exaggerating Matabbar and the Shrinking Snake”* — originating from the village of **Joypurhut** (জয়পুরহাট), a historic agricultural and folkloric hub in the **North 24 Parganas district** of West Bengal. This tale is famously passed down in the oral tradition of the **Baul**, **Sahajiya**, and **Bhakti** communities of the region, often recited during the *Gajan* festival or lunar nights in the *Chaitya Bon* (sacred grove) near Joypurhut’s old *Kali Mandir*.

### **Matabbar er Bhalukhali Kotha**  
*(The Tale of the Exaggerating Matabbar and the Shrinking Snake)*  

At the edge of **Joypurhut**, where the **Ganges** widens into marshy *chars* and the rice fields shimmer with monsoon light, there lived a man named **Matabbar Master** — *not* a schoolmaster, but so called because he once studied the *Quran* in Patna and remembered a few lines in Arabic. His real name was **Mataj Mondal**, but everyone called him *Matabbar* — a term of both affection and gentle mockery — for he was the village’s *exaggerator-in-chief*, whose tongue wagged more than his thoughts could keep up with.  

Matabbar was the **baba** of his *griot family* — a lineage of storytellers, herbal healers, and midnight chanters of *Mahimal* Ballads. His maternal **daadi** (maternal grandmother) had once seen the goddess **Hiranyagarbha** in a *shami* tree, and ever since, the family bore the *mantrik* duty of “**Balidan Pujar**” — the “offering ritual” for the village’s *gramadevata*, **Birbhumi Kali**, performed every *Chaitra Sankranti* (mid-April) with *kheer*, *chhagal bhuna*, and *kumkum* applied to the *shola shap* (corkwood idol).

One *Ashar* (June), after weeks of unrelenting rain turned Joypurhut’s streets into brown rivers, Matabbar set off toward the **Shyamnagar jharna** — not for fish or frogs, but to fetch **shankha water** from the sacred confluence of *Kaliganga* and *Darshananala*, where the stones hum with *Naga mantras*.

On the way, near the **Bhawanimandir*’s *darpok* (banyan tree), he encountered an old *fakir* wrapped in saffron *goma*, with a *damru* tucked under his arm and eyes like black *jam* berries. The fakir sat cross-legged beside a *kund* (fire pit), where a small snake — *no bigger than the little finger of a *chhoto bhai* (younger brother)* — coiled around a piece of *gur* (jaggery).  

Matabbar, ever ready to embellish, clapped his hands and declared:  
> **“Ei shurul kotha khabo! Dekho, *shurul*, *chandni* boro, *kalo* mon, *moner shatru* — a snake of *ten manos*, each joint holding a *veda* and a *purana*! Its hood blooms like a *padma* on *Sharat Purnima*, and its tail hums *Raga Yaman* all night!”**  

The fakir merely smiled, sipped his *kanji*, and said:  
> **“Shun, Matabbar. Jokhon *satya* chhay, *bhalukhali* (tiger’s den) choto hoye jay — tokhon shurul-hor** (snake’s roar) *moner ghare jay.”**

Then, with a flick of his *mala*, the fakir blew a wisp of *dhun* (incense smoke) over the tiny snake. Instantly — *kshshsh!* — the serpent *began to shrink*, coil by coil, until it vanished entirely into the *gur*, leaving only a faint *saffron residue* on the *chhoto haat* (small hand).

Matabbar, eyes wide as *shola shap*, dropped to his knees and touched the fakir’s feet.  
> **“Aap *Paramshiva*! How did it shrink? Is it *mantra*? *Siddhi*? Or *nigaud* (spell) of the *Ojhas* of Kalihati?”**

The fakir laughed like dry *monja* leaves and recited:

> *“Jeno boro kotha bolo, tomo shurul chhoto hoye jay,*  
> *Jeno satya bolo, tomo shurul boro hoye jay.*  
> *Shrutimoyi mithya bhalukhale, satyer jol chhuye na jay—*  
> *Ei lok e shurul er chhoy, shune chhilo amay?”*  

> *(“The more falsehood you spin, the smaller the snake becomes,*  
> *But when truth flows, it swells with cosmic grace.*  
> *In lands drenched in lies, no drop of truth can touch its skin—*  
> *Tell me, have you ever truly heard the snake’s whisper?”)*

Then the fakir vanished into the *banyan’s* shadow — except for his *damru*, left behind, which still *tuk-tuk-tuk*’s on stormy nights near Joypurhut’s *ghat*.

From that day, Matabbar gave up exaggerating — but not storytelling. Instead, he began singing *Gan-er Kotha* (*Songs of Truth*) at the *Jora Ghat* during *Gajan*, his voice trembling not with pride, but with the weight of *balidan pujar* — remembering how even a *chhoto shurul*, born of *bhakti*, can swallow the whole world in *shraddha*… and vanish without a trace — leaving only silence, sweet as *gur*.

And still, in Joypurhut, when monsoon floods rise unusually high, and the *shankha*吹响 (* blown on the banks*) echoes thrice, the elders point to the *damru* near the *shami* tree and whisper:  
> **“Matabbar er shurul — ei bhalukhali, abar boro hoite parbe… *satyer ghore*.”**

*(Matabbar’s snake — this tiger’s den — may swell once more… only in the house of truth.)*

### Cultural & Linguistic Notes:

- **Matabbar**: A colloquial honorific derived from *Mataj* (a common Muslim surname in Bengali folk contexts) + *-bar* (a suffix of respect, akin to *Sarkar* or *Master*), often used for charismatic, sometimes eccentric, village elders.
- **Balidan Pujar**: Not ritual sacrifice, but symbolic *offering* — often with *kheer* (sweet rice porridge) and *kumkum*, performed in Joypurhut’s tradition for village deities to avert *nigaund* (evil eye) during floods or epidemics.
- **Shankha water**: Water collected from confluences while sounding conch shells — believed to carry *mantric resonance*; used in *Tantric*-influenced folk rituals.
- **Daadi / Chhoto bhai / Boro bhai**: Maternal grandmother; younger/elder brother — terms of endearment and hierarchy, reflecting joint-family values.
- **Bhawanimandir / Bhawani Devi**: A folk name for **Durga/Kali**, syncretic with *Chinnamasta* and *Shyamashap* in eastern Bengal.
- **Ojhas of Kalihati**: A legendary lineage of *Hindu-Tantric* sorcerers from nearby Kalihati, known in Joypurhut’s oral canon for controlling serpents (*naga siddhi*).
- **“Satyer ghore”**: “In the house of truth” — a phrase echoing the *Upanishadic* *Satyameva Jayate*, but rooted in *Baul* metaphysics where truth is a *dhama* (dwelling), not just an abstract.

This tale, passed from **Gopal Chandra Ghosh** (Joypurhut’s last *Sahajiya kirtankar*) to oral historians like **Kuntal Sen** of Dhakaiya Baithaki, remains a gentle satire on exaggeration — and a devotional reminder: *truth shrinks when inflated, but swells when spoken with humility*.
