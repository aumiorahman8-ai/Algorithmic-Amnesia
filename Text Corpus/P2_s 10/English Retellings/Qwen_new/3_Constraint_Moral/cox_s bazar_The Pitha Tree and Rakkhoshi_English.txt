The Bengali folktale *“The Pitha Tree and Rakkhoshi”* centers on greed, respect for nature, and the consequences of broken promises. A humble woodcutter named Karna discovers a sacred *pitha* tree (Syzgium_cumini, or jamun) guarded by the gentle forest spirit Rakkhoshi. She warns him: *“Take only what you need, and never cut the tree—its fruits feed the lost and the weary.”* Grateful, Karna agrees and lives harmoniously, taking ripe fruit only for sustenance.

One day, Karna’s greedy neighbor, Nand Kumar, overhears and tricks Rakkhoshi into revealing the tree’s deepest secret: its roots hold hidden treasure. Tempted, Nand Kumar ignores the warning and cuts the tree down, hoping to unearth gold. But instead, Rakkhoshi—now enraged by the betrayal—transforms the land into a desolate marsh, and Nand Kumar is lost in the blinding fog, forced to wander forever, a warning to others.

Meanwhile, Karna continues to honor his vow. When famine strikes, he shares the remaining fruit with the village, and Rakkhoshi returns—blessing the land anew and making the tree flourish once more.

**Moral Lessons:**  
1. **Respect nature**: Exploiting the environment for greed invites disaster; harmony with nature brings abundance.  
2. **Integrity is rewarded**: Keeping one’s promises—even to unseen forces—leads to divine favor.  
3. **Greed blinds and destroys**: Disregarding wisdom and boundaries leads to personal ruin and communal harm.  

The tale remains a poignant reminder: nature’s gifts must be taken with gratitude, not taken *by* force.
