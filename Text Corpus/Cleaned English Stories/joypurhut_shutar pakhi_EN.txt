In a certain country, there lived a king. Near the palace lived a blacksmith named Mathur Kamar. He was not just a blacksmith; he had the power to achieve the impossible. In the same area lived a tailor named Abbas Khalifa, who also possessed extraordinary skills. These two men were very famous throughout the king's realm. One day, the king summoned them both to his court. The king said to Mathur Kamar, "Your fame is spread across the kingdom. Can you do something that will be remembered forever in this land?" Mathur Kamar replied, "Your Majesty, if you permit, I can make 101 iron fish fry that will come to life and dance about as soon as they are released into a pond." The king was astonished! He then turned to the tailor and asked, "And what wondrous feat can you perform?" The tailor said, "I can make a bird of thread that, in a single swoop, can devour all 101 of Mathur Kamar's fish fry." This aroused great curiosity among the king and the others. It was decided that a competition would be held to prove whose power was greater—the blacksmith's or the tailor's. A date was set and announced throughout the kingdom by the beat of a drum. The name of a traditional pond where the competition would take place was also announced. On the appointed day, the king and a crowd of eager citizens gathered at the designated spot to witness this amazing event. The blacksmith and the tailor were also present. As soon as the king gave the order, Mathur Kamar released his 101 iron fish fry into the pond. And truly, upon hitting the water, they began to swim around in a school. The assembled crowd declared that the blacksmith had kept his word, and now it was the tailor's turn. The tailor was ready with his thread-bird. As soon as he received the king's command, he released his thread-bird, which took flight into the sky, circled once, then swooped down and devoured all the fish. The king was amazed! He then asked the tailor what other tricks his thread-bird could perform. The tailor informed him that if a two-and-a-half-day-old infant was available, his bird could perform tricks with the child while flying in the air! An uproar ensued. At one point, the king himself said that his youngest queen had a child of that very age. The king went himself and asked the queen for the child. Being a mother, she was at first unwilling, but considering the king's honor and prestige, she relented to his request. The youngest queen gave the infant to the king on the condition that the child would be returned to her arms immediately after the performance. The king returned to the scene with the queen. Everyone was waiting with bated breath. The king told the tailor to begin the show. The tailor then warned the king that his bird could only perform this trick for a specific amount of time. If the designated time was exceeded, he would no longer be able to bring the bird back down to the ground. He therefore cautioned the king not to request any extra time for the performance.

Finally, the king gave the order to begin. The tailor released his bird. The thread-bird took off from the ground, circled the area once, then picked up the infant and flew upwards. High in the air, it began to perform various acrobatics with the child. The spectators were astonished, for they had never seen such a spectacle in their entire lives. After some time, the tailor told the king, "The bird must be brought down now. If you grant permission, I will bring it down." The king, however, requested a little more of the show. The tailor reminded him of the condition, telling the king that if the bird was not brought down within the time limit, it could not be brought down at all—it would go out of control and fly away somewhere else. But the king was adamant. He silenced the tailor with a sharp command. By then, the thread-bird had begun to ascend higher into the sky with the child! The mother, having lost her son, began to wail loudly and lament in a sorrowful tone:

Oh, you are a bird of virtue, O bird, look to the side of righteousness.
Oh bird, you have taken a liking to my son, look to the side of righteousness.
Oh, my chest bursts with the pain of milk,
Give me back my child.

The king then tried to console the queen, saying:

Oh Queen, if it is in his fate, the child will return.
If it is in his destiny, O Queen, the child will return.

Their lamentations were of no use. The thread-bird continued to rise higher with the child. Eventually, it left that country and crossed into the borders of another. With sorrow in their hearts, the curious crowd, the king, and the queen all returned to their homes.

The bird flew on and on, eventually landing in a garden. The owner of that garden was a flower-seller. She had no one in her family besides her husband. The flower-seller earned her livelihood by gathering flowers from various places, making garlands, and selling them. But for a long time, no flowers had bloomed in their own garden. The next morning, seeing the garden filled with flowers, the neighborhood boys ran to inform the flower-seller. She couldn't believe it at all, so she sent her husband to the garden. What he saw there made him doubt his own eyes. The garden was truly full of flowers. He began to shout, "Who are you, O virtuous one, by whose presence the dead trees in my garden have bloomed? Please, show yourself." At this, the bird appeared with the small child. The flower-seller and her husband had no children of their own. Seeing the little child, the husband's heart filled with pity. He took the child and the bird to his home. Upon arriving, he told his wife, "We have no children. God himself has given us a child today. We will raise this child as our own." The husband and wife were overjoyed. They quietly began to raise the child, feeding him cow's milk. The problem, however, was that society would not accept the sudden appearance of a child after so many years. So, the two of them devised a plan to deal with this problem. To spread the word that she was pregnant, the flower-seller started visiting her neighbors, intentionally tying a small bundle to her belly so that everyone would believe she was going to be a mother. Some wondered how she could be expecting a child after so long, while others said that God had finally looked upon the flower-seller in her old age, providing a way to continue their lineage. When about ten months had passed in this way, the flower-seller called for Loya Bewa, the mother of Kurani. Loya was a midwife. The flower-seller told Loya Bibi that on a designated day, she should announce to the village that the flower-seller had given birth to a son. She would be rewarded for this. Loya Bewa, thinking it a good deed, agreed. Exactly two days later, the midwife announced the birth. The news spread through the village that the flower-seller had given birth to a son. There was no further fuss about it. Thus, the child began to grow up with the flower-seller as if he were her own son. The flower-seller named the child Manik Chan.

Several years passed. By then, Manik had reached his youth. One day, Manik Chan told the flower-seller that he would weave a threadless garland for her, and he truly did weave a beautiful garland without using any thread. He also insisted on accompanying the flower-seller to the place where she sold her garlands. The flower-seller agreed. One day, she went to the palace of that country's king. The king had a single daughter, Chandramukhi. While buying garlands, Chandramukhi's eyes fell upon the threadless one. As she bought it, she asked the flower-seller why she had never brought such a beautiful garland before. She also wanted to know who had made it. The flower-seller repeatedly insisted that she had made it herself, but Chandramukhi did not believe her. Eventually, the flower-seller was forced to admit that the young man who had come with her had made it. Upon seeing the young man, Chandramukhi was smitten. She told the flower-seller that she wanted to marry him. Ultimately, the princess told her father about her wish to marry the young man. Though reluctant at first, her father eventually agreed for the sake of his daughter's happiness. The flower-seller returned home.

Within a year, a beautiful baby boy was born to Manik and Chandramukhi's family. Four or five years later, Chandramukhi became pregnant again. Just when there were only a few days left to complete the tenth month, Manik said to his wife, "It has been a long time since I've had any contact with my parents. We could go and visit them." They both agreed. But where was that kingdom? Then, he remembered the thread-bird. Only the bird knew the way, so it was brought from the flower-seller's house. With their son, they mounted the thread-bird! The bird set off towards their native land. In the meantime, Chandramukhi went into labor. She repeatedly told her husband to land somewhere. But if the bird was made to land before reaching the destination it was first told, it would lose its ability to fly. Writhing in labor pains, Chandramukhi at one point cried out, "For the sake of Gaur! O bird, land on the ground." The thread-bird then landed on a river's sandbar. The bird's wing was also broken. Chandramukhi gave birth to another son. To warm the shivering infant, Manik went into the nearby woods on the sandbar to gather dry wood for a fire. Chandramukhi, leaving her two children, went to the riverbank to wash some clothes. Meanwhile, a huge python began to slither towards the newborn child. Seeing this, the thread-bird thought, "As long as I live, I will not let my master's child die." So, it too moved slowly towards the snake. At one point, it bit the snake's head with all its might. The snake coiled around the bird. As a result, both the snake and the bird died. Immediately after this incident, a ferryman was passing by in his boat. Seeing two children in the middle of the sandbar, he moored his boat there. Finding no one with the children, he took them to his home and began to raise them.

Meanwhile, after finishing her work at the riverbank, Chandramukhi returned to the sandbar. But where were her children? She began to wail! Just then, a merchant's boat, returning to his own country after trading, was passing a short distance from the riverbank. Seeing a lone woman on the sandbar, he was filled with greed. He stopped his boat at the sandbar, forcibly took Chandramukhi onto the boat, and brought her to his home. There, the merchant proposed marriage to Chandramukhi. The helpless Chandramukhi devised a strategy to protect herself. She told the merchant that they would marry, but only after four years. Until then, the merchant would have to arrange for her to live in a separate house to protect her chastity. She added another condition: whenever he went on a trading voyage, he must take her with him and provide a separate boat for her. The merchant agreed to her terms. A year after this incident, the merchant set out on a trading voyage again.

Now, let us return to the two sons and their father. Manik Chan, returning to the sandbar with firewood, was nearly mad with grief upon not finding his wife and children. He went to the king of that country and told him that he firmly believed someone had abducted his wife and children. The righteous king took pity on him. He consoled him, saying he would do everything possible to rescue his wife and children. As a result, he arranged for a guard to be posted at the sandbar's landing and issued a decree that all merchants who passed along this river route for trade, either coming or going, must stay there overnight. During this time, their boats would be searched. As luck would have it, the ferryman who had taken the two children from the sandbar and was raising them was assigned the duty of the guard.

One day, the merchant was returning home after a trading voyage. With him, in another boat, was Chandramukhi. As per the rule, the boat was moored at the sandbar's landing for the night. But the ferryman was ill that day, so instead of standing guard himself, he sent the two brothers to perform the duty. The merchant left his boat to attend to his business elsewhere. On one side, the two brothers were stationed at the river landing; on the other, Chandramukhi was in the moored boat. At one point, when the younger brother was dozing off, the elder brother said, "Look, little brother, don't fall asleep. If any harm comes to the boat due to our negligence, there will be trouble. Instead, let me sing you a song." Saying this, he began to sing in a mournful tune:

Oh, father went to fetch fire,
Alas, O Allah, a tiger must have eaten him, this sorrow is unbearable, O Fate.
Mother went to the banks of the Yamuna,
Alas, O Allah, a crocodile must have eaten her, this sorrow is unbearable, O Fate.
The bird went to kill the python,
Alas, O Allah, it died there in the fight.
Oh, we two brothers were left behind, alas, O Allah, on the river's sandy shore, this sorrow
is unbearable, O Fate!

Hearing this mournful song, Chandramukhi, inside the boat, had no doubt that these two brothers were none other than her own two sons. She then came up with another plan. When the merchant returned to the boat, she complained about the two brothers, alleging that they had made lewd remarks to her. She told the merchant to complain to the king. When the merchant reported the matter to the king, the king summoned everyone to his court. First, the king asked Chandramukhi to tell him what lewd remarks the two brothers had made. Chandramukhi said, "No, I will not say it. Let them say what they said themselves." The king ordered the two brothers to say what they had said. The two brothers thought, "We didn't say anything that could offend anyone. Why this false accusation?" With God as their witness, they began to sing that same song:

Oh, father went to fetch fire,
Alas, O Allah, a tiger must have eaten him, this sorrow is unbearable, O Fate.
Mother went to the banks of the Yamuna,
Alas, O Allah, a crocodile must have eaten her, this sorrow is unbearable, O Fate.
The bird went to kill the python,
Alas, O Allah, it died there in the fight.
Oh, we two brothers were left behind, alas, O Allah, on the river's sandy shore, this sorrow
is unbearable, O Fate!

As soon as the song ended, Chandramukhi embraced her two sons and told the king the entire story from beginning to end. The king then also remembered the incident of Manik Chan and summoned him to the court. Manik Chan recognized his wife upon seeing her and told the king that this was indeed his lost wife! The king rebuked the merchant and ordered him to take his boat and return to his own region. He then ordered that all arrangements be made to send Manik Chan and his wife and children back to their own country. A boat was prepared as per the king's command, and on the appointed day, Manik Chan set off for his native land with his wife and children.
